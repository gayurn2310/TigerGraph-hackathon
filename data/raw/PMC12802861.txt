Impact of Variation in APOE on Rheumatoid Arthritis, Psoriasis, and Psoriatic Arthritis: Sequencing and Genotyping in General Population Cohorts

Objective Emerging evidence suggests that apolipoprotein E (apoE) may be involved in the immune system and diseases driven by chronic inflammation. The impact of the full range of structural genetic variation in APOE for risk of rheumatoid arthritis, psoriasis, and psoriatic arthritis in the general population is not known. Further, whether apoE is associated with the common blood inflammatory biomarker profile warrants characterization. Methods We systematically sequenced APOE in 10,369 individuals from the Copenhagen City Heart Study (CCHS) and genotyped nine variants (frequency ≥ 2/10,369) in 95,228 individuals from the Copenhagen General Population Study (CGPS). The UK Biobank was used for validation of the observed associations between the ε2/ε3/ε4 APOE polymorphism and disease end points. Results Concentrations or cell counts decreased stepwise from ε33 to ε43 to ε44 for C‐reactive protein ( P = 4 × 10 −223 ), eosinophils ( P = 5 × 10 −5 ), monocytes ( P = 8 × 10 −4 ), complement C3 ( P = 0.001), and fibrinogen ( P = 0.009). The multifactorially adjusted hazard ratio (HR) for ε32 versus ε33 was 0.81 (95% confidence interval [CI] 0.70–0.94) for rheumatoid arthritis in CCHS+CGPS. Similarly, the HR for ε32 versus ε33 was 0.88 (95% CI 0.79–0.99) for psoriasis in a meta‐analysis of CCHS+CGPS and the UK Biobank. Conclusion We found that APOE ε4 is associated with a biomarker profile consistent with a decreased general immune response. The ε32 genotype is associated with an intermediate biomarker profile and with a decreased risk of rheumatoid arthritis in the Copenhagen cohorts and of psoriasis in the meta‐analysis. This suggests that the low‐risk combination of apoE3 and apoE2 may play a role in the normal noninjurious inflammatory response.

INTRODUCTION Apolipoprotein E (apoE) plays a well‐known role in cholesterol metabolism, in which it serves as a ligand for the low‐density lipoprotein receptor family, clearing triglyceride‐rich lipoproteins. 1 APOE genotypes are associated with development of cardiovascular disease 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 and is central for Alzheimer disease pathology, 6 , 7 in which APOE ε4 is a strong genetic risk factor for dementia. 6 , 8 , 9 , 10 Involvement of apoE in the inflammation‐driven part of Alzheimer pathology emphasizes the importance to shed light on a potential role of apoE in chronic inflammatory diseases. 11 ApoE is important in the innate immune system 12 and plays a role in bacterial, viral, and parasite‐induced infections 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 and in inflammation in the retina. 18 Some previous studies indicate that the APOE genotype plays a role in rheumatoid arthritis 19 , 20 and psoriasis, 21 whereas others indicate no APOE genotype effects. 22 The importance of investigating systemic mechanisms of inflammation is underlined by studies showing that psoriasis is associated with an increased risk for cardiovascular disease 23 , 24 , 25 and that conventional risk factors do not fully explain the excess burden of cardiovascular disease in rheumatoid arthritis 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 in general and in rheumatoid arthritis activity. 30 , 31 Previous observational studies have shown that ε4 carriers have the lowest plasma level of C‐reactive protein (CRP) among the APOE genotypes. 32 , 33 , 34 However, patterns for other inflammatory biomarkers need to be explored. Further knowledge about the systemic inflammatory mechanisms in chronic inflammatory arthritis and psoriasis are needed to optimize approaches for prevention and treatment of chronic inflammatory diseases as well as the associated comorbidities. We tested the hypothesis that common and rare variants in the APOE gene are associated with lower markers of inflammation in plasma and risk of rheumatoid arthritis, psoriasis, and psoriatic arthritis in the general population. For this purpose, we used 10,369 individuals from the Copenhagen City Heart Study (CCHS), 95,228 individuals from the Copenhagen General Population Study (CGPS), and 367,905 individuals from the UK Biobank. MATERIALS AND METHODS The CCHS and the CGPS studies were conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki and were approved by institutional review boards and Danish ethical committees (KF‐100.2039/91 and H‐KF‐01‐144/01). All participants were White and of Danish descent, and study participation did not overlap. The UK Biobank study was approved by the North‐West Multi‐Centre Research Ethics Committee (16/NW/0274). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants from all three studies. Participants We included individuals from the CCHS and the CGPS. Individuals for these two similar prospective studies of the Danish general population were randomly selected from the national Danish Civil Registration System to reflect the adult population aged 20 to 100+ years. The CCHS was initiated in 1976 to 1978, with follow‐up examinations in 1981 to 1983, 1991 to 1994, and 2001 2003. 10 , 35 , 36 The CGPS was initiated in 2003, with the first enrollment period from 2003 to 2015. 10 , 35 , 36 In both studies, examinations included a questionnaire, a physical examination, and blood sampling for biochemical and DNA analyses. For validation, we included unrelated White individuals from the UK Biobank, a general population study with multiple assessment centers across the UK in 2006 to 2010 and an age range for inclusion of 40 to 69 years. 37 As only White individuals were included, no further adjustment for ethnicity was performed. From the CCHS, we included 10,369 individuals who gave blood for biochemical and DNA analyses at the 1991 to 1994 or 2001 to 2003 examinations; among these, 275, 145, and 31 developed rheumatoid arthritis, psoriasis, or psoriatic arthritis. From the CGPS, we included 95,228 individuals; among these, 1,662, 1,257, and 446 developed rheumatoid arthritis, psoriasis, or psoriatic arthritis. For validation, we included 367,905 participants in the UK Biobank, of whom 3,820, 1,878, and 311 developed rheumatoid arthritis, psoriasis, or psoriatic arthritis. End points For the CCHS and the CGPS, information on diagnoses was collected from the national Danish Patient Registry, which contains data on all patient contacts from all clinical hospital departments in Denmark since 1977, and from the national Danish Causes of Death Registry, which contains data on causes of all deaths in Denmark as reported by hospitals and general practitioners since 1977. In the UK Biobank, the ascertainment of diseases came from the linkage to electronic health records, including inpatient hospital records, primary care records, and death registrations. Information on diagnosis of rheumatoid arthritis by International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes was as follows: ICD‐8, 712.1 to 712.3; ICD‐9, 714.0 to 714.2; and ICD‐10, M05 to M06. Information on diagnosis of psoriasis was as follows: ICD‐8, 696.10 and 696.19; ICD‐9, 696.1; and ICD‐10, L40. Information on diagnosis of psoriatic arthritis was as follows: ICD‐8, 696.09; ICD‐9, 696.0; and ICD‐10, M07.0 to M07.3. For main analyses for the CCHS and the CGPS, ICD‐8 and ICD‐10 codes were used (ICD‐9 was never used in Denmark). For validation in the UK Biobank, ICD‐9 and ICD‐10 codes, as well as self‐reported disease, were used for information on diagnoses. For Cox regression models for APOE variants, follow‐up began at birth or start of the registries (January 1, 1977), whichever came last. Follow‐up ended at occurrence of event, at death, at emigration, or on December 8, 2018 (last update of the registries), whichever came first. For CCHS+CGPS, median follow‐up from start of the registries was 42 years (range &lt;1–42 years), with no individuals lost to follow‐up due to the completeness of the Danish nationwide registries. For validation in the UK Biobank, follow‐up began at study entry, last update of the diagnoses was March 31, 2022, and median follow‐up time was 13 years (range &lt;1–16 years). Gene screening and genotyping The translated region of APOE covering exons 2, 3, and 4 and exon–intron boundaries was screened. Mutation screening was performed by Lightscanner end‐point melting analyses, followed by Sanger sequencing and with confirmation of variants by a second round of Sanger sequencing. Details of the sequencing and genotyping strategy have been described previously. 38 Details on the genotyping in the UK Biobank have been described previously. 22 Biochemical analyses and other covariates in CCHS + CGPS Plasma apoE levels were measured using nephelometry or turbidimetry (Dade Behring or Dako), as previously described. 10 , 39 , 40 Plasma complement C3 levels were measured turbidimetrically on fresh samples with a Kone autoanalyzer (Konelab, Thermo Fisher Scientific), as previously described. 41 , 42 Leucocyte count, including differential count, high‐sensitivity CRP, fibrinogen, lipids, lipoproteins, and apolipoproteins, was measured using standard hospital assays. All measurements were on nonfasting individuals. There were no major differences in sample preparation across biomarkers, and measurements of inflammatory biomarkers were conducted concurrently on fresh samples and blinded to APOE genotypes. Statistical analysis We used Stata/S.E. version 17.0 (Stata Corp). P values &lt;0.05, corrected for multiple comparisons when applicable, were considered significant. P values &lt;0.0001 are given as powers of 10. Kruskal–Wallis equality‐of‐populations rank test and Pearson's χ 2 test were used in comparisons of continuous and categorical variables, respectively. Missing data were imputed from age and sex in each population separately (&lt;0.5% for both CCHS and CGPS, 3.7% for UK Biobank) using multinomial logistic regression for categorical variables and linear regression for continuous variables. We used Cox regression models to test associations between common and rare APOE variants and disease end points using a model adjusted for known biologically relevant risk factors and markers of life style: age (time scale), sex, body mass index, smoking, hypertension, diabetes, lipid‐lowering therapy, alcohol consumption, physical inactivity, postmenopausal status and hormonal replacement therapy (women only), education, total cholesterol, low‐density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol, apolipoprotein B, high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, apolipoprotein A1, and triglycerides. Proportionality of hazards over time were assessed by plotting −ln(−ln[survival]) versus ln(analysis time). There was no suspicion of nonproportionality. For meta‐analysis, we used a fixed‐effect model using the inverse‐variance method. The metan command in Stata was used. DISCUSSION In large prospective general population cohort studies including up to 470,000 individuals, the principal findings were that ε4 was associated with a biomarker profile consistent with a decreased general immune response, whereas the ε32 genotype was associated with an intermediate biomarker profile and decreased risks of rheumatoid arthritis and psoriasis in the Copenhagen cohorts and in a meta‐analysis of the Copenhagen cohorts and the UK Biobank. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to present the full spectrum of structural genetic variation in APOE and associations with inflammatory plasma biomarkers and diseases in large general population cohorts. The APOE ε2/ε3/ε4 polymorphism is associated with plasma levels of CRP, with ε4 carriers having the lowest plasma levels of CRP among the APOE genotypes; this has previously been shown in several observational studies. 32 , 33 , 34 One study found that apoE serves as a direct checkpoint inhibitor of unresolvable innate inflammation; compared to apoE–deficient mice, all human apoE isoforms attenuated the classical complement cascade activity via high‐affinity binding to the activated classical complement cascade initiating C1q protein. 43 CRP activates C1q and apoE binds to C1q, and these interactions may play a role in the observed association between the ε4 allele and plasma CRP levels. However, the underlying biologic mechanisms warrant further scrutiny. In contrast, ε2 has been linked to accumulation of remnant lipoproteins causing hepatic inflammation and enhanced immune vigilance, consistent with elevated levels of immune markers and a more robust response to inflammatory cues. 12 , 44 , 45 Our study shows a generally decreased inflammatory biomarker response for the ε4 allele compared to the ε3 allele in a predominantly healthy general population cohort. This involves not only CRP but also another general acute‐phase reactant, fibrinogen, as well as the leucocyte differential count, implicating a general impact on the immune response in plasma. A lower general inflammatory activation for ε4 is in line with our finding of no increased risk for the ε4 allele for the three disease entities. Possessing one or two copies of ε2 is not associated with a significantly per allele increased or decreased biomarker response relative to the wild‐type allele ε33. That we find isoform‐specific differences for several cell types in the leucocyte differential count is in line with indications from previous studies. Macrophages synthesize apoE, macrophages activate T lymphocytes by antigen presentation, and interferon‐γ from T lymphocytes inhibits macrophage apoE expression. 46 Further, it is suggested that apoE inhibits T cell activation by reducing the density of immune stimulatory proteins on antigen‐presenting cells. 46 In line with this, apoE has also been suggested to be involved in T lymphocyte proliferation. 47 Although autoimmune diseases are thought to be mainly driven by T1 response and, to a lesser degree, by a T2 response, allergic diseases are more or less driven entirely by a T2 response, so there is a potential for an overlap of pathogenic CD4 T cells and B cells and involvement of apoE in allergic diseases. 48 We found that ε4 was associated with a decreased general biomarker immune response and no increased risk of rheumatoid arthritis, psoriasis, or psoriatic arthritis. One previous study found that the APOE genotype was associated with inflammation and lipid levels in rheumatoid arthritis, 19 and another found increased radiographic joint damage in ε2 carriers. 20 Both studies (sample sizes &lt;2,000 participants) did not find differences in the distribution of genotypes between patients with rheumatoid arthritis and controls. 19 , 20 This is consistent with the current findings that no large‐scale genotype effects for development of rheumatoid arthritis were present, although our large study was able to detect a protective association for ε32. In contrast to our findings of a protective effect of the ε32 genotype for development of psoriasis, a meta‐analysis with seven studies and 2,000 participants throughout 1997 to 2010 indicated that the ε2 allele was associated with an increased risk of psoriasis. 21 That the ε4 allele does not pose a risk factor for any of the three disease entities is in accordance with previous findings in the UK Biobank. 22 Here we further meta‐analyze the UK Biobank data with the use of similar multifactorially adjusted Cox regression models for CCHS+CGPS and for the UK Biobank. Of note, some studies in Middle Eastern and Asian populations found an association between ε2 and psoriasis; thus, there is the possibility of races and ethnicities other than White influencing the role of apoE in the development of psoriasis. 21 , 49 , 50 APOE ε32 is in many ways considered as the “healthy” genotype; ε32 is associated with decreased all‐cause mortality, decreased risk of dementia, and a moderate lipid profile. 10 , 39 ApoE3 and apoE2 might therefore serve as a healthy combination. A previous study underlines the importance of normally functioning apoE3 in both the brain and periphery by showing an increased inflammatory response in immune‐stimulated peritoneal macrophages in mice (apoE4/4 &gt; apoE3/0 &gt; apoE3/3). 12 Various studies indicate that lack of normal apoE3 and substitution to apoE4 are associated with disease progression in bacterial, viral, and parasite‐induced infections. 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 51 In the brain, apoE4 and lack of apoE, but not apoE3 or apoE2, lead to blood–brain barrier breakdown by a cyclosporine‐sensitive pathway. 11 These previous findings and the currently observed biomarker profile in this study lead us to hypothesize that the presence of normally functioning apoE3 in combination with LDL receptor–defective apoE2 is important for normal general systemic inflammatory response, however with higher levels of markers of inflammation, which at first glance might appear contradictory to lower disease risk. The role of healthy inflammatory response versus nonbeneficial inflammatory response is yet unclear, and causal disease mechanisms are thought also to involve local immune feedback loops, citrullination of proteins, and possibly genetic variation in HLA, which is not captured by more traditional biochemical markers in blood or plasma. 52 , 53 , 54 , 55 The gut microbiome plays a role in psoriasis, and “leaky gut syndrome” is one prevailing theory. 56 Interestingly, a recent study in mice indicated that microbiota‐induced changes in fatty acid metabolism may play an important role in the development of psoriasis. 57 Also, one study found that a polygenic risk score was associated with differences in the gut microbiota profile in individuals at high risk of developing rheumatoid arthritis. 58 Gut microbiota has recently been shown to be differentially affected by APOE status, possibly through these microbially produced short‐chain fatty acids as mediators of inflammation, and this is associated with neurodegeneration. 59 Whether APOE ‐mediated differences in the microbiome could play a role in rheumatoid arthritis and psoriasis remains to be elucidated, and potentially large‐scale consortia or collaborations should be formed to directly explore microbiome– APOE interactions. A major strength of the study is the large prospective general population design with no losses to follow‐up due to the complete Danish registries. One potential limitation of the study is the availability and completeness of the diagnostic information. However, the national Danish Patient Registry includes all hospital visits, as well as all out‐patient visits. A second potential limitation is that we studied White individuals only, and the allelic frequency of APOE ε2/ε3/ε4 varies among ethnicities, 1 which could affect the relative contribution. Indeed, more evidence is needed in other populations, especially for Middle Eastern and Asian ethnicities, and we suggest performing replication studies for other ethnicities, for example, in Asian large‐scale population studies. The generalizability of our study may thus potentially be limited, although the impact of common APOE variation is well described among ethnicities, and results are therefore likely to be applicable to most humans. A third potential limitation is the rarity of psoriatic arthritis in the general population as well as the rarity of rare APOE genotypes. However, we were able to meta‐analyze findings for psoriatic arthritis in the largest dataset to date. A fourth potential limitation is the difference in prevalence of rheumatoid arthritis between CCHS+CGPS (2.6% and 1.7%) and the UK Biobank (0.5%), the latter of which is closer to the worldwide prevalence of 0.5% to 1%. 60 However, the prevalence in CCHS+CGPS is in accordance with the reported lifetime risk in Denmark. 61 This is due to the longitudinal nature of the Copenhagen studies. A fifth potential limitation is potential diagnostic misclassification, underdiagnosis with only severe cases reported, and change of diagnostic criteria. The CCHS was initiated decades earlier in comparison to the more contemporary CGPS and the UK Biobank. Prevalence of rheumatoid arthritis, psoriasis, and psoriatic arthritis changed a lot during the observation period, and there were differences between Denmark and the United Kingdom. These changes for both Denmark and the United Kingdom could potentially introduce time‐dependent ascertainment biases. However, because we were investigating a general population in a prospective study design using genetic APOE variants—unchanged during that short time interval—as exposure, these changes in disease diagnostics and prevalences were nondifferential. Biomarkers were all measured a baseline, blinded to the APOE genotyping, and thus again were nondifferential with respect to time‐dependent biases. A sixth potential limitation concerns healthy survivor bias; it is well established that nonresponders display higher morbidity and mortality. The UK Biobank has a response rate of approximately 5%. However, this study also includes data from CCHS+CGPS with a response rate of 45%, which is similar or higher compared to other contemporary cohorts. This difference in response rates between the UK Biobank and CCHS+CGPS may also introduce sampling bias; however, random effects models were used in the meta‐analysis pooling data; thus, it is unlikely to disturb our findings in any major way. A seventh potential limitation is residual confounding from unmeasured variables. However as genetic variation in APOE remains fixed from conception, one source of confounding originates from population stratification, that is, not taking differences in population or recruitment bias into account in a case‐control study or interactions. However, we employed a prospective cohort design and kept analyses separated between the Copenhagen studies and the UK Biobank. The second source of confounding is from interactions, in particular interactions from residual confounding from unmeasured variables, which we cannot exclude; however, there were no interactions observed for measured confounders in the study. A final eighth limitation is that we cannot rule out that the results for APOE ε32 and rheumatoid arthritis potentially could be a spurious finding given the lack of independent replication in the UK Biobank. Therefore, this finding should be independently confirmed or refuted in other cohorts and large‐scale genetic consortia. In conclusion, we found that APOE ε4 is associated with a biomarker profile consistent with a decreased general immune response in plasma. The ε32 genotype is associated with an intermediate biomarker profile similar to the ε33 wildtype and a decreased risk of rheumatoid arthritis in the Copenhagen cohorts and of psoriasis in a meta‐analysis of the Copenhagen cohorts and the UK Biobank. This suggests that the low‐risk combination of apoE3 and apoE2 may play a role in a normal noninjurious inflammatory response. Our findings may be used to encourage continued research into APOE ‐related therapeutic targeting in anti‐inflammatory therapies, for example, CRISPR/Cas9 therapy for gene editing, and is useful for risk stratification and for further research into potential absolute risk reduction achievable in a clinical setting, which may help to identify high‐risk individuals among individuals with other risk factors and of different ethnicities.
