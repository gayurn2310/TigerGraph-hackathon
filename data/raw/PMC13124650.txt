GPND‐AI NULISA: A 15‐Protein AI classifier for diagnosis and co‐pathology profiling across neurodegenerative diseases

Abstract INTRODUCTION Accurate clinical diagnosis of neurodegenerative diseases remains challenging, particularly when individuals have mixed pathologies. We implemented the generalizable protein‐based neurodegenerative disease artificial intelligence (GPND‐AI) classifier using the NUcleic acid‐Linked Immuno‐Sandwich Assay (NULISA) central nervous system (CNS) panel to classify Alzheimer's disease, Parkinson's disease, frontotemporal dementia, dementia with Lewy bodies, and healthy controls, while disentangling mixed pathologies. METHODS Proteomic and clinical information from the Charles F. and Joanne Knight Alzheimer's Disease Research Center (Knight‐ADRC) and Movement Disorder Clinic were used to train and test the GPND‐AI classifier. External validation was performed in a Banner Sun Health Research Institute cohort and additional Knight‐ADRC samples with neuropathologically confirmed diagnoses. RESULTS GPND‐AI identified 15 proteins that achieve an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.955 and 92.3% accuracy across five diagnostic categories. In validation cohort, predicted co‐pathologies significantly correlated with clinical characteristics. DISCUSSION GPND‐AI identified a 15‐protein panel that accurately classifies individuals across the four major neurodegenerative diseases. Validation against neuropathology‐confirmed diagnoses supports the utility of proteomics‐based approaches for mapping disease‐specific and co‐existing neurodegenerative processes.

1 BACKGROUND Dementia is the seventh leading cause of death worldwide and a major contributor to disability, particularly in older adults. 1 Aging increases the likelihood of comorbid neurodegenerative diseases (NDs) 2 , 3 , 4 and overlapping neuropathological processes, 5 including amyloid beta (Aβ), tau, α‐synuclein, and TAR DNA‐binding protein 43 (TDP‐43). Over the past decade, proteomics 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 has emerged as a tool that reflects disease and underlying pathologies at an individual level. 12 Platforms such as SomaScan, Olink, and mass spectrometry enable scalable detection of plasma biomarkers and provide a more accessible alternative to neuroimaging. 14 The recently developed NUcleic acid‐Linked Immuno‐Sandwich Assay (NULISA) platform provides high multiplexing capacity and analytical accuracy with low sample volume requirements. 15 , 16 Mixed or co‐existing pathologies are common in older individuals. 17 For example, dementia with Lewy bodies frequently presents with concurrent α‐synuclein, Aβ, and tau pathology. 18 , 19 Such complex pathological patterns remain challenging to resolve using traditional binary classification models. In this study, we developed and validated the Generalizable Protein‐based Neurodegenerative Disease Artificial Intelligence (GPND‐AI) Classifier encompassing the most common NDs. Previous work demonstrated the feasibility of GPND‐AI framework using SomaScan‐based data, although large protein panels were required. 14 Although these models achieved strong performance, the number of proteins included in them may limit clinical translation, highlighting the need for more compact and targeted biomarker panels. RESEARCH IN CONTEXT Systematic review : We conducted a systematic review of the literature using PubMed and Google Scholar, focusing on blood‐based biomarkers, proteomics, and artificial intelligence (AI)–driven diagnostic models for neurodegenerative diseases. The majority of prior studies have focused on binary classification tasks, such as distinguishing Alzheimer's disease, Parkinson's disease, frontotemporal dementia, or dementia with Lewy bodies from cognitively normal controls, with relatively few models addressing multiple neurodegenerative diseases simultaneously. Even fewer studies have explicitly modeled mixed neuropathology, despite evidence that co‐existing amyloid beta (Aβ), tau, α‐synuclein, and TAR DNA‐binding protein 43 (TDP‐43) pathologies are common, particularly in older individuals. To our knowledge, only one prior study has directly addressed multi‐pathology modeling across diseases, from the same first and corresponding authors. Existing plasma biomarkers and AI frameworks therefore largely target single disease entities or isolated pathological processes, limiting their ability to capture the biological heterogeneity observed in real‐world neurodegenerative disease. In contrast, the present study leverages high‐sensitivity NUcleic acid‐Linked Immuno‐Sandwich Assay (NULISA) proteomic data and a streamlined 15‐protein panel to directly quantify and model co‐existing neuropathological processes. Interpretation : This study demonstrates that a streamlined 15‐protein plasma panel measured using NULISA technology, combined with the Generalizable Protein‐based Neurodegenerative Disease AI (GPND‐AI) framework, enables accurate multiclass diagnosis and quantification of co‐existing pathologies across major neurodegenerative diseases. The strong concordance between model‐derived probability signatures and neuropathological burden supports the biological validity of this approach. These findings suggest that probabilistic, multi‐pathology modeling provides a more faithful representation of neurodegenerative disease biology than traditional single‐label classification. Future directions : Future studies should evaluate the longitudinal performance of GPND‐AI for early disease detection, disease progression tracking, and prediction of clinical outcomes. Expansion to broader populations and integration with imaging, genetics, and clinical biomarkers may further enhance its utility. Ultimately, this framework may facilitate precision medicine approaches, clinical trial enrichment, and individualized risk stratification in neurodegenerative diseases. Here, we applied GPND‐AI to a targeted NULISA‐based platform to derive a streamlined and clinically feasible protein panel. Model performance was benchmarked in the Charles F. and Joanne Knight Alzheimer's Disease Research Center (Knight‐ADRC) and Movement Disorder Clinic (MDC) cohorts and then externally validated without relabeling, retraining, or imputation, in the Banner Sun Health Research Institute (BSHRI). Of note, the latter included neuropathologically confirmed diagnoses, enabling validation against a pathological standard of truth. This is essential given the high prevalence of co‐pathology across NDs, even in relatively “clean” disease entities, such as autosomal dominant Alzheimer's disease (ADAD) and autosomal dominant Parkinson's disease. 20 , 21 Finally, we tested whether the GPND‐AI model can stratify risk in individuals with ambiguous clinical diagnoses. 2 MATERIAL AND METHODS 2.1 Study participants Washington University Knight‐ADRC and MDC: A total of 3233 unique individuals with a primary clinical diagnosis of Alzheimer's disease (AD), Parkinson's disease (PD), dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB), frontotemporal Dementia (FTD), or healthy controls (CO) from Knight ADRC 22 and MDC. Biomarker information, including amyloid positron emission tomography (PET) status and amyloid/tau status, was further used to identify cognitive normal individuals without AD pathology 23 for model training. The final dataset comprised 983 biomarker‐negative CO, 714 biomarker‐positive AD, 126 PD, 58 FTD, 75 DLB, and 236 individuals with other diagnoses (OT; not major NDs at the time of blood draw; Figure 1, Table 1 ). Banner Sun Health Research Institute (BSHRI): We additionally included samples from the Arizona Study of Aging and Neurodegenerative Disorders (AZSAND) and Brain and Body Donation Program (BBDP; www.brainandbodydonationprogram.org ), 24 as an independent validation cohort, with 72 CO, 53 AD, 52 PD, 8 DLB, 2 FTD, and 38 individuals with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) (Table S1 ). 2.2 Collection of plasma samples and NULISA‐based proteomics data measurement Blood samples were processed and plasma aliquots were stored at −80°C until analysis. Proteins were quantified with the NULISAseq CNS panel, which measures 123 targets, following previously described protocols. 15 Concentrations were reported as NULISA Protein Quantification (NPQ) values normalized and log2‐transformed. Quality control procedures followed the workflow described by Gong et al. 16 Outliers and low‐quality measurements were removed using standard quality control (QC) procedures. After QC, the internal datasets retained 123 analytes across 3947 samples (3233 participants), whereas the BSHRI cohort retained 116 analytes across 280 samples. Limit‐of‐detection and coefficient‐of‐variation metrics were assessed but not used as exclusion criteria (Figure S1 ). Following QC, NPQ values were back‐transformed to linear scale, log10‐transformed, and z‐score standardized to enable cross‐cohort comparability; principal component analysis (PCA) was further performed on the harmonized data (Figure S2 ). 2.3 Study design We adapted the GPND‐AI classifier 14 to the NULISA platform to identify a panel of proteins that can perform disease classification and disentangle mixed pathologies (Figure 1 ). The multi‐stage modeling framework began with feature selection, incorporating disease‐associated proteins identified through differential abundance analysis, with age and sex included as covariates (Table S2 ), followed by variance thresholding (threshold = 0.01), F‐test–based selection, yielding a final 15‐protein panel. The multiclass model was implemented using a Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM) model. 25 To mitigate class imbalance, we incorporated a resampling strategy (SMOTETomek) to address class imbalance. To further interpret the feature contributions, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) 26 values were used for feature interpretation. The model was trained and tested using Knight‐ADRC and MDC samples, and subsequently applied to the BSHRI cohort to assess predictive performance and neuropathology concordance without retraining. Finally, we used the NULISA‐based GPND‐AI model to reclassify BSHRI MCI cases and Washington University in St. Louis (WashU) OT samples to evaluate its ability to resolve ambiguous diagnoses. 2.4 Statistical analyses Statistical analyses were applied to evaluate predicted probability distributions, neuropathological associations, and cognitive performance patterns across all cohorts (Figures 2 , 3 ). Within each cohort, individuals were stratified into AD‐higher‐probability or CO‐higher‐probability groups based on GPND‐AI probability vectors. Clinically diagnosed PD cases were further re‐stratified by predicted probabilities and compared with neuropathology‐defined AD and CO reference groups to assess prediction–pathology concordance. For MCI samples, associations between predicted diagnostic probabilities and continuous pathology measures were assessed using linear regression and Spearman correlation, with effect estimates and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) reported. 3 RESULTS 3.1 GPND‐AI model performance Starting from a QC‐processed matrix of 123 NULISA analytes (104 disease‐associated proteins), a final panel of 15 proteins was selected. In addition to data‐driven selection, domain knowledge was incorporated by including proteins consistently associated with disease in previous studies, inculding phosphorylated tau 217 (p‐tau217), p‐tau231, vascular endothelial growth factor D (VEGFD), and Parkinson disease protein 7 (PARK7). 27 , 28 , 29 This step was intended to improve robustness and generalizability across cohorts while reducing single‐site‐driven results. Using these 15 features, the classifier generated individual‐level probability estimates for five categories: AD, PD, FTD, DLB, and CO. The model achieved a mean area under the curve (AUC) of 0.955 and an overall accuracy of 0.923 (Figure 1 ). All five diagnostic categories reached AUCs &gt;0.92, with specificities exceeding 0.96 (Table S3 ; Figure S3 ), yielding mean probabilities of 0.75 for CO and 0.83 for AD, which mirror the expected gradient of underlying pathology across diagnostic groups. Calibration analyses showed general agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes across categories (Figure S4 ). Brier scores further supported reasonable probabilistic accuracy (AD: 0.025; CO: 0.058; DLB: 0.018; FTD: 0.016; PD: 0.031; multi‐class: 0.147; Table S4 ), although some variability was observed in less represented classes. Unlike traditional binary disease classifiers, GPND‐AI provides a full multiclass probability profile, enabling simultaneous assessment of multiple ND signatures and interpretation of overlapping pathologies. SHAP analyses identified key driven proteins of the model. By far the strongest influence was shown by p‐tau217, with the highest global importance (mean |SHAP| = 0.64) and substantial class‐specific effects (|SHAP| = 1.51 for AD; 1.21 for CO; Table S5 ; Figure S5 ). Neurofilament light polypeptide (NEFL) exhibited elevated SHAP contributions, particularly for DLB (0.28) and FTD (0.33), 30 yielding a global mean importance of 0.19. Additional contributions came from metabolic/erythrocytic proteins (Hemoglobin Subunit Alpha 1 [HBA1], Neuropeptide Y [NPY]), vascular‐related markers (VEGFD), and inflammatory chemokines (C‐C motif chemokine 2 [CCL2], C‐C motif chemokine 4 [CCL4], C‐C motif chemokine 13 [CCL13]), each showing modest but consistent class‐specific effects. Model performance across panel sizes (Table S6 ; Figure S6 ) showed that increasing the number of proteins from a single marker (p‐tau217) to a small panel (5–15 proteins) substantially improved performance, particularly for non‐AD diseases, whereas further expansion provided only marginal gains. Notably, a p‐tau217‐only model showed strong performance for AD (AUC = 0.977; Figure S7 ) but substantially reduced performance for non‐AD diseases (PD, FTD, and DLB), highlighting that single‐marker models do not generalize across heterogeneous neurodegenerative conditions. 3.2 Replication in an independent external cohort (BSHRI) with neuropathological traits We applied the GPND‐AI classifier in the BSHRI cohort without any data imputation or retraining, and compared predictions with clinical and neuropathological data. Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) embeddings (Figure 2A ) revealed clustering aligned with neuropathology, with AD‐predicted individuals exhibiting higher Braak and Thal staging. Among the clinical AD cases, 41 of 53 (77.4%) were classified as AD (Table S3 ). Furthermore, individuals predicted as AD (n = 41) exhibited a significantly greater pathological burden than samples without a clear pathological trend ( n = 7), with higher mean Thal phase (4.40 vs 2.86, p = 4.01 × 10 −3 ), higher mean Braak stage (5.27 vs 3.86, p = 6.57 × 10 −4 ), and lower MMSE scores (13.90 vs 21.71, p = 4.01 × 10 −2 ; Figure 2B ), indicating that higher predicted AD probability was associated with more advanced AD neuropathological staging and greater cognitive impairment. Among “controls,” 41 of 72 (57%) were correctly classified as CO (Table S3 ; Figure 2C ). Those predicted as AD in this group exhibited elevated amyloid pathology (mean Thal = 2.6) relative to those predicted as CO (mean Thal = 1.2, p = 6.39 × 10 −4 ), meaning that the CO diagnosis may include individuals with amyloid pathology captured by the model. For the remaining non‐AD neurodegenerative samples, the model showed patterns consistent with underlying pathology. Among two FTD cases, the model correctly classified 1 of 2; the case with AD prediction exhibited substantial AD co‐pathology, with Thal Phase 3 (A2) and Braak Stage V, consistent with mixed pathology contributing to prediction ambiguity. In PD, individuals generally showed higher PD probability, but a subset also showed elevated AD or CO probabilities, reflecting underlying co‐pathology in this group. For example, within the clinical PD cases, individuals with high AD probability ( n = 11) exhibited substantially higher amyloid accumulation (mean Thal = 3.09 vs 1.57; p = 5.62 × 10 −3 ; Figure 2D ), high tau pathology (mean Braak 3.91 vs 2.57; p = 3.11 × 10 −4 ), and lower MMSE than Neuropath‐confirmed CO (20.91 vs 27.58; p = 4.76 × 10 −3 ) or PD with higher probability for CO ( n = 32, 20.91 vs 24.39), reflecting that a subset of PD samples have AD pathology and cognitive decline. PD cases with high probability for CO (mean CO probability = 68.8% ± 16.9%) showed minimal amyloid burden (mean Thal = 1.25), markedly lower tau burden than Neuropath‐confirmed AD (mean Braak 3.38 vs 5.15; p = 3.89 × 10 −12 ), and similar MMSE to other PD subgroups revealed a clear and biologically coherent gradient. Crucially, Lewy body pathology was uniformly and markedly elevated across all PD samples regardless of GPND‐AI output. Sum of Lewy body burden remained high in all PD subgroups, with higher probability for CO and AD (29.84 vs 26.18), and greater than both benchmark groups (Neuropath‐confirmed AD = 5.96; Neuropath‐confirmed CO = 1.74). Together, these results highlight the ability of GPND‐AI to sensitively detect amyloid or tau co‐pathology within PD, even in a fully independent clinical cohort. 3.3 Risk stratification of ambiguous diagnosis Proteomic and clinical data were available for four individuals with pathogenic genetic variants in presenilin 1, PSEN1 (ADAD variants), including an amyloid beta precursor protein, APP ( p.V287G ) 31 (Figure 3A ). The GPND‐AI classified as AD, individuals with the mutations in PSEN1 . These individuals also have AD‐compatible biomarker profiles (all p‐tau217‐positive and amyloid‐PET–positive). The remaining individual was predicted as CO and exhibited negative amyloid PET. Additional analyses of the APP‐p. V287G variant confirmed that this is not a pathogenic mutation, and the individuals were mis‐diagnosed as ADAD. Thus, the CO prediction is concordant with both genetic and biomarker evidence, as this sample does not carry a pathogenic ADAD mutation. We further applied GPND‐AI to those diagnostically ambiguous or presymptomatic populations: WashU OT and the BSHRI MCI samples. Both cohorts displayed a comparable distribution across predicted classes (WashU OT: 30% CO, 36% AD, 12% PD; BSHRI MCI: 29% CO, 38% AD, 14% PD;Figure 3B, C ; Figure S8 ), consistent with their heterogeneous and early‐stage nature. Within these ambiguous groups, the classifier's assignments were strongly supported by underlying neuropathology or clinical information. In WashU OT, individuals predicted as AD exhibited significantly higher amyloid pathology than those predicted as CO (Neuritic Plaques: p = 5.56 × 10 −5 ; Thal: p = 2.50 × 10 −2 ). MCI showed the same directionally consistent pattern, with MCI individuals predicted as AD demonstrating substantially greater amyloid burden than those predicted as CO (Thal: p = 1.98 × 10 −4 ). Moreover, when we looked into the quantitative plaque burdens instead of the stage information, AD probability correlated positively with total plaque accumulation (Spearman ρ = 0.601; p = 6.51 × 10 −5 ), whereas CO correlated negatively ( ρ = −0.613; p = 4.25 × 10 −5 ), further supporting the biological validity of the model's stratification. 4 DISCUSSION Neurodegenerative diseases (or NDs) frequently occur in older adults with mixed or co‐existing pathologies. 5 , 32 Traditional classification frameworks struggle to capture this biological complexity. 33 In contrast, GPND‐AI, 14 the first AI‐based NULISA classifier, provides a streamlined 15‐protein panel for detecting overlapping pathological processes. By generating continuous, multiclass probability signatures rather than single‐label outputs, this model provides multi‐dimensional disease pattern. This approach can capture distinct pathological signals from plasma samples and support risk stratification. A key advance of this study relative to our prior SomaScan‐based GPND‐AI work is the use of a targeted NULISA platform to derive a compact 15‐protein panel while maintaining strong multi‐disease classification performance. Of note, this panel is not simply a reduced subset of prior features, but a refined, non‐redundant set that includes key biomarkers such as p‐tau217, supporting improved biological specificity and clinical feasibility. GPND‐AI demonstrated strong performance across multiple cohorts, achieving a mean AUC of 0.955 and an overall accuracy of 92.3%, with all five diagnostic categories reaching AUCs &gt;0.92. Across neuropathology‐confirmed individuals in the BSHRI cohort, higher predicted AD probabilities corresponded to higher proportion of amyloid and tau pathology, as indexed by increasing Thal phases and Braak stages and lower MMSE scores. This pattern aligns with prior reports showing that plasma p‐tau217 27 , 34 tracks amyloid and tau pathology in AD. Evidence for co‐existing pathology was further reflected by intermediate probability assignments (e.g., 20%–40%) across secondary disease categories, consistent with mixed pathology. Notably, these probability signatures remained robust in diagnostically ambiguous or presymptomatic populations. Among the WashU OT and BSHRI MCI samples, individuals assigned to the AD‐dominant category displayed significantly larger numbers of neuritic plaques and Thal burdens than those classified as CO, and AD probability correlated positively with continuous plaque accumulation, whereas CO probability correlated negatively. Evaluation in ADAD further demonstrates the model's sensitivity to early‐stage or atypical biomarker trajectories. All four ADAD‐designated cases were classified concordantly with pathogenic status, with three pathogenic mutation carriers correctly identified as AD and one non‐pathogenic APP variant carrier classified as CO. Collectively, these results demonstrate that a streamlined 15‐protein NULISA panel, implemented through GPND‐AI, captures co‐pathology beyond clinical diagnosis across AD, PD, DLB, FTD, and CO. By capturing mixed pathological signatures, this framework offers a scalable, interpretable, and biologically well‐grounded approach for population‐level screening and precision stratification in neurodegenerative disease research. However, validation in more diverse, real‐world populations is needed before clinical application. Prospective validation in larger, multi‐center, and community‐based populations will be essential to establish generalizability and real‐world clinical utility. In addition, given class imbalance, particularly for FTD and DLB, class‐specific estimates should be interpreted with caution.
