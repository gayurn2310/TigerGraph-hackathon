Interchangeability of CSF and PET to identify Alzheimer's disease pathology

Abstract INTRODUCTION Establishing interchangeability between cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and positron emission tomography (PET) for patient identification may improve access to Alzheimer's disease (AD) therapies. METHODS Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative participants with mild cognitive impairment or AD dementia and available CSF, florbetapir PET, and/or flortaucipir PET were included. The interchangeability of CSF (per label threshold/method) and PET was evaluated for patient identification. RESULTS Fujirebio Lumipulse amyloid beta (Aβ) 42/Aβ40 ( N = 288) and Roche Elecsys phosphorylated tau 181 (P‐tau181)/Aβ42 ( N = 251) Conformité Européene (CE)‐marked CSF assays were in high agreement with and non‐inferior to florbetapir PET for identifying patients with AD pathology. High agreement was observed between Roche Elecsys CSF P‐tau181/Aβ42 ( N = 127) and an early (temporal) flortaucipir PET stratification. DISCUSSION CSF assays were interchangeable with amyloid PET to identify patients with AD pathology and were in high agreement with elevated early temporal flortaucipir PET. CSF biomarkers may be used as a robust alternative to PET, potentially increasing access to AD diagnosis and disease‐modifying treatments.

1 BACKGROUND Alzheimer's disease (AD) is a progressive neurological disorder marked by cognitive, functional, and behavioral impairment. AD is the most common cause of dementia in the elderly, accounting for approximately 60% to 70% of cases. 1 Abnormal aggregation of neocortical amyloid beta (Aβ) plaques represents a pathological hallmark of AD, occurring prior to the accumulation of neurofibrillary tau tangles and neurodegeneration. 2 The latest diagnostic criteria and recommendations advise that, in addition to clinical evaluation, biomarker confirmation of the presence of Aβ plaque pathology is required to establish a diagnosis of AD. 3 – 5 Additionally, confirming the presence of neurofibrillary tau tangles would further support a diagnosis of AD and may be indicative of disease stage or severity. 3 , 4 , 5 Furthermore, appropriate use criteria, as well as clinical trial inclusion criteria of amyloid‐targeting therapy, require evidence of AD pathology. 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 Positron emission tomography (PET) imaging is one method recommended by the International Working Group criteria for in vivo estimation of amyloid and tau proteins, two hallmarks of AD pathology. 3 However, the manufacturing and supply logistics for radiopharmaceuticals are complex, and the short half‐life of radiolabeled drugs limits widespread access to a PET‐biomarker‐supported diagnosis of AD. 11 Additionally, the high costs associated with PET imaging are often not reimbursed. 12 All these considerations may lead to inequitable access to diagnosis and, thus, treatment for AD. Several cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) biomarkers have been validated and approved for assessment of amyloid and tau protein by regulatory agencies globally, including the US Food and Drug Administration and European Medicines Agency and Conformité Européene (CE). 13 As CSF assays are generally more accessible, less costly, and less resource‐intensive than PET imaging and part of the standard diagnostic workup in clinical practice, use of CSF biomarkers as an alternative to PET may enable more equitable access to AD diagnosis and therapy. 14 While the latest recommendations indicate both CSF‐ and PET‐based biomarkers are valid for the assessment of AD pathology, there is a scarcity of evidence on their interchangeability, especially considering specific inclusion criteria used in amyloid‐targeting therapy trials. 3 , 5 , 8 , 15 Using the criteria for detecting AD pathology as defined in biomarkers’ approved labels, this study aimed to investigate the interchangeability between CE‐marked CSF assays and PET for the identification of a patient with AD pathology. 2 METHODS 2.1 Patients and design Patients included a subset of participants from the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI; adni.loni.usc.edu). The ADNI was launched in 2003 as a public–private partnership, led by Principal Investigator Michael W. Weiner, MD. The primary goal of ADNI has been to test whether serial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), PET, other biological markers, and clinical and neuropsychological assessment can be combined to measure the progression of mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and early AD. For up‐to‐date information, see www.adni‐info.org . To be eligible, patients must have had a clinical diagnosis of MCI or dementia due to AD; a CSF draw with available results for the Fujirebio CE‐marked Lumipulse CSF Aβ42/Aβ40 within 6 months of an amyloid PET scan (using florbetapir F 18 or florbetaben F 18); and/or a CSF draw with available results for the Roche Elecsys CSF P‐tau181/Aβ42 ratio within 8 months of a tau PET scan (using flortaucipir F 18). No patients with a florbetaben scan met inclusion criteria; thus, only florbetapir scans were used for amyloid PET analysis. Of note, 8 months was employed to maximize sample size while minimizing the time difference between evaluations. Additionally, not enough patients met the criteria to permit a comparison between Lumipulse Aβ42/Aβ40 and tau PET. For CE‐marked CSF assays, the presence of AD pathology was defined using Lumipulse Aβ42/Aβ40 ≤0.062 or Elecsys P‐tau181/Aβ42 &gt;0.028. Thresholds were based on product labels in the European Union, Canada, and the United States; the Elecsys CSF P‐tau181/Aβ42 cut point was adjusted from the product label (0.023) to correct for disparate pre‐analytical handling of the CSF samples from those stipulated in the product label as well as for bridging between Generation 1 and 2 assays. 16 , 17 Florbetapir PET and flortaucipir PET acquisitions followed ADNI protocol and are available on the ADNI website. 18 RESEARCH IN CONTEXT Systematic review : The authors evaluated the literature for the studies comparing regulatory approved and accessible CSF tests and PET in early symptomatic AD populations. Interpretation : CSF assays using label thresholds were interchangeable with amyloid PET for detecting patients with AD pathology and were in high agreement with elevated early temporal tau PET. Using CSF biomarkers as an alternative to PET may increase access to both an AD diagnosis and disease‐modifying treatment. Future directions : New strategies, including new CSF thresholds should be considered to establish whether CSF tests can identify patients with widespread neocortical tau pathology. Florbetapir PET and flortaucipir PET image preprocessing is detailed on the ADNI website. 19 For the present analysis, for florbetapir PET, fully preprocessed images were downloaded from the University of Southern California Laboratory of Neuro Imaging (LONI; https://loni.usc.edu/ ). Florbetapir PET scans were then processed using previously validated methods and pipelines, 8 , 15 , 19 , 20 as well as blinded visual evaluation following manufacturer indication. 21 , 22 Three amyloid PET stratifications were considered: (1) visual read, (2) quantitation stratified to indicate amyloid positivity using 24.1 Centiloids (CL) to align with autopsy evaluation, 23 and (3) quantitation stratified to indicate amyloid positivity using ≥37 CL to align with previously adopted clinical trial inclusion criteria. 8 , 15 For flortaucipir PET, raw images were downloaded from LONI. After data conversion and preparation, flortaucipir PET dynamic datasets were processed through a validated pipeline (including motion, timing correction, and realigning) 24 yielding a quantitated AD‐weighted neocortical standardized uptake value ratio (SUVR) using a subject‐specific white matter reference region. 24 , 25 , 26 Additionally, mean images were visually evaluated in a blinded manner, following manufacturer's indications. 27 , 28 Four tau PET stratifications were considered: (1) binary visual read, (2) quantitation stratified at SUVR = 1.1 to align with autopsy data, 29 (3) a combination of three‐tier flortaucipir PET visual read and quantitation, in line with trial enrichment criteria for TRAILBLAZER‐ALZ2 (Figure S1 ), 8 , 29 and (4) an additional flortaucipir PET quantitation performed on a temporal region of interest capturing early (temporal) tau accumulation, using a cut‐off based on 2.5 times standard deviation of a cohort of young, cognitively normal subjects. 30 The study was carried out in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice guidelines. All patients provided informed consent. 2.2 Agreement between CSF and PET The agreement between CSF per label cut point and florbetapir‐PET‐based patient identification was assessed for (1) Fujirebio Lumipulse CSF Aβ42/Aβ40 in vitro diagnostic test (IVD) and florbetapir PET (visual read, 24.1 CL stratification, and 37 CL stratification) and (2) Roche Elecsys CSF P‐tau181/Aβ42 IVD and florbetapir PET (visual read, 24.1 CL stratification, and 37 CL stratification). The agreement between Roche Elecsys CSF P‐tau181/Aβ42 IVD per label cut point and flortaucipir‐PET‐based patient identification was also assessed for visual read, quantitation with 1.1 SUVR stratification, trial enrichment criteria, and early (temporal) tau stratification. Outcome measures included positive percent agreement (PPA), negative percent agreement (NPA), overall percent agreement (OPA), positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV). Two‐sided 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using the Wilson score method. A lower bound CI &gt;65% for both PPA and NPA was used to indicate high agreement between CSF and PET. The cut point for Elecsys CSF P‐tau181/Aβ42 was established to maximize agreement with amyloid PET as the reference standard. Given flortaucipir PET captures a later disease stage, the CSF test, stratified at the established cut point, is expected to have high sensitivity (PPA) but a lower specificity (NPA) for flortaucipir PET. This was previously reported. 31 Therefore, the lower bound CI &gt;65% was set to align with expectations of both PPA and NPA for Elecsys CSF P‐tau181/Aβ42 against flortaucipir PET. 2.3 Non‐inferiority of CSF to amyloid PET quantitation The non‐inferiority of Fujirebio Lumipulse CSF Aβ42/Aβ40 and Roche Elecsys CSF P‐tau181/Aβ42 to florbetapir PET clinical trial inclusion criteria for identifying AD pathology was also assessed (Figure S2 ). To determine non‐inferiority, both CSF assays and the quantified PET image, stratified at 37 CL, were compared with the reference standard: presence of AD pathology as determined by the approved amyloid PET visual read. The agreements between the florbetapir PET quantitation, stratified at 37 CL, and florbetapir PET visual read (PPV c ) and between the CSF assay and florbetapir PET visual read (PPV a ) were calculated. 32 The non‐inferiority margin was estimated as the difference in agreements, PPV c – PPV a . A prespecified difference of &lt;15% on the upper bound CI was used to indicate the non‐inferiority of CSF assays to florbetapir PET quantitation for identifying AD pathology. The non‐inferiority margin of 15% is consistent with regulatory guidance for non‐inferiority trials. 33 It represents the largest clinically acceptable difference and was selected based on the modest sample size of the study. Bootstrapped two‐sided 95% CIs were calculated. 2.4 Agreement between stratifications of PET (amyloid and tau) The agreement between the different PET stratifications was also evaluated. For amyloid PET the agreement between (1) florbetapir PET visual read and florbetapir PET quantitation, stratified at 24.1 CL, (2) florbetapir PET visual read and florbetapir PET quantitation, stratified at 37 CL, and (3) florbetapir PET quantitation stratified at 24.1 and 37 CL were evaluated. For tau PET the agreement between (1) flortaucipir PET visual read and flortaucipir PET quantitation stratified at 1.1 SUVR, (2) flortaucipir PET visual read and flortaucipir PET trial enrichment criteria, (3) flortaucipir PET visual read and early (temporal) tau PET stratification, (4) flortaucipir PET quantitation stratified at 1.1 SUVR and flortaucipir PET trial enrichment criteria, (5) flortaucipir PET quantitation stratified at 1.1 SUVR and early (temporal) tau PET stratification, and (6) flortaucipir PET trial enrichment criteria and early (temporal) tau PET stratification were evaluated. 2.5 Longitudinal clinical and cognitive measures As an exploratory analysis, the clinical and cognitive trajectories of flortaucipir‐PET‐negative patients were evaluated and compared between patients who were positive versus negative for AD pathology according to Roche Elecsys CSF P‐tau181/Aβ42. The difference in slopes in linear mixed‐effects models were evaluated for the Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE), Clinical Dementia Rating Sum of Boxes (CDR–SB), and Alzheimer's Disease Assessment Scale–Cognitive Subscale 13 (ADAS–Cog 13 ). John Wiley &amp; Sons, Ltd. 4 DISCUSSION This analysis of patients with MCI or dementia due to AD demonstrates that Fujirebio Lumipulse CSF Aβ42/Aβ40 and Roche Elecsys CSF P‐tau181/Aβ42 assays, used according to label thresholds, are in high agreement with and non‐inferior to amyloid PET criteria employed for patient selection in clinical trials. These findings support the interchangeability of CSF biomarkers and amyloid PET in identifying AD pathology in symptomatic patients potentially eligible for amyloid‐targeting therapies. Overall, an OPA of 86% to 94% was observed between Lumipulse CSF Aβ42/Aβ40 and amyloid PET and Elecsys CSF P‐tau181/Aβ42 and amyloid PET. The CSF assays, Lumipulse CSF Aβ42/Aβ40 and Elecsys CSF P‐tau181/Aβ42, were able to identify (rule in) the same patients as positively identified by amyloid PET quantification (PPV: 89% to 96%). Similarly, participants identified as negative by the CSF assays were identified as negative by the amyloid PET (NPV: 64% to 96%). The results are consistent with other studies showing high agreement between CSF Aβ42/Aβ40 and amyloid PET 34 , 35 , 36 , 37 , 38 , 39 , 40 , 41 , 42 and between CSF P‐tau181/Aβ42 and amyloid PET 34 , 35 , 42 , 43 for the identification of AD pathology. Though many analyses demonstrate high concordance between CSF Aβ42 and amyloid PET, several studies show that CSF Aβ42 ratios have an improved ability to predict amyloid PET positivity over CSF Aβ42 alone. 37 , 38 , 40 , 44 In the current study, the Aβ42/Aβ40 and P‐tau181/Aβ42 provided comparable agreement parameters, consistent with an analysis showing strong correlation between these assays in the assessment of amyloid pathology. 45 The current study provides further evidence of the diagnostic accuracy of CSF Aβ42/Aβ40 and P‐tau181/Aβ42, supporting the use of these assays as a reliable alternative to amyloid PET. Though several clinical trials of amyloid‐targeting therapies rely on PET to select patients with AD pathology, several studies have used CSF‐confirmed positivity as inclusion criteria. 7 , 8 , 9 , 46 PET‐ and CSF‐based biomarkers are both considered valid methods for identifying AD pathology in symptomatic patients. 3 Results from the present study and similar analyses further support their interchangeability. While patients identified as negative for AD pathology by Roche Elecsys CSF P‐tau181/Aβ42 were consistently identified as negative for tau pathology by flortaucipir PET, the CSF assay did not consistently positively identify the same individuals as flortaucipir PET. However, as the thresholds for the CSF assays were validated against a reference standard of amyloid PET and not tau PET, 47 and since widespread tau pathology is expected to be downstream of amyloid pathology in the pathophysiology of AD, 48 , 49 it was expected that a higher proportion of patients would be identified as positive for CSF P‐tau181/Aβ42 in comparison to positive for flortaucipir PET. There was higher agreement between the CSF assay and an early (temporal) tau stratification. Results also showed that patients identified as CSF‐positive but negative by flortaucipir PET had faster rates of clinical decline than patients negative for both assessments. Together, these findings suggest that current CSF assays using label thresholds may be accurate at identifying individuals at an earlier disease stage than widespread neocortical tau PET signal. This aligns with current thinking suggesting that amyloid PET or CSF positivity is associated with early changes in tau (e.g., peripheral hyperphosphorylated tau changes or localized aggregation in medial temporal regions) and is sufficient biomarker evidence to support an etiological diagnosis of AD. 50 Further, higher thresholds for CSF assays than those on current labels may better align with widespread neocortical tau PET. Limitations of this analysis include a modest sample size, particularly for comparisons using tau PET. ADNI participants are generally highly educated with few comorbidities and thus may not be representative of the general population. The analysis only included one approved amyloid PET tracer, though data were acquired from multiple centers and scanners and used CL thresholds, which enable more direct comparisons and pooled analyses across tracers. The analysis was also limited to comparisons of two CSF assays. However, they are the most recent and the only two automated in vitro CSF assays approved in both the United States and the European Union. 21 , 22 , 27 , 28 The high similarity in results between the CE‐marked CSF tests suggests that other well‐validated CSF assays for establishing the presence of amyloid may potentially be utilized to support diagnosis. Some strengths of this study include that label thresholds were applied meaning that the results are representative of findings applicable to the clinical setting, different PET stratifications were implemented demonstrating that the high concordance of CSF with amyloid PET is not dependent on the PET stratification method, a novel non‐inferiority analysis was undertaken demonstrating that approved CSF assays would select the same patient population as typical amyloid PET trial criteria. Overall, the findings indicate that currently available CSF assays using label thresholds are interchangeable with and non‐inferior to amyloid PET in detecting patients with AD pathology. CSF assays were in agreement with an early temporal tau PET positivity signal, and overall, the findings may suggest that current CSF assays identify individuals at an earlier disease stage than widespread neocortical tau PET. However, new strategies for tau, including new thresholds derived against widespread neocortical tau and selection/stratification based on regional tau PET measurements, should be considered. The use of CSF biomarkers as an alternative to PET biomarkers may increase access for AD diagnosis and, thus, improve equitable access to treatment.
