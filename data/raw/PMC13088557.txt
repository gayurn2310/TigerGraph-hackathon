NOTCH3 Mutations in CADASIL: a multicellular perspective on neurodegeneration

Cerebral Autosomal Dominant Arteriopathy with Subcortical Infarcts and Leukoencephalopathy (CADASIL) is a hereditary cerebral small-vessel disease caused by NOTCH3 cysteine-altering mutations. Clinical evidence, including early-onset migraines, psychiatric symptoms, and diffuse white-matter hyperintensities preceding strokes, suggests a nonvascular pathology. NOTCH3 is broadly expressed in neural progenitors, neurons, astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, and brain microvascular endothelial cells (BMECs), and is responsible for neurodevelopment, blood-brain barrier (BBB) homeostasis, glymphatic function, and myelination. In vitro studies suggest that mutant NOTCH3 disrupts hippocampal neurogenesis via impaired neural stem cell self-renewal, causes astrocytic aquaporin-4 (AQP4) mislocalisation leading to glymphatic failure, compromises endothelial integrity, and derails oligodendrocyte maturation. These dysfunctions can collectively perturb neurovascular unit (NVU) integrity, accelerating neurodegeneration independent of infarcts. This review puts together evidence for nonvascular NOTCH3 pathology, emphasizing its role in neuroglial development and NVU crosstalk. We aim to reframe CADASIL as a multicellular interface disorder that stems from neurodevelopmental pathways, suggesting that advanced 3D in vitro NVU models to unravel disease mechanisms leading to the new therapeutic strategies that target glial and neuronal NOTCH3 signalling to mitigate early symptoms and slow progression.

Introduction NOTCH3 protein and CADASIL Cerebral Autosomal Dominant Arteriopathy with Subcortical Infarcts and Leukoencephalopathy (CADASIL) stands as the most common monogenic form of cerebral small-vessel disease, with over 200 pathogenic variants identified within the NOTCH3 gene on chromosome 19 [ 1 – 4 ]. NOTCH3 encodes a transmembrane receptor integral to cell fate specification, vascular development, and tissue homeostasis, playing a particularly pivotal role within the arterial vasculature [ 5 , 6 ]. Pathogenic mutations in CADASIL typically alter the number of cysteine residues, usually introducing or omitting a cysteine, within one of the 34 epidermal growth factor-like repeat (EGFr) domains of the NOTCH3 ectodomain (N3ECD) [ 1 , 7 , 8 ]. This aberrant cysteine arrangement results in abnormal N3ECD oligomerization, protein misfolding, and the progressive accumulation of the misfolded proteins on the surface of vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) and pericytes, leading to the formation of the pathognomonic granular osmiophilic material (GOM) deposits, as observed via electron microscopy [ 7 , 9 – 13 ]. Genotype-phenotype correlations and the anatomical selectivity of CADASIL CADASIL exhibits a well-established genotype-phenotype gradient related to the position of cysteine-altering mutations within the N3ECD. Variants located in EGFr domains 1–6 consistently associated with earlier symptom onset, heavier magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) lesion burden, and more rapid clinical decline compared with mutations in EGFr domains 7–34, which often produce miler or more variable presentations [ 14 – 16 ]. These observations suggest that the biophysical and trafficking properties of mutant NOTCH3 differ across domains, potentially altering the extent of N3ECD accumulation, GOM formation, and downstream effects on vascular integrity [ 7 , 12 ]. Importantly, CADASIL pathology shows a distinct subcortical predilection. Long penetrating arteries supplying the deep white matter and basal ganglia are particularly susceptible to N3ECD/GOM-driven vessel wall thickening, fibrosis, and impaired vasoreactivity [ 1 , 17 ]. This anatomical vulnerability provides a vascular framework upon which neurovascular unit (NVU) dysfunction is superimposed and helps explain why subcortical systems, rather than cortical microcircuits, are disproportionately affected despite broad NOTCH3 expression across NVU cell types. Clinical manifestations of CADASIL Classically, neuropathological investigations have focused on VSMC degeneration [ 18 – 21 ], GOM deposition [ 9 , 10 , 22 ], and progressive fibrosis of arterioles leading to luminal stenosis [ 23 , 24 ], chronic cerebral hypoperfusion [ 25 , 26 ], and subsequent lacunar infarction within the subcortical white matter and basal ganglia [ 27 , 28 ]. Despite such vascular-centric patterns, the clinical progression of CADASIL reveals a predictable chronology: early-onset migraines (often with aura) in the third decade, the emergence of mood and psychiatric symptoms in the fourth decade, and overt ischemic strokes or cognitive decline manifesting only by mid-adulthood [ 1 , 29 , 30 ]. This temporal dissociation between symptom onset and ischemic events could suggest that overt infarctions represent late-stage outcomes of protracted dysfunction at the NVU level. Reconciling NVU dysfunction with subcortical predominance in CADASIL A defining clinical feature of CADASIL is the predominance of subcortical symptoms, including slowed processing speed, executive dysfunction, gait disturbance, mood changes, and apathy [ 31 – 33 ], with comparatively rare cortical manifestations such as aphasia, apraxia, or focal seizures, even in advanced disease [ 27 , 34 – 36 ]. This subcortical bias could reflect the selective vulnerability of long penetrating arterioles supplying deep white matter and basal ganglia structures, which are preferentially affected by NOTCH3-related arteriopathy. In this context, NVU dysfunction in CADASIL is unlikely to manifest as a spatially uniform cortical process. Instead available clinical, imaging, and pathological evidence suggests an involvement of subcortical structures and deep white matter, regions supplied by long penetrating arterioles [ 37 – 39 ]. NVU impairment within these territories may therefore affect subcortical networks, providing a plausible explanation for the characteristic cognitive slowing, executive dysfunction, and gait disturbance observed in CADASIL, while cortical functions are preserved. The neurovascular unit in CADASIL The NVU constitutes a highly dynamic, multicellular ensemble, including brain microvascular endothelial cells (BMECs), pericytes, astrocytes, neurons, and oligodendrocytes, which orchestrates cerebral homeostasis [ 40 ]. This consortium regulates hemodynamics, maintains the stringent integrity of the blood-brain barrier (BBB), couples neuronal activity to metabolic demands, and modulates the clearance of metabolic waste via the glymphatic system [ 40 – 44 ]. Disruption of NVU components and BBB function has also been implicated in the pathogenesis of a range of neurodegenerative diseases (NDDs) [ 45 – 49 ]. Emerging transcriptomic evidence has revealed abundant NOTCH3 expression across all principal NVU cell types, positioning this receptor as a molecular lynchpin in NVU integrity [ 50 ]. Single-cell RNA sequencing of human brain tissue confirmed that NOTCH3 transcripts are expressed in VSMCs, pericytes, astrocytes, hippocampal neurons, oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPCs), and BMECs, highlighting its functional breadth [ 50 ]. Functional analyses revealed that CADASIL-associated NOTCH3 mutations perturb canonical and noncanonical NOTCH3 signalling across these neural and glial lineages, resulting in: impaired hippocampal neurogenesis due to disrupted neural stem cell (NSC) proliferation and differentiation [ 51 – 53 ], glymphatic collapse owing to astrocytic aquaporin-4 (AQP4) mislocalization [ 54 ], BBB compromise from endothelial tight junction dysregulation [ 45 , 55 ], and hypomyelination secondary to OPC dysfunction [ 56 , 57 ] (Fig. 1 ). We critically evaluate the multifaceted roles of NOTCH3 in neural, glial, and vascular development, delineate the molecular mechanisms by which pathogenic NOTCH3 variants disrupt NVU synergy, and explore the limitations of current genetically engineered mouse models and in vitro disease platforms. We further propose next-generation, multilineage in vitro systems for investigating NOTCH3-driven pathophysiology at single-cell resolution. Crucially, we posit that essential neurodevelopmental functions of NOTCH3, particularly within the fetal and postnatal brain, establish latent vulnerabilities, possibly via subtle, cumulative disruptions in neurogenesis and NVU maturation, that become apparent only as overt neurodegeneration or vascular compromise in adulthood during periods of increased metabolic or hemodynamic stress [ 58 ]. This review highlights the value of considering neurodevelopmental and neurodegenerative mechanisms along a shared continuum in CADASIL, and underscores the potential role of early molecular insights in shaping future diagnostic and therapeutic strategies for NVU-related diseases. NOTCH3 in neural progenitors and neurons: compromised plasticity and neurodevelopmental legacy NOTCH signalling is an evolutionarily conserved pathway fundamental for regulating asymmetric cell division, progenitor cell maintenance, and neuronal differentiation during central nervous system development [ 59 ]. While NOTCH1 has been extensively characterized as the dominant Notch receptor responsible for embryonic neurodevelopment, NOTCH3 is increasingly recognized for its distinct and critical roles in both postnatal and adult NSC niches, thereby contributing to brain plasticity throughout life [ 60 ]. NOTCH3 expression is notably enriched within the subventricular zone (SVZ) bordering the lateral ventricles and the subgranular zone (SGZ) of the dentate gyrus in the hippocampus, regions that serve as specialized microenvironments that support the persistence of quiescent radial glia-like cells (RGLs) and foster lifelong neurogenesis [ 61 ]. These RGLs retain the remarkable capacity to generate new neurons and glia, a process crucial for cognitive adaptation, memory consolidation, and emotional regulation. Disruption of these developmental programs may have lasting neurological implications. Mechanisms underlying neurogenesis deficits The activation of NOTCH3 within NSCs plays a pivotal role in maintaining their self-renewal and undifferentiated state, primarily through the inhibition of proneural gene networks. This is accomplished via canonical downstream targets such as HES5 (hairy and enhancer of split 5), which acts as a transcriptional repressor of pro-differentiation factors such as ASCL1 (achaete-scute family bHLH transcription factor 1). Loss-of-function studies using global NOTCH3 knockout mice have demonstrated marked hypocellularity within the SGZ, a significant reduction in proliferative RGLs, and notable depletion of doublecortin-positive (DCX+) neuroblasts, collectively indicating major impairment in postnatal neuronal production [ 51 , 62 ]. These findings delineate a crucial role for NOTCH3 in sustaining the neural precursor pool and ongoing neurogenesis. Translational studies employing transgenic CADASIL mouse models that express the pathogenic human NOTCH3 R169C allele (Notch3 R169C ) have yielded similar evidence. In these models, a significant reduction in newly generated hippocampal neurons is observed, even in the absence of overt vascular pathology or the accumulation of GOM [ 51 , 62 ]. This finding supports the concept of a cell-intrinsic, NSC-autonomous defect in neuronal lineage specification as a direct consequence of mutant NOTCH3 expression. Furthermore, transcriptomic profiling of neural progenitors from these mutant models revealed broad dysregulation of NOTCH downstream effectors, perturbed cell cycle gene expression, and premature exit from the proliferative pool. Functional and neurodevelopmental implications Adult hippocampal neurogenesis is integral to a spectrum of higher-order cognitive and affective processes, including pattern separation (the ability to discriminate between similar spatial or contextual inputs), contextual memory formation, and the fine-tuning of emotional responses. Notch3 R169C mutant mice consistently demonstrate pronounced spatial memory deficits in the Morris water maze, aberrant extinction of conditioned fear responses, and heightened anxiety-like and depressive behaviours [ 51 , 62 ]. These phenotypes closely recapitulate the prodromal psychiatric manifestations reported in CADASIL patients, such as depression, apathy, and anxiety, often preceding cerebrovascular events or cognitive decline for decades [ 29 ]. This temporospatial dissociation between neuropsychiatric symptoms and vascular pathology further supports the hypothesis that NOTCH3 dysfunction in neural lineages is an early, primary contributor to CADASIL pathogenesis. While direct evidence from human CADASIL brain tissue remains technically challenging owing to limited access to premorbid samples, postmortem morphometric analyses have revealed a significant reduction in dentate gyrus volume and neural cell density in affected individuals, which is consistent with impaired neurogenesis and/or increased neuronal attrition [ 52 , 53 ]. Single-nucleus RNA sequencing of available CADASIL brain specimens confirmed the diminished expression of neurogenic and synaptic plasticity markers compared to age-matched controls, supporting the translational validity of the findings in NOTCH3 mice models [ 50 ]. Importantly, developmental studies have highlighted robust NOTCH3 expression in the fetal human brain, particularly within proliferative neuroepithelial layers and migratory streams [ 63 ]. These findings suggest that heterozygous NOTCH3 mutations may exert subtle but enduring neurodevelopmental effects, potentially priming the central nervous system (CNS) for increased vulnerability to subsequent vascular insults or environmental stressors. Early-life perturbations of neurodevelopmental programs may establish a latent state of vulnerability that shapes the timing, trajectory, and therapeutic responsiveness of CADASIL and potentially other neurodegenerative conditions. Although CADASIL has traditionally been viewed as an adult-onset neurovascular disorder, emerging evidence suggests a more extended diseases continuum in which developmental processes intersect with progressive vascular and neurodegenerative pathology [ 64 – 66 ]. NOTCH3 in Astrocytes: Glymphatic Dysfunction and Accelerated Brain Senescence Astrocytes, the predominant glial cell type in CNS, are responsible for orchestrating a wide array of neurophysiological processes, including but not limited to the structural assembly and maintenance of the BBB, the mediation of neurovascular coupling, neurometabolic support, and homeostatic regulation of extracellular ions and neurotransmitters. These star-shaped cells exhibit intricate morphologies, with protoplasmic processes that ensheath neurons, synapses, and critically, the cerebral microvasculature. A hallmark feature of astrocytes is the formation of specialized perivascular endfeet, which collectively envelop over 99% of the cerebral capillary surface area [ 67 , 68 ], creating a dynamic interface between neural and vascular compartments. These endfeet are densely populated by orthogonal arrays of particles (OAPs), which are molecular assemblies that are predominantly constituted by AQP4 water channels [ 69 ]. The polarized distribution of AQP4 within astrocytic endfeet is essential for the bidirectional movement of water and solutes, forming the anatomical substrate of the glymphatic system, a brain-wide perivascular pathway responsible for clearing interstitial solutes, metabolic waste, and neurotoxic proteins such as amyloid-β and tau from the parenchyma [ 70 ]. NOTCH3 is robustly and selectively expressed in human cortical astrocytes, with transcriptomic and proteomic analyses confirming particularly high enrichment at the perivascular endfoot membrane, indicating that NOTCH3 is a critical molecular regulator of astrocyte-vascular and astrocyte-neuronal interplay [ 54 ]. AQP4 polarization defects and glymphatic failure A pivotal study published in 2025 provided mechanistic evidence linking mutant NOTCH3 to glymphatic dysfunction in CADASIL through the dysregulation of AQP4 expression and localization [ 54 , 71 ]. Under physiological conditions, wild-type NOTCH3 signalling sustains AQP4 gene expression and its precise polarization at perivascular endfeet via a transcriptional axis involving NOTCH3, RUNX1 (Runt-related transcription factor 1), and c-MYB. Upon ligand-induced cleavage, the intracellular domain of NOTCH3 (NICD), upon ligand-induced cleavage, forms a regulatory complex with RUNX1, activating c-MYB, which in turn binds to the AQP4 promoter to drive expression of this essential water channel [ 72 ]. In CADASIL, pathogenic NOTCH3 mutations affect this axis at multiple levels. Mutant NOTCH3 fails to efficiently recruit RUNX1, leading to a pronounced attenuation of c-MYB activity and a substantial reduction in AQP4 mRNA and protein levels [ 72 ]. Equally critical, the proper targeting and clustering of AQP4 to astrocytic endfeet is severely disrupted; instead, AQP4 is aberrantly distributed throughout the astrocyte soma and processes, with a marked loss of OAP formation and endfoot polarity. This mislocalization fundamentally impairs the structural and functional integrity of the glymphatic system, diminishing both cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) influx into the brain parenchyma and the effective efflux of interstitial solutes, including not only metabolic byproducts but also potentially neurotoxic aggregates implicated in neurodegeneration [ 54 , 71 , 73 ]. Experimental validation of these defects comes from in vivo imaging studies and histological analyses in Notch3ᴿ¹⁷⁰ C CADASIL mouse models, which demonstrate significantly delayed glymphatic clearance kinetics for intrathecally administered tracers, as well as radiologically prominent, radiating perivascular spaces (PVS) on MRI, hallmarks of impaired glymphatic solute drainage [ 74 – 76 ]. Moreover, these glymphatic deficits directly correlate with progressive neuronal loss and cognitive dysfunction. Importantly, astrocyte-targeted restoration of AQP4 expression via adeno-associated virus (AAV)-mediated gene therapy in mutant mice not only normalizes AQP4 localization and OAP assembly at the endfeet but also rescues glymphatic flow and attenuates neuronal degeneration, establishing a clear link between AQP4 dysfunction, impaired waste clearance, and neurodegeneration in CADASIL [ 54 ]. An important clinical paradox in CADASIL is that migraine with aura, often prominent in young adults, tends to diminish or resolve as the disease progresses, despite worsening vascular pathology and NVU impairment [ 77 – 80 ]. While early susceptibility to cortical spreading depolarization (CSD) [ 81 ] may arise from astrocytic AQP4 mislocalisation, ionic dysregulation, or glymphatic inefficiency, these mechanisms alone cannot account for the late-phase remission. One plausible explanation is that progressive white-matter disconnection, axonal degeneration, and cortical excitability to a degree that no longer supports the initiation or propagation of CSD [ 82 ]. Additionally, microinfarcts and chronic hypoperfusion may dampen neuronal responsiveness and synchrony, thereby diminishing the neurophysiological substrate required for migraine aura. Given that arterial stiffening and impaired vasoreactivity are more pronounced in EGFr 1–6 variants [ 7 , 12 ], it is plausible that glymphatic dysfunction may may be exacerbated in these genotypes through reduced arterial pulsataility and altered perivascular flow dynamics, although direct comparative data are currently lacking. These mechanisms are hypothetical and highlight the need for longitudinal neurophysiology imaging studies to resolved this enduring clinical question. Glymphatic dysfunction beyond AQP4 mislocalisation While AQP4 mislocalisation represents a compelling mechanism linking astrocytic pathology to early glymphatic failure in CADASIL [ 71 , 83 ], recent insights into glymphatic physiology indicate that perivascular CSF flow depends on multiple convergent factors [ 73 , 83 ]. Vessel stiffening from N3ECD/GOM deposition, reduced arterial pulsatility [ 84 , 85 ], basement-membrane remodelling, neuroinflammatory activation, and altered perivascular extracellular matrix (ECM) can impair CSF-interstitial fluid exchange [ 12 , 17 ]. Enlarged perivascular spaces, an increasingly recognized, imaging hallmark in early CADASIL, likely reflect this broader disruption [ 86 , 86 – 89 ]. Thus, glymphatic failure in CADASIL can be viewed as an integrated NVU defect, arising from interactions between astrocytes, VSMCs, pericytes, and the ECM [ 85 ]. Broader astrocytic dysfunction In addition to the focal deficit in AQP4, emerging evidence implicates astrocytes in CADASIL pathogenesis. A recent mechanistic study in a CADASIL mouse model suggests an astrocyte-intrinsic link between mutant NOTCH3 and impaired AQP4 expression/polarization with glymphatic dysfunction [ 90 ]. More broadly, canonical NOTCH signalling orchestrates numerous astrocytic processes essential for CNS homeostasis [ 91 , 92 ]. This includes the transcriptional regulation of glutamate transporter GLT-1 (EAAT2), which is critical for the uptake and detoxification of synaptically released glutamate, thus guarding against excitotoxicity [ 93 – 97 ]. In CADASIL, additional disturbances in glutamate uptake, potassium buffering, and redox balance are plausible contributors to a pro-degenerative microenvironment, but direct evidence that NOTCH3 mutations drive these specific astrocytic changes in human CADASIL tissue remains limited, and many observations may reflect secondary responses to vascular/pericyte pathology [ 98 ]. Astrocyte-oligodendrocyte coupling is increasingly recognized as a central regulator of myelin maintenance and white-matter stability. In CADASIL, astrocytic dysfunction (particularly involving metabolic support), potassium buffering, or perivascular organization may plausibly compromise oligodendrocyte survival and myelin integrity [ 99 , 100 ]. This provides a potential mechanistic bridge between early astrocytic abnormalities and the appearance of white-matter hyperintensities (WMHs) even before overt ischaemic injury. Although emerging work supports this cross-talk framework, direct evidence in human CADASIL tissue remains limited, and further studies are needed to validate these interactions. In summary, astrocyte dysfunction as orchestrated by mutant NOTCH3 constitutes a central, nonvascular pillar of CADASIL pathogenesis, integrating glymphatic failure, excitotoxic risk, chronic oxidative stress, and disrupted neuronal-glial and glial-glial interactions into a cohesive pathogenic framework. This expanded recognition of astrocytic contributions builds a case for glial-targeted interventions in the quest for effective CADASIL therapeutics. NOTCH3 in oligodendrocytes: linking leukoencephalopathy to primary myelin dysregulation The defining neuroimaging feature in CADASIL patients is the presence of extensive leukoencephalopathy, observed as diffuse T2-weighted hyperintensities on MRI scans. These signal abnormalities indicate widespread myelin loss or disruption within the white matter tracts [ 28 , 101 ]. Historically, this white matter pathology was attributed primarily to chronic cerebral hypoperfusion and repeated microinfarcts secondary to the small vessel vasculopathy characteristic of CADASIL. However, imaging and pathological data reveal that WMHs can appear in asymptomatic NOTCH3 mutation carriers well before the onset of clinical stroke or overt ischemic events [ 2 , 75 , 102 ]. This temporal dissociation is a plausible indication of a primary defect in myelin integrity, suggesting that oligodendrocyte lineage dysfunction may be a direct and early consequence of mutant NOTCH3. In addition, the extent and tempo of white matter injury may differ substantially across NOTCH3 mutation classes, with EGFr 1–6 variants showing earlier and more extensive lesion burden. This raises the possibility that oligodendrocyte lineage cells in these patients are exposed to more severe or prolonged vascular and NVU stress. Developmental and postnatal roles of NOTCH3 in myelination NOTCH signalling has evolutionarily conserved roles in regulating oligodendrocyte development, differentiation, and myelination. Several studies have implicated NOTCH signalling broadly, and more limited evidence points to a role for NOTCH3. Mechanistically, active NOTCH signalling maintains the OPC pool by repressing premature differentiation via the HES5 transcriptional repressor, which in turn inhibits MYRF (myelin regulatory factor), a key driver of myelin gene expression and the initiation of the myelination program [ 1 – 4 ]. While this regulatory axis is well establish for canonical NOTCH signalling, its specific attribution to NOTCH3 in mammalian systems remains incompletely defined. In zebrafish models, targeted notch3 knockdown leads to a significant reduction in myelin basic protein (MBP) expression, depletion of OPCs, and severely impaired developmental myelination [ 103 ]. These findings support a role for NOTCH3 in oligodendrocyte lineage maintenance during development, although the extent to which similar mechanisms operate in mammalian myelination and adult white-matter maintenance is still under investigation. Possible pathogenic oligodendrocyte mechanisms in CADASIL Mutations in NOTCH3 may perturb OPC function and myelination through a constellation of interrelated mechanisms. First, dysregulated Jagged1-NOTCH3 signalling within mutant OPCs can alter canonical pathway activation, thereby impairing cell migration, proliferation, and the timely differentiation into mature, myelinating oligodendrocytes [ 104 ]. The result may be both a diminished pool of functional OPCs and insufficient replacement of damaged oligodendrocytes following injury. Second, NOTCH3 is implicated in the regulation of mitochondrial biogenesis and energy metabolism [ 105 ]. Mutant NOTCH3 may compromise the high bioenergetic and biosynthetic demands of myelin lipid and protein synthesis, rendering OPCs especially vulnerable to metabolic stress in the energetically demanding white matter environment. Third, the integrity and function of OPCs are intimately tied to their perivascular niche, where they receive paracrine trophic signals from adjacent vascular and perivascular cells, particularly pericytes. NOTCH3-mutant pericytes, as evidenced by altered platelet-derived growth factor receptor beta (PDGFRβ) signalling and impaired trophic support [ 42 , 106 ], may provide inadequate cues for OPC proliferation, survival, and differentiation, further destabilizing white matter homeostasis. Finally, beyond intrinsic defects, oligodendrocyte lineage cells in CADASIL can be exposed to a neurotoxic milieu generated by astrocyte and microglial dysfunction. Chronic oxidative stress, characterized by upregulated glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) and decreased antioxidant gene expression, along with the release of proinflammatory mediators, could create a hostile environment for myelin maintenance and repair, accelerating oligodendrocyte loss and demyelination. Advanced neuroimaging in presymptomatic CADASIL carriers and symptomatic patients reveals diffuse WMHs that cannot be fully explained by ischaemic injury alone [ 75 , 107 – 109 ]. Recent studies suggest that primary oligodendrocyte lineage dysfunction, impaired myelin turnover, or altered glial metabolic support may contribute to these early radiological findings [ 88 , 110 – 112 ]. These interpretations remain emergent, and existing data derive largely from model systems rather than human brain tissue. Nevertheless, they position oligodendrocytes, and their precursors, as important non-vascular cellular contributors to CADASIL pathogenesis. Expanding our understanding of these mechanisms is essential for the development of novel therapeutic strategies aimed at promoting remyelination, protecting oligodendrocyte populations, and ultimately preserving white matter integrity in individuals affected by NOTCH3 mutations. NOTCH3 in endothelial and perivascular cells: compromising the blood-brain barrier While VSMC degeneration is a hallmark of the classical neuropathology observed in CADASIL, emerging evidence indicates that NOTCH3 dysfunction within endothelial cells and pericytes at the neurovascular interface could play a direct role in compromising BBB integrity. This represents another pivotal axis in the multifactorial disease mechanism of CADASIL, potentially linking microvascular dysfunction to white matter degeneration and broader neurodegenerative processes. Dysfunction of Brain Microvascular Endothelial Cells BMECs form the principal physical and metabolic barrier of the BBB. These cells are characterized by highly specialized tight junction (TJ) complexes, composed of proteins such as claudin-5, occludin, and ZO-1, which collectively enforce a restrictive paracellular seal and regulate molecular trafficking between the blood and brain parenchyma [ 113 – 116 ]. The proper function of these TJs is essential for maintaining CNS homeostasis and neuronal viability. Recent transcriptomic and proteomic analyses have demonstrated that NOTCH3 is expressed within the brain vascular compartment, particularly in small vessels, highlighting its relevance to cerebral vascular integrity and injury responses. Functional studies using induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived BMECs from CADASIL patients have revealed barrier defects: these BMECs exhibit a significant reduction in transendothelial electrical resistance (TEER) [ 117 ]. Additionally, permeability assays revealed significantly increased passage of albumin and small-molecule tracers across the endothelial monolayer, indicating a compromised barrier [ 1 ]. Importantly, targeted knockdown of notch3 in healthy BMECs has been shown to impair barrier properties, supporting a role for NOTCH3 in BBB maintenance [ 1 ]. This loss of BBB selectivity may permit the entry of blood-derived neurotoxins, immune cells, and ions into the brain parenchyma, driving neuronal stress and inflammation and ultimately contributing to the progressive white matter damage characteristic of CADASIL. In addition, the severity and timing of endothelial dysfunction may vary across NOTCH3 mutation classes, as clinical and imaging studies show more aggressive vascular pathology in carriers of EGFr 1–6 variants [ 14 – 16 ]. Whether these domain-specific differences translate into earlier or more pronounced BBB stress remains an important question for future investigation. Dysfunction of pericytes Pericytes are specialized contractile cells embedded within the microvascular basement membrane, that are closely associated with BMECs. They are essential regulators of capillary stability, angiogenesis, and BBB maturation during both development and adulthood [ 113 , 119 ]. Pericytes maintain a delicate, bidirectional communication with BMECs, modulating endothelial proliferation, TJ assembly, and vascular permeability through paracrine and juxtacrine signalling. In the context of CADASIL, iPSC-based models incorporating NOTCH3-mutant pericytes have shown deficits in critical signalling axes, most notably the transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) and PDGFRβ pathways [ 120 ]. These mutant pericytes demonstrate reduced association with endothelial tubes in angiogenesis assays, visible as diminished pericyte coverage and fragmented capillary networks, underscoring their inability to support and stabilize the cerebral microvasculature. This insufficient pericyte-endothelial interaction further exacerbates BBB breakdown. Moreover, NOTCH3 mutations in pericytes may indirectly influence the local microenvironment by altering secretion profiles of trophic factors and extracellular matrix components, further impairing OPC survival, white matter repair, and synaptic plasticity. Thus, based on reported cellular models, CADASIL pathogenesis may involve a dual insult at the BBB: direct, cell-autonomous tight junction disruption in BMECs and indirect destabilization through compromised pericyte support. This convergent dysfunction severely impairs barrier function from the earliest stages of disease progression. The integration of these pathways highlights the complexity of NVU dysfunction in CADASIL, setting the stage for downstream neurodegenerative cascades and broadening therapeutic targets beyond the traditional focus on vascular smooth muscle cells. Integrated NVU dysfunction: from crosstalk failure to neurodegeneration The NVU operates not as a collection of independent cells, but rather as a functional syncytium, where coordinated cell-cell signalling via direct contact, paracrine factors, and ion fluxes ensures integrated homeostasis [ 40 ]. NOTCH3 mutations disrupt this delicate equilibrium at multiple critical nodes simultaneously, creating a self-reinforcing cycle of dysfunction, summarized in Table 1 . The failure of one component inevitably impacts others. For example, astrocytic endfeet detachment, caused by both AQP4 mislocaliation [ 54 , 69 ] and underlying capillary dysmorphology due to pericyte/VSMC loss [ 18 , 121 ], impairs potassium (K⁺) siphoning from the neuronal interstitial space. This ionic imbalance can trigger cortical spreading depression, the electrophysiological correlate of migraine aura, a hallmark early symptom of CADASIL [ 29 ]. Metabolic crisis ensues as the dysfunctional endothelium can fail to adequately transport glucose (potentially via downregulated GLUT1) [ 105 ], resulting in the starvation of energy-demanding neurons and oligodendrocytes. Glymphatic failure, stemming from astrocytic AQP4 defects [ 69 ] and potentially enlarged perivascular spaces [ 75 , 76 ], permits the accumulation of neurotoxic waste products, exacerbating neuronal stress and accelerating neurodegenerative processes. This intricate web of multicellular disruption explains why therapeutic strategies focused solely on repairing VSMCs or preventing macroscopic infarcts have consistently failed to arrest the progression of cognitive decline or psychiatric symptoms in CADASIL patients. Emerging evidence has supported the notion that this disease is fundamentally rooted in the breakdown of NVU communication and support systems. Limitations of traditional models and the promise of advanced NVU systems Although conventional [ 125 ], summarized in Table 2 , are valuable for identifying specific aspects of pathology, they inadequately capture the inherent complexity and multicellular crosstalk of the human NVU, limiting our understanding of integrated pathophysiology: Notch3 transgenic mice : These models reliably develop vascular GOM deposits, confirming the link between Notch3 mutation and classical vascular pathology [ 121 , 126 ]. However, they often exhibit minimal or no neurological phenotype (e.g., lack significant cognitive deficits or white matter lesions), failing to recapitulate key aspects of the human disease spectrum, likely due to species-specific compensatory mechanisms or redundant Notch signalling. The Notch3 R169C transgenic mice show valuable phenotypes, such as neurogenesis deficits and white matter abnormalities [ 74 ]. However, significant differences in NVU architecture, glymphatic system organization, and immune responses between mice and humans limit the direct translation of findings, particularly regarding complex cell-cell interactions within the unit. Zebrafish mutants : Excellent for rapid genetic screening and visualization of developmental processes, zebrafish models have revealed OPC and vascular defects related to Notch3 [ 103 ]. However, their nonmammalian brain structure, fundamentally different glymphatic system organization, and lack of complex gyrencephalic white matter make them unsuitable for modelling the integrated human NVU dysfunction or late-onset neurodegenerative aspects of CADASIL. iPSC 2D monoculture systems : Patient-specific iPSC-derived cells (e.g., neurons, astrocytes, and BMECs) are invaluable for studying the cell-autonomous effects of mutations in a human genetic background [ 118 , 120 ]. However, culturing these cells in isolation (monoculture) or simple cocultures on 2D plastic surfaces are unable to replicate the intricate 3D cytoarchitecture, diverse ECM interactions, mechanical cues, and hemodynamic forces (shear stress) present in the living brain. This severely limits their ability to model the emergent properties of the NVU as a functional unit. Compared with traditional 2D or non-vascularized organoid systems, vascularized organoids permit the study of vessel-parenchyma crosstalk, endothelial-pericyte interactions, and astrocytic endfoot organization within a shared microenvironment. Importantly, such systems allow interrogation of how NOTCH3 mutations may influence multiple NVU components simultaneously, including vascular maturation and perivascular signalling, but also neuronal network formation, astrocyte polarization, and oligodendrocyte lineage dynamics [ 129 ]. A recent review highlights that vascularized organoids are uniquely positioned to capture multicellular phenotypes, such as altered barrier properties, abnormal glial maturation, and disrupted neurovascular coupling, that cannot be fully resolved in other existing in vitro models [ 130 ]. Therefore, vascularized organoids are particularly well suited for addressing unresolved questions regarding early-life and developmental effects of NOTCH3 mutations, including whether subtle alterations in NVU assembly or neuroglial maturation create latent vulnerabilities that later manifest as small-vessel disease. While current organoid models remain limited by incomplete perfusion, variability, and immature vasculature, ongoing integration of microfluidic perfusion, controlled ECM environments, and standardized differentiation protocols is rapidly improving physiological relevance [ 131 ]. In the context of CADASIL, vascularized organoids can bridge developmental biology, network-level dysfunction, and long-term multicellular disease processes in a human genetic background. Microfluidic systems Microfluidic “BBB-on-chip” or “NVU-on-chip” systems represent a revolutionary approach designed to address these critical gaps. These platforms aim to emulate key aspects of the in vivo human brain microenvironment: 3D cytoarchitecture : Cells are embedded within biologically relevant 3D ECM scaffolds (e.g., collagen, fibrin, Matrigel, or decellularized brain matrix) that mimic the physical and biochemical properties of the brain parenchyma [ 132 , 133 ]. Multicellular complexity : Human iPSC-derived cell types, including BMECs, pericytes, astrocytes, neurons, and microglia, can be cocultured in spatially defined arrangements reflecting their natural positions (e.g., endothelial tubes lined by pericytes, surrounded by astrocytes and neurons) [ 134 ]. Physiological hemodynamics : Continuous perfusion of culture medium through vascularized microchannels applies physiological levels of shear stress, a crucial mechanical cue for endothelial health, barrier function, and cellular differentiation [ 135 ]. Real-Time functional readouts : Integrated electrodes enable continuous monitoring of TEER, a sensitive indicator of barrier integrity. Microfluidic design allows for controlled tracer perfusion and sampling to quantify permeability [ 136 ]. Embedded microelectrode arrays can potentially measure neuronal activity, on-chip sensors can also monitor cytokine release or metabolic changes. The AIM biotech organiX platform: a case study Commercial systems such as AIM Biotech’s organiX platform exemplify this advanced technology (Fig. 2 ). It facilitates the coculture of multiple iPSC-derived cell types within a perfusable, self-assembled 3D microvascular network embedded in a tunable hydrogel matrix [ 135 , 136 ]. Its potential applications for CADASIL research are substantial: Patient-specific vascularized organoids : Generate 3D NVU models using iPSCs derived from CADASIL patients with different NOTCH3 mutations, capturing patient-specific pathophysiology within a complex multicellular environment. Cell-specific genetic rescue : CRISPR activation can be used to selectively increase wild-type NOTCH3 expression, or cell-type-specific AAV vectors can be used to deliver therapeutic genes (e.g., AQP4 and CLDN5) exclusively to astrocytes, endothelial cells, or other target cells within the assembled NVU, allowing precise dissection of cell-autonomous versus non-cell-autonomous effects. Functional glymphatic assays : Introduce fluorescent solutes of varying sizes into the vascular channel or the parenchymal compartment and track their distribution and clearance dynamics under flow, directly assessing glymphatic function within the engineered tissue. Mechanistic Cascade Analysis : Test hypotheses regarding how a mutation in one cell type (e.g., pericytes) impacts the function of others (e.g., the endothelial barrier and astrocyte polarization). For example, specifically rescuing NOTCH3 signalling in astrocytes improves endothelial barrier function via improved endfeet coverage and signalling. Such sophisticated in vitro human models hold immense promise for unravelling the complex, multicellular pathophysiology of CADASIL and screening potential therapeutics in a more physiologically relevant context than ever before. Personalized Drug Testing : Leveraging these sophisticated BBB-on-chip and NVU-on-chip platforms, researchers can expose patient-derived 3D NVU models to an array of candidate therapeutics under physiologically relevant conditions. This enables high-throughput, longitudinal assessment of drug efficacy, toxicity, and mechanistic impact across diverse cellular compartments in a genotype-specific context. By recapitulating individual patient mutations and microenvironmental nuances, such platforms hold promise for stratified, precision-based pharmacological screening, accelerating the identification of optimal therapeutic regimens tailored to the unique molecular pathology of each CADASIL patient. Fig. 2 Schematic diagram of existing NOTCH3 and CADASIL model systems and NVU-like modeling using the OrganiX platform Therapeutic implications and future directions Repositioning CADASIL as a primary NVU disorder, rather than a simple vasculopathy, fundamentally reshapes the therapeutic landscape, revealing novel avenues for intervention aimed at restoring neuroglial and barrier functions: Possible targeted interventions across the NVU Astrocytes : Therapeutic strategies could focus on restoring AQP4 expression and polarization. This might involve AQP4-targeted gene therapy delivered via AAV vectors specifically designed to transduce astrocytes, or small molecule chaperones designed to increase AQP4 trafficking and correct its mislocalisation to endfeet [ 137 , 138 ]. Antioxidant therapies might also mitigate the chronic oxidative stress observed in CADASIL astrocytes. Neurons : Given the deficit in γ-secretase-mediated cleavage of mutant NOTCH3, γ-secretase modulators that specifically increase the cleavage efficiency of mutant NOTCH3 (without increasing amyloidogenic Aβ production, a concern with non-selective inhibitors) could be explored to increase productive NOTCH3 signalling in neural progenitors and mature neurons, potentially rescuing neurogenesis and synaptic function. Oligodendrocytes : Supporting OPC maturation and myelination could be achieved via the use of agonists of key trophic pathways, such as PDGFRβ agonists, to overcome deficits potentially caused by mutant pericytes or intrinsic OPC dysfunction. Remyelination-promoting agents under investigation for the treatment of multiple sclerosis might also be repurposed [ 139 , 140 ]. Endothelia : Stabilizing the BBB is paramount. Rho-associated kinase inhibitors (e.g., fasudil) show promise in preclinical studies of other BBB disorders because they promote actin cytoskeleton stability and enhance tight junction integrity [ 141 – 143 ]. Agents targeting endothelial transcytosis pathways (e.g., inhibiting caveolin-1) represent another strategy [ 144 ]. Pericytes : Restoring pericyte-endothelial crosstalk via PDGFRβ or TGF-β pathway modulation could improve capillary stability and endothelial health [ 145 ]. Critical unresolved questions and knowledge gaps Developmental impact : Do heterozygous NOTCH3 mutations subtly alter prenatal NVU assembly or neuroglial development, creating a latent vulnerability? Investigating NVU development in human iPSC-derived models or conditional knock-in mouse models with embryonic/neonatal NOTCH3 mutation expression is crucial. Mutation-specific stratification : Comparisons between EGFr 1–6 and 7–34 variants, into human NVU models may help detangle how domain-dependent NOTCH3 effects modulate vascular severity, secondary NVU dysfunction, and clinical heterogeneity. Combinatorial therapies : Given the multicellular dysfunction, will monotherapies suffice, or are combinations targeting both vascular protection (e.g., VSMC health) and specific neuroglial support (e.g., AQP4 correction, neurogenesis enhancement) necessary for maximal efficacy? How should such combinations be prioritized and sequenced? Biomarker development : Identifying reliable fluid biomarkers (CSF, blood) reflecting specific aspects of NVU dysfunction (e.g., glymphatic failure, BBB leakage, neuroaxonal injury, and astrocyte reactivity) is essential for early diagnosis, monitoring progression, and assessing therapeutic response in clinical trials. NVU penetration barriers : Even when mechanistically promising targets are identified, the fundamental challenge of achieving effective drug delivery across the dysfunctional NVU remains unresolved. CADASIL-associated BBB stiffening, altered transcytosis, and perivascular fibrosis may further alter molecular access to regions affect by the disease [ 146 , 147 ]. It remains unclear which delivery platforms, receptor-mediated transcytosis [ 144 , 148 ], nanoparticle-based carriers [ 149 ], or transient BBB-modulating approaches, can reliably achieve therapeutically meaningful concentrations in the human CADASIL brain. Determining which delivery strategies align best with CADASIL NVU biology is therefore a critical translational priority. Future research imperatives Mapping the precise spatiotemporal expression patterns of NOTCH3 within the developing and adult human brain using high-resolution techniques such as spatial transcriptomics integrated with brain atlases is essential. Employing multiomics approaches (spatial transcriptomics, proteomics, metabolomics) directly on microdissected NVU components or single cells isolated from CADASIL patient tissue, postmortem or potentially via biopsies, can provide unprecedented molecular insights. Validating the predictions made by NVU-on-chip models against clinical data, such as patient CSF biomarker profiles (e.g., the albumin ratio for BBB integrity, neurofilament light for axonal injury, and GFAP for astrocytosis) and advanced neuroimaging metrics (e.g., diffusion tensor imaging for white matter integrity, dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI for BBB permeability, and PVS volume for glymphatic function), is important to establish their translational relevance. Conclusion CADASIL exemplifies how a monogenic disorder initially defined by its vascular pathology may also engage broader neurodevelopmental pathways and neurodegenerative processes across the NVU. While VSMC degeneration, GOM deposition, and small vessel dysfunction remain central and defining features of the disease, accumulating evidence suggests that NOTCH3 signalling perturbations extend beyond the vascular compartment and may influence astrocytes, neurons, oligodendrocytes, and endothelia in interconnected ways. Emerging data suggests that astrocytic AQP4 mislocalisation and glymphatic impairment, deficits in hippocampal neurogenesis compromising plasticity, and intrinsic myelin instability or impaired maintenance driven by oligodendrocyte lineage dysfunction may contribute to clinical features such as migraine with aura, mood disorders, and diffuse white matter changes that often precede overt ischaemic injury. However, the extent to which these phenomena represent primary drivers of disease, secondary consequences of evolving vascular pathology, or context-dependent amplifiers of neurovascular dysfunction remains unresolved. In particular, clinical features such as the subcortical predominance of symptoms and the paradoxical remission of migraine with disease progression underscore the need to interpret NVU dysfunction within the anatomical constraints of small-vessel architecture and progressive network disconnection. Collectively, current evidence supports a conceptual framework in which CADASIL pathogenesis unfolds through temporally layered mechanisms. A potential early phase may involve subtle developmental alterations in NVU assembly or resilience arising from heterozygous NOTCH3 mutations, establishing latent vulnerabilities without overt clinical expression. This may be followed, decades later, by a second phase driven by progressive vascular pathology (VSMC degeneration, hypoperfusion, and microinfarcts), combined with age-related decline and the chronic impact of NVU dysfunction (BBB leakage, neuroinflammation, and toxin accumulation), triggers the full manifestation of the disease. Importantly, this framework does not supplant the established vascular paradigm of CADASIL, but rather situates multicellular NVU dysfunction as an interacting and potentially modulating component of disease evolution. Viewing CADASIL through this integrated framework highlights broader implications for adult-onset neurological disorders, suggesting that genetically driven perturbations of vascular-glial-neuronal interactions may exert long-lasting effects that only manifest clinically when compounded with aging and vascular stress. Advanced 3D human NVU models, that recapitulate this multicellular complexity, offer powerful and complementary tools to unravel the intricate cascade of events initiated by NOTCH3 mutations across the NVU. While significant challenges remain, particularly in effective brain delivery and in translating mechanistic insights into disease-modifying therapies, such integrated experimental approaches may enable rational development of combinatorial strategies targeting both vascular integrity and neuroglial resilience. Together, these efforts hold promise for refining our understanding of CADASIL pathogenesis and, more broadly, for informing therapeutic approaches to small-vessel related cognitive impairment.
