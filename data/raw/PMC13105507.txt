Memantine for Premature Atrial Contractions: A Phase 2 Randomized Clinical Trial

BACKGROUND: Premature atrial contractions (PACs) are independently associated with atrial fibrillation, stroke, and heart failure, yet no pharmacological therapy is approved for PAC suppression. Experimental studies have identified a functional cardiac glutamatergic system in which N-methyl-D-aspartate receptors regulate atrial electrophysiology. Preclinical studies show that pharmacological antagonism of N-methyl-D-aspartate receptors with memantine suppresses atrial arrhythmias. METHODS: We conducted an investigator-initiated, phase 2, multicenter, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial. Symptomatic adults with frequent PACs (≥1000/24 h) were randomly assigned to receive memantine or placebo for 6 weeks. The primary end point was the percentage change in mean 24-hour PAC count from baseline to the end of treatment. The primary analysis was performed in the intention-to-treat population. Prespecified secondary end points included the responder rate (≥50% PAC reduction), percentage change in nonsustained atrial tachycardia burden, and cumulative incidence of new-onset atrial fibrillation. RESULTS: Among 241 patients included in the efficacy analysis, memantine resulted in a greater reduction in PAC count than placebo (between-group difference, 47.1 percentage points; P =0.0045). The responder rate was higher with memantine than with placebo (52.4% versus 23.1%; P &lt;0.0001). Memantine also reduced nonsustained atrial tachycardia burden (between-group difference, 30.98 percentage points; P =0.0043) and was associated with a lower cumulative incidence of new-onset atrial fibrillation (4.8% versus 23.9%; P &lt;0.0001). No clinically meaningful differences were observed in electrocardiographic intervals or left ventricular function, and no drug-related serious adverse events occurred. CONCLUSIONS: In patients with frequent symptomatic PACs, memantine reduced atrial ectopy and atrial tachyarrhythmia burden and demonstrated a favorable safety profile. These findings provide proof of concept for a novel, non–ion channel–based therapeutic strategy targeting the cardiac glutamatergic system. Clinical Perspective What Is New? Premature atrial contractions (PACs) are commonly seen in clinical practice and are associated with increased risks of atrial fibrillation, stroke, and heart failure. However, no pharmacological therapy is currently approved for PAC suppression. This phase 2, multicenter, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial demonstrates that memantine, a well-established drug for Alzheimer disease, significantly reduces PAC burden in patients with symptomatic PACs. Memantine treatment also resulted in a lower burden of nonsustained atrial tachycardia and substantially reduced the cumulative incidence of new-onset atrial fibrillation without adversely affecting ventricular conduction or repolarization. What Are the Clinical Implications? These findings provide proof of concept for targeting the cardiac glutamatergic system as a novel, non–ion-channel–based therapeutic strategy for managing atrial arrhythmias. By selectively modulating atrial electrophysiological properties without affecting ventricular electrical function, memantine presents a potentially safer pharmacological approach for patients with frequent PACs, a population who currently lacks well-established pharmacological treatments. Although further long-term studies are needed to confirm the durability of these effects and their effect on hard clinical outcomes, targeting PACs with memantine could represent an upstream strategy to reduce the progression to atrial tachyarrhythmias, including atrial fibrillation. Premature atrial contractions (PACs) are common in clinical practice and have long been considered benign. However, accumulating epidemiological and clinical evidence has established that frequent PACs are independently associated with an increased risk of atrial fibrillation (AF), ischemic stroke, heart failure, and all-cause mortality. 1 – 8 Beyond serving as markers of atrial vulnerability, frequent PACs are increasingly recognized as active contributors to atrial electrical and structural remodeling, thereby facilitating the initiation and maintenance of AF. 2 , 6 These observations support the concept that suppression of frequent PACs may represent an upstream therapeutic strategy to modify the natural history of atrial arrhythmogenesis. Despite this growing recognition, no pharmacological therapy is currently approved specifically for PAC suppression. 9 – 12 The absence of effective drug therapy for PACs largely reflects fundamental safety limitations of conventional antiarrhythmic agents. Historical trials such as CAST (Cardiac Arrhythmia Suppression Trial) and SWORD (Survival With Oral d-Sotalol) demonstrated that drugs capable of suppressing ectopy through ion channel blockade paradoxically increased mortality, primarily because of proarrhythmic ventricular effects. 13 – 16 These risks are intrinsic to their mechanisms of action: Sodium and potassium channel blockers nonselectively alter cardiac conduction and repolarization in both atrial and ventricular myocardium, creating a substrate for malignant ventricular arrhythmias. 13 , 15 , 16 As a consequence, the potential benefits of atrial ectopy suppression have been outweighed by unacceptable safety concerns, leaving patients with frequent PACs without a safe, mechanism-based pharmacological option. These limitations underscore the need for therapeutic approaches that dissociate atrial antiarrhythmic efficacy from ventricular proarrhythmic risk. Recent experimental work has identified a previously unrecognized endogenous glutamatergic signaling system within the heart, particularly in atrial cardiomyocytes. This system is centered on ionotropic glutamate receptors, including the N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor subtype, which plays a crucial role in regulating atrial excitability and electrophysiological stability. It is important to note that functional expression of this glutamatergic system appears to be largely confined to atrial myocardium, providing a mechanistic basis for atrium-selective modulation. 17 , 18 Memantine is a noncompetitive antagonist of the NMDA receptor, commonly prescribed for the treatment of moderate to severe Alzheimer dementia. It is generally well tolerated and has an established favorable safety profile. 19 Recent studies have suggested that memantine can inhibit atrial arrhythmogenesis. By blocking NMDA receptors, memantine reduces ionotropic glutamate receptor–mediated inward currents, which raises the threshold for action potential initiation, effectively preventing and terminating AF in animal models. 20 These findings provide evidence supporting the hypothesis that the glutamatergic system in the atrium plays a pivotal role in regulating atrial electrophysiology. Therefore, targeting the cardiac glutamatergic system may offer a novel, non–ion channel–based therapeutic strategy for managing atrial arrhythmias. To determine whether this mechanistically distinct approach translates into clinical benefit, we conducted the STOP-AP trial (Efficacy and Safety of Memantine in the Treatment of Frequently Symptomatic Atrial Premature Beats), registered on ClinicalTrials.gov , an investigator-initiated, phase 2, multicenter, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study. The trial was designed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of memantine in patients with frequent symptomatic PACs and to assess its effects on atrial ectopy and subsequent atrial tachyarrhythmias. Methods The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request. Because of patient consent restrictions, individual participant data cannot be shared. However, the aggregate results, study protocol, statistical analysis plan, and analysis code are available on request. Trial Design and Oversight This investigator-initiated, multicenter, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, phase 2 trial was conducted at 15 tertiary hospitals in China. The trial protocol was approved by the institutional review board or independent ethics committee at each participating center, and all participants provided written informed consent before enrollment. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice guidelines. The trial was overseen by a steering committee, which was responsible for the design, conduct, and interpretation of the study. An executive committee provided operational oversight. An independent data monitoring committee, prespecified in the protocol, convened but did not perform the planned interim analysis because enrollment was nearly complete by the time the prespecified trigger point was reached (defined as 50% of participants having completed the week 6 primary end-point assessment). The members of these committees are listed in the Supplemental Material . The authors vouch for the accuracy and completeness of the data and for the fidelity of the trial to the protocol. Participants Eligible participants were adults 18 to 80 years of age with symptomatic PACs, defined as ≥1000 PACs per 24 hours on baseline 72-hour ambulatory electrocardiographic monitoring. Participants were required to report symptoms attributable to PACs such as palpitations or dizziness. Key exclusion criteria included a documented history of atrial arrhythmias (AF, atrial flutter, or sustained atrial tachycardia) within the preceding 6 months or detected at baseline; ventricular arrhythmias other than brief, nonsustained ventricular tachycardia during sleep; myocardial infarction, stroke, coronary revascularization, or cardiac surgery within 3 months before screening; left ventricular ejection fraction ≤40% or New York Heart Association class III or IV heart failure; and use of amiodarone within 4 weeks or other antiarrhythmic drugs (including traditional Chinese medicines with antiarrhythmic effects) within 1 week before screening. Additional eligibility criteria are provided in the Supplemental Material . Randomization and Study Treatment Participants were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to receive memantine or matching placebo. Randomization was stratified according to baseline 24-hour PAC count (≥5000 versus &lt;5000) and age (≥65 years versus &lt;65 years) and was implemented through a centralized, web-based randomization system. The allocation sequence was generated by an independent statistician to ensure allocation concealment. Memantine was administered orally at a dose of 5 mg twice daily for the first week, followed by 10 mg twice daily for the subsequent 5 weeks. This titration regimen was selected on the basis of integrated safety and efficacy considerations. Matching placebo tablets were identical in appearance and administered according to the same schedule. After completion of the 6-week treatment period, participants entered a 2-week off-treatment observation period. Follow-up assessments were conducted at screening, week 1 (telephone visit), week 4 (midtreatment visit), week 6 (end-of-treatment visit), and week 8 (end-of-study visit), with an allowable window of ±3 days for in-person visits ( Figure S1 ). Electrocardiographic Monitoring and Adjudication Continuous 72-hour ambulatory electrocardiographic monitoring was performed at baseline and at each in-person follow-up visit with the Weiling VV-330 device, a validated single-lead patch recorder. This device was used consistently across all study sites to ensure uniformity in data collection. The algorithms of the device for arrhythmia detection were standardized across sites, and all recordings were transmitted to an independent core laboratory for standardized, blinded analysis. Automated algorithms were used to quantify PACs and other atrial arrhythmias, and all recordings underwent independent review by 2 blinded cardiologists. Discrepancies were resolved by a senior electrophysiologist. Quality assurance procedures included random reanalysis of 10% of recordings, and core laboratory staff remained blinded to treatment assignment throughout. Detailed adjudication criteria and quality control procedures are provided in the Table S1 . Outcomes The primary end point was the percentage change in mean 24-hour PAC count from baseline to week 6, assessed by serial 72-hour ambulatory electrocardiographic monitoring ( Table S2 ). Key secondary end points were prespecified and analyzed with a fixed-sequence testing procedure to control the type I error rate. These end points included the proportion of responders (defined, consistent with standards in β-blockers for premature ventricular complexes, 20 , 21 as participants achieving a ≥50% reduction in mean 24-hour PAC count from baseline to week 6), the percentage change in nonsustained atrial tachycardia (NSAT) burden from baseline to week 6 (defined as the proportion of total recording time occupied by NSAT episodes), and the cumulative incidence of new-onset AF from randomization through weeks 4, 6, and 8 ( Table S2 ). Additional secondary end points included other rhythm-based measures (eg, mean 24-hour number of NSAT or sustained atrial tachycardia episodes), the incidence of other new-onset atrial arrhythmias, and patient-reported quality-of-life scores assessed with the 36-Item Short Form Health Survey. These analyses were considered exploratory and were not adjusted for multiplicity. Safety end points included the incidence of adverse events (AEs), serious AEs, laboratory abnormalities, and clinically relevant changes in electrocardiographic parameters. Safety assessments comprised serial vital signs, laboratory testing, transthoracic echocardiography (including left ventricular ejection fraction and left atrial diameter, 12-lead ECG, and ambulatory electrocardiographic monitoring; Table S3 ). Statistical Analysis The primary efficacy analysis was conducted in the full analysis set, defined according to the intention-to-treat principle and including all randomized participants who received at least 1 dose of study medication. Safety analyses included all participants who received at least 1 dose of the study medication. The primary end point was analyzed with ANCOVA) with adjustment for baseline PAC count and age. Missing data were handled by multiple imputation using 20 imputed datasets, with estimates combined according to the Rubin rules. Key secondary end points were analyzed hierarchically with a prespecified fixed-sequence testing procedure. For continuous end points, the between-group effect was reported as the least-squares mean difference, along with its 2-sided 95% CI and P value. Binary outcomes were analyzed with Cochran-Mantel-Haenszel tests, with stratification by baseline PAC burden and age. Treatment effects are reported as absolute risk differences, relative risks, and corresponding 95% CIs. Prespecified subgroup analyses were performed to assess the consistency of the treatment effect across demographic and clinical characteristics. Sensitivity analyses were conducted with mixed models for repeated measures to evaluate the robustness of the primary end point. All statistical tests were 2 sided, with a significance level of 0.05. Analyses were performed with SAS version 9.4. Sample Size Determination The sample size calculation was based on a preliminary single-arm study (n=30) in which memantine reduced PAC burden by 75.4±5.8%. Given the limited sample of the pilot study, a conservative treatment effect (50% reduction in PAC burden) was assumed for the memantine group. A placebo effect of a 30% reduction in PAC burden (SD, 35%) was assumed for sample size calculation, accounting for both spontaneous PAC variability and the potential effect of enhanced clinical follow-up. 22 , 23 With these parameters, a total of 256 participants provided 95% power to detect a between-group difference at a 1-sided α of 0.025, allowing for a 15% dropout rate. Although the power calculation was based on a 1-sided test (α=0.025), all statistical tests in the primary analysis were 2 sided (α=0.05), in line with standard clinical trial reporting conventions. This 2-sided approach ensures a conservative inference framework, considering both directions of the treatment effect. Additional Secondary Outcomes The percentage reduction in the number of NSAT episodes from baseline to week 6 was greater in the memantine group than in the placebo group (between-group difference, 26.46 percentage points; 95% CI, 15.02–37.90). The incidence of other new-onset atrial arrhythmias, including sustained atrial tachycardia and atrial flutter, was numerically lower in the memantine group; however, these analyses were exploratory and not adjusted for multiple comparisons. No significant between-group differences were observed in other rhythm-based measures or in quality-of-life scores assessed with the Short Form Health Survey ( Table S7 ). Safety Outcomes Among the 241 patients who received at least 1 dose of study medication, AEs occurred in 49.2% of patients in the memantine group and 33.3% of those in the placebo group. Serious AEs were infrequent and occurred at similar rates in the 2 groups (1.6% with memantine versus 0.9% with placebo). No drug-related serious AEs were reported in either group (Table 3 ). Adverse Events in the Safety Population* Drug-related treatment-emergent AEs were more frequent in the memantine group than in the placebo group (23.1% versus 13.7%), with dizziness, constipation, asthenia, and somnolence being the most commonly reported events. Most drug-related treatment-emergent AEs were mild or moderate in severity. Discontinuation of study treatment due to drug-related treatment-emergent AEs was low (1.6% with memantine versus 0.9% with placebo), and no deaths occurred during the trial (Table 3 and Table S8 ). No clinically meaningful differences were observed between treatment groups in electrocardiographic parameters (heart rate, PR interval, QRS duration, or Fridericia-corrected QT interval), echocardiographic measures (left ventricular ejection fraction or left atrial diameter), or laboratory indices, including hepatic and renal function markers and NT-proBNP (N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide) levels ( Tables S9 and S10 ), with minimal and comparable changes in Fridericia-corrected QT interval and QRS duration from baseline to week 6 ( Table S9 ). Discussion In this investigator-initiated, multicenter, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled phase 2 trial, treatment with memantine significantly reduced PAC burden compared with placebo in patients with frequent symptomatic PACs. This reduction was accompanied by parallel decreases in NSAT burden and a substantially lower cumulative incidence of new-onset AF without clinically meaningful effects on ventricular conduction or repolarization and without drug-related serious AEs. Together, these findings provide clinical proof of concept that targeting the cardiac glutamatergic system represents a viable, non–ion channel–based strategy for atrial arrhythmia modulation. PACs as an Underrecognized Therapeutic Target Although PACs have traditionally been viewed as benign incidental findings, a growing body of epidemiological and mechanistic evidence has established frequent PACs as potent predictors of AF, stroke, heart failure, and mortality. 1 – 8 Despite this recognition, PACs have remained a neglected therapeutic target, in part because available antiarrhythmic drugs were developed primarily to suppress ventricular ectopy or sustained arrhythmias and are limited by proarrhythmic risk. Prior landmark trials demonstrated that ventricular ectopy suppression through ion channel blockade can paradoxically increase mortality, effectively precluding pharmacological treatment of atrial ectopy. 13 – 16 Against this background, the present trial helps address an important unmet need by suggesting that selective suppression of PACs may be achievable with a mechanistically distinct and potentially safer therapeutic approach. Association Between PAC Reduction and Downstream Atrial Arrhythmias A key finding of this study is that effective PAC suppression was associated with downstream reductions in atrial tachyarrhythmias, including AF. This observation supports the concept that frequent PACs are not merely markers of atrial disease but may actively contribute to atrial arrhythmogenesis by promoting electrical instability and triggering reentrant or focal activity. 2 , 6 By substantially reducing PAC burden, memantine may attenuate this upstream trigger, thereby interrupting progression along the arrhythmia cascade from atrial ectopy to sustained tachyarrhythmias. Although the present trial was not designed or powered to establish definitive prevention of AF, the consistent reductions observed across PACs, NSAT, and AF incidence suggest biologically coherent effects rather than isolated findings. Differential Temporal Effects on PACs and AF An important observation of this trial is the temporal dissociation between the effects of memantine on PACs and on AF. Suppression of PACs was most evident at week 6, whereas little effect was observed at week 4 or week 8, whereas the reduction in AF incidence emerged earlier and persisted beyond treatment discontinuation. This pattern suggests fundamental mechanistic differences between PACs and AF. 1 – 4 Electrophysiologically, PACs act predominantly as trigger events arising from focal atrial excitability or short-lived reentrant activity, whereas AF is sustained by a vulnerable atrial substrate characterized by conduction heterogeneity, dispersion of refractoriness, and impaired electrical integration across the atrial myocardium. These substrate-level properties are more amenable to modulation by therapeutic interventions that reduce electrical heterogeneity and stabilize atrial conduction. 15 , 16 , 24 – 27 Experimental studies have shown that modulation of the cardiac glutamatergic system and NMDA receptor signaling can attenuate atrial electrical heterogeneity and improve conduction stability, effects that are particularly relevant to AF initiation and maintenance. 17 – 19 , 28 Accordingly, memantine may exert effects on AF at an earlier stage and maintain a degree of efficacy by improving atrial substrate vulnerability, even when its influence on PACs is temporally limited. Symptomatic PACs are commonly treated empirically with β-blockers or nondihydropyridine calcium channel blockers. Class Ic agents (eg, flecainide) are used selectively in patients without significant structural heart disease because of contraindications and safety considerations. However, the effects on atrial ectopy and AF burden are variable, and randomized evidence is limited in cohorts defined primarily by frequent PACs. Accordingly, this mechanism-driven proof-of-concept trial was not designed as a definitive active-comparator study. A pragmatic, multicenter randomized trial with an active control arm is needed to test whether memantine provides incremental benefit beyond usual care for PAC burden, symptoms, and long-term AF outcomes. A Non–Ion Channel–Based Mechanism for Arrhythmia Modulation The mechanistic implications of these results are particularly noteworthy. Unlike conventional antiarrhythmic agents that target sodium or potassium channels and exert nonselective effects on atrial and ventricular myocardium, memantine antagonizes the NMDA receptor, a key component of an endogenous glutamatergic signaling system that is functionally enriched in atrial cardiomyocytes. Preclinical studies have demonstrated that this system modulates atrial excitability and conduction while exerting minimal influence on ventricular electrophysiology. 17 – 19 , 28 The absence of clinically meaningful changes in heart rate, PR interval, QRS duration, or Fridericia-corrected QT interval in this trial is consistent with this atrium-selective mechanism and provides a plausible explanation for the favorable safety profile observed. An Upstream Therapeutic Shift in the Management of Atrial Arrhythmias From a clinical perspective, these findings suggest a potentially informative shift in the conceptual approach to the management of atrial arrhythmias. Rather than only relying on downstream rhythm control strategies that target established AF or atrial flutter, early intervention aimed at suppressing frequent atrial ectopy may represent an upstream approach to modifying arrhythmia risk. Current management strategies for AF emphasize risk factor modification and treatment of established arrhythmia but do not address atrial ectopy as a therapeutic target. 15 , 16 , 29 – 32 Memantine, a drug with an established neurological safety record, may therefore offer a novel therapeutic option for patients with symptomatic PACs, a population for whom no approved pharmacological therapy currently exists. Methodological Context for Elevated AF Detection The incidence of AF observed in this trial was higher than that typically reported in general community cohorts. 1 – 4 Several factors likely contributed to this observation. First, the study population was enriched for arrhythmic risk, with a high baseline PAC burden and a high prevalence of concomitant NSAT, features that may reflect a pre-AF substrate. 2 , 5 Second, intensive rhythm surveillance with repeated 72-hour ambulatory electrocardiographic monitoring substantially increased sensitivity for detecting paroxysmal AF compared with standard 24-hour monitoring. 3 , 7 These methodological considerations likely enhanced event detection and should be taken into account when AF rates are compared across studies. Furthermore, intensified monitoring may detect preexisting subclinical AF, meaning that the detected episodes do not solely represent new-onset disease during the trial. Although randomization helps balance unmeasured factors on average, residual confounding due to chance remains possible because of the limited sample size. Therefore, the AF findings should be interpreted with caution and require validation in larger, adequately powered studies. Limitations This study has several limitations. The follow-up duration was relatively short and does not allow assessment of long-term efficacy or durability of benefit. Although intensive monitoring improved arrhythmia detection, transient or asymptomatic episodes occurring outside monitoring windows may still have been missed. This limitation underscores the potential value of continuous monitoring devices such as implantable loop recorders in future studies designed to accurately quantify arrhythmia burden. Exploratory secondary analyses were not adjusted for multiplicity and should be interpreted cautiously. In addition, the study population consisted predominantly of Han Chinese patients with high PAC burdens, which may limit generalizability to broader populations ( Table S11 ). Although no new safety concerns were identified in this trial, rare AEs such as bradycardia or QTc prolongation have been reported with memantine in other clinical contexts and warrant continued vigilance in future studies. 33 , 34 Conclusions This phase 2 randomized trial demonstrates that targeting the cardiac glutamatergic system with memantine significantly reduces atrial ectopy and is associated with lower atrial tachyarrhythmia burden in patients with frequent symptomatic PACs. These findings establish a novel, mechanistically distinct, non–ion channel–based therapeutic approach to atrial arrhythmia modulation. Larger randomized trials with longer follow-up will be required to confirm the durability of these effects, to define their effect on clinical outcomes, and to determine whether early suppression of atrial ectopy can ultimately reduce the incidence of AF and its associated complications.
