Naringenin-loaded nanoparticles ameliorate scopolamine-induced neurotoxicity

Background Naringenin has shown attractive neuroprotective effects; however, it is characterized by low oral bioavailability. Naringenin-loaded nanoparticles (Nar-NPs) were prepared for enhanced delivery, and their effects in scopolamine-induced cholinergic hypofunction model were tested, either alone or in combination with donepezil. Methods Nar-NPs were developed via solvent evaporation with Span-80/Tween-80 and then characterized for morphological characteristics (TEM), hydrodynamic size/PDI, and zeta potential (DLS/ELS). Encapsulation efficiency (EE%), drug loading (DL%), and stability in simulated physiological buffer were also determined. Three different batches were used to evaluate the reproducibility of the formulation. Male mice were injected with scopolamine (3 mg/kg, i.p.) and orally administered Nar-NPs (37.75 mg/kg) or Nar-NPs combined with donepezil (10 mg/kg). Results were evaluated as Morris Water Maze, oxidative stress and inflammatory markers, lipid profile, qRT-PCR, and histopathology. Molecular docking analyses examined possible bonds of naringenin interaction with GABRA5α and GSK-3β as hypothesis-generating results. Results Nar-NPs were spherical in shape (~ 95 nm), of monodisperse size (PDI &lt; 0.2) and negatively charged (ζ ≈ -28.5mV). The formulation showed high encapsulation efficiency (EE% = 89.2 ± 3.1%), demonstrated good colloidal stability in phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) over 24 h, and exhibited a sustained drug release profile as well with sustained release profile (Korsmeyer-Peppas, n ≈ 0.56). The batch-to-batch reproducibility of ζ was high (− 28.47, -28.71, − 28.52mV; mean = − 28.57 ± 0.13mV; CV% ≈ 0.44%). In vivo, Nar-NP attenuated scopolamine-induced deficits as evidenced by the amelioration of acquisition and probe trial performance, normalization in levels of antioxidant defenses, reduction in neuroinflammatory mediators, and reinforcement of hippocampal architecture. The combination with donepezil produced effects greater than either monotherapy alone, suggesting potential additive or synergistic interaction. Docking indicated reasonable binding to GABRA5α and GSK-3β, but this is exploratory and needs validation ex vivo/in vivo for the mechanism of action. Conclusions Nar-NPs are a predictable, stable, and bioavailable formulation with multidomain neuroprotective potential in a cholinergic impairment model. Since the reversible model we use mimics acute cholinergic dysfunction with associated oxidative and inflammatory sequelae rather than the progressive proteinopathies (amyloid-β accumulation, tau hyperphosphorylation) and chronic neurodegeneration seen in Alzheimer’s disease, the current findings ought to be interpreted as an example of symptom-relevant rather than disease-modifying neuroprotection. As such, the multi-domain therapeutic potential implied by our molecular and histopathological endpoints must be tested stringently in chronic, pathology-induced models (e.g., transgenic APP/PS1 or 3xTg-AD mice) for determining translational relevance to Alzheimer’s disease. Introduction The most important health concerns worldwide are neurodegenerative diseases 1 . Over 55 million people worldwide are presently affected by conditions like Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, Huntington’s disease, and other dementias; a new case is discovered every three seconds 2 . These discouraging data point to a pressing and worsening issue that calls for creative therapeutic strategies 3 . Simply, temporary relief is provided by current medications, which just alleviate symptoms without slowing the disease’s progression 4 . This drawback emphasizes the need for innovative approaches that address several disease processes while mitigating side effects 5 . Through a network of interrelated pathogenic pathways, neurodegenerative disorders significantly impair cellular function 6 . The reduction of GABAergic transmission, along with a significant decrease in GABRA5α receptor expression, eliminates essential inhibitory regulation of neuronal activity, resulting in detrimental excitotoxicity 7 . The electrical imbalance correlates with the heightened activation of the enzyme GSK-3β, which phosphorylates essential tau proteins into detrimental neurofibrillary tangles, a defining characteristic of Alzheimer’s disease progression 8 . Additionally, pro-inflammatory mediators in the brain, such as IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α, interfere with the ERK pathway, thereby hindering the processes of memory storage and modification 9 . Moreover, the accumulation of MDA due to oxidative stress, along with the depletion of GSH, SOD, and CAT levels, weakens the brain’s defenses and leads to neuronal damage 10 . The scopolamine-induced model is effective in reliably reproducing cholinergic impairment and cognitive abnormalities associated with Alzheimer’s disease. Scopolamine causes temporary but notable deficits in learning and memory by blocking muscarinic acetylcholine receptors 11 . It concurrently induces oxidative stress and neuroinflammation, which are two principal indicators of neurodegenerative disorders 12 . This experimental system provides a suitable platform for evaluating innovative medicines, enabling researchers to measure cognitive enhancements and disease-modifying effects, including decreased oxidative damage and inflammation 13 . Donepezil serves as the primary treatment for Alzheimer’s disease, exhibiting both advantages and disadvantages 14 . This acetylcholinesterase inhibitor temporarily increases acetylcholine concentrations in synaptic clefts 15 . This may enhance cognitive function in some circumstances. Clinical studies demonstrate that donepezil is effective in about 40–60% of patients, and those who do respond typically have benefits for 6–12 months until a deterioration in their condition ensues 16 . Bioactive compounds in medicinal plants affect disease processes. Naringenin, a flavonoid present in citrus fruits, has potential therapeutic effects on neurological disorders 17 . This molecule is crucial for maintaining cellular homeostasis. Antioxidants assist the body in combating free radicals and enhancing its strength 18 . Anti-inflammatory methods involve the inhibition of NF-κB signaling pathways and the production of cytokines. Naringenin selectively inhibits damage and regulates neuronal excitability via GABA receptors 19 . Naringenin exhibits significant therapeutic potential; however, its clinical application is limited by suboptimal pharmacokinetics, specifically low oral bioavailability of less than 5%, attributed to inadequate solubility and rapid metabolism 20 . Nanoparticle-mediated delivery constitutes a feasible approach. Naringenin can cross biological barriers when encapsulated in nanoparticles 21 . Particles with dimensions ranging from 80 to 120 nm and a zeta potential of −28.5 mV can evade immune clearance by utilizing natural transport mechanisms to cross the blood-brain barrier 22 , 23 . The nanoparticle matrix enables sustained release, improving therapeutic concentration and minimizing toxicity. Additionally, ligands may be incorporated to improve receptor-specific delivery 24 . This study hypothesized that the concurrent use of donepezil and Nar-NPs will produce synergistic therapeutic effects in models of neurodegenerative diseases by promoting immediate cognitive improvement via neuroprotection, antioxidant properties, anti-inflammatory actions, and disease-modifying mechanisms 25 , 26 . This combination allows for a reduction in the dosage of donepezil, thus decreasing side effects while maintaining or enhancing overall efficacy. The first key conceptual point to frame is the explanatory one: that the cholinergic hypofunction model induced by scopolamine administration, while useful for studying short-term cognitive impairments and its corollaries of oxidative/inflammatory cascades, does not model the ongoing proteinopathy-related process in Alzheimer’s disease (AD) most relevant here: amyloid-β plaque accumulation and hyperphosphorylated tau neurofibrillary tangle formation. Therefore, the ‘neuroprotective’ effects seen in this model relate largely to an abrogation of acute neurotransmission failure and its downstream consequences, but not an inhibition or slowing of ongoing chronic neurodegenerative processes. As such, the present investigation places Nar-NPs as a potential therapeutic for symptom-specific and acute injury-alleviating treatment with multi-domain molecular endpoints (GABRA5α, GSK-3β, pERK, oxidative/inflammation markers) serving as mechanistic proxies of functional plasticity within this limited biological template. Translation to modification of Alzheimer’s disease will need validation in chronic pathology-driven models. While the therapeutic potential of naringenin is evident, its poor pharmacokinetic profile presents a major translational hurdle. Furthermore, existing treatments like donepezil offer only symptomatic relief and do not effectively halt disease progression. Therefore, a compelling research gap exists for a therapeutic strategy that simultaneously enhances the delivery of a multi-target agent like naringenin and combines it with standard care for synergistic effects. We hypothesized that a dual-therapy approach, integrating nanoformulated naringenin (Nar-NPs) with a reduced dose of donepezil, would produce synergistic neuroprotective effects by concurrently enhancing cholinergic transmission and mitigating oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, and synaptic dysfunction via complementary mechanisms. The objective of this study is to develop and assess a novel dual-therapy strategy that integrates Nar-NPs with low-dose donepezil to target both the symptoms and the underlying pathology of neurodegeneration. While previous studies have explored naringenin nanoformulations for enhancing bioavailability, the novelty of the present work lies in the development of a dual-targeted, surfactant-stabilized nanoparticle system specifically optimized for neuroprotection. Unlike earlier formulations focused on multi-mechanistic therapeutic strategies, our Nar-NPs are designed to simultaneously modulate GABAergic signaling (via GABRA5α), inhibit GSK-3β, and synergize with low-dose donepezil. Furthermore, while scopolamine models are widely used, our study uniquely integrates behavioral, biochemical, molecular docking, and histopathological analyses to provide a multi-modal validation of neuroprotective efficacy, offering a comprehensive therapeutic strategy beyond symptomatic relief. Materials and methods Materials High-quality ingredients were obtained from prominent international suppliers: pharmaceutical-grade naringenin (≥ 95% purity, Sigma-Aldrich N5893, Germany) was encapsulated utilizing a meticulously calibrated surfactant combination including Span 80 (S6760) and Tween 80 (P1754) from the same provider. Scopolamine hydrobromide (SCO) (≥ 98% purity, S0929, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was administered to elicit cognitive impairment. Donepezil hydrochloride (≥ 99% purity, D6820, TCI Chemicals, Japan) was used as the reference treatment. Methods Synthesis of naringenin nanoparticles (Nar-NPs) Naringenin nanoparticles were synthesized by a solvent evaporation method. Naringenin (Sigma-Aldrich, ≥ 95% purity) was solubilized in ethanol (10 mg/mL) and combined with an aqueous phase comprising Span 80 and Tween 80 (1:1 ratio, 2% w/v total surfactant concentration) while being magnetically stirred at 1000 rpm for 30 min. The organic phase was further evaporated under reduced pressure (Rotavapor R-100, Büchi) at 40 °C, and the resultant suspension was subjected to probe sonication (Qsonica Q700, 20% amplitude, 5 min, pulse mode 5 s on/5 s off) to diminish particle size. The nanosuspension was subjected to centrifugation (15,000 rpm, 30 min, 4 °C) to exclude unencapsulated medication, rinsed twice with deionized water, and subsequently lyophilized (Labconco FreeZone) for further characterization. Characterization of Nar-NPs Morphological analysis (TEM) The morphology of Nar-NPs was analyzed utilizing transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEOL JEM-1400). A droplet of nanoparticle suspension (0.1 mg/mL) was applied to a carbon-coated copper grid, negatively stained with 2% phosphotungstic acid, and air-dried before imaging at 80 kV. The particle size distribution was assessed from at least 100 nanoparticles utilizing ImageJ software. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) and zeta potential The hydrodynamic diameter and polydispersity index (PDI) were measured using dynamic light scattering (DLS) using a Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS at 25 °C and a scattering angle of 173°. Zeta potential was assessed via electrophoretic light scattering in deionized water (pH 6.8) utilizing the same instrument. All measurements were performed in duplicate. Stability assessment The physical stability of Nar-NPs was evaluated by monitoring hydrodynamic size, polydispersity index (PDI), and zeta potential over 30 days at two storage conditions: 4 °C (refrigerated) and 30 °C (accelerated). Samples were stored in sealed vials and analyzed at predetermined intervals (0, 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, and 30 days) using Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS) for size and PDI, and Electrophoretic Light Scattering for zeta potential (Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS). Measurements were performed in triplicate, and results were expressed as mean ± SD. UV-visible spectroscopy The UV spectra of naringenin and Nar-NPs (10 µg/mL in methanol) were obtained from 200 to 400 nm using a Shimadzu UV-1800 spectrophotometer with quartz cuvettes of 1 cm path length. Baseline correction was conducted using methanol as the blank reference. Human recombinant GABRA5α (Catalog Number EH158RB) and GSK-3β (Catalog Number EH454RB) proteins were acquired from Thermo Fisher Scientific, located in Waltham, MA, USA. ELISA kits for GABRA5α (Catalog Number MBS2602019) and GSK-3β (Catalog Number MBS288518) were sourced from MyBioSource, located in San Diego, California, USA. Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy FTIR spectra (PerkinElmer Spectrum Two) were obtained in the 4000–500 cm⁻¹ range (resolution: 4 cm⁻¹) for crude naringenin, surfactants (Span 80, Tween 80), physical mixes, and Nar-NPs via the KBr pellet method. Data were examined using Spectrum™ software. Encapsulation efficiency and loading capacity measurement The EE% and DL% of Nar-NPs were measured by an indirect method. Upon synthesis, after centrifuging (15,000 rpm, 30 min, 4 °C) to pellet the nanoparticles and measuring UV-Vis absorbance at λ max = 290 nm of the supernatant for free naringenin, we quantified the amount of unencapsulated (free) naringenin. The concentration in the pure naringenin standard curve was determined. Stability in simulated physiological conditions The colloidal stability of Nar-NPs at pHs physiologically relevant in the short term was evaluated by dispersing nanoparticles into phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) at a concentration of 1 mg/mL. The suspension was then gently agitated at 100 rpm and 37 °C. Samples were taken at 0, 2, 6, 12, and 24 h, and the hydrodynamic diameter and polydispersity index (PDI) were immediately analysed by DLS (Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS) to check for any aggregation or change in size. Reproducibility of formulation: zeta potential between different batches For fabrication reproducibility testing, Nar-NPs were produced in triplicate (A–C) on different days according to the same solvent-evaporation condition (organic: aqueous ratio, Span80:Tween80 1:1; total surfactant 2% w/v; sonication amplitude/time; evaporation temperature). For each batch, zeta potential (ζ) was determined in deionized water (pH 6.8) at 25 °C by electrophoretic light scattering (Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS-173° backscatter). Assay was done in triplicate for each batch, and data are shown as mean ± SD. CV% was calculated as the ratio of SD/mean × 100 for all three-batch means. Each batch was measured in triplicate; one-way ANOVA indicated no significant differences among batch means ( p &gt; 0.05), supporting inter-batch consistency. Experimental animals The study protocol obtained ethical approval (No. 20250314) from the Faculty of Applied Health Sciences Technology Ethical Review Committee on October 6th University, Egypt. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations of this committee and followed the National Research Council’s Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Additionally, this study is reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0. A total of 96 adult albino mice (Mus musculus) were sourced from the National Cancer Institute in Cairo, Egypt. The subjects were categorized into two groups: 60 mice for the determination of the median lethal dose (LD 50 ) and 36 mice for evaluating the hypolipidemic and antioxidant effects of naringenin nanoparticles (Nar-NPs). Before experimentation, all mice were subjected to a 7-day acclimatization period in standard laboratory conditions, with unrestricted access to commercial rodent chow and water. Acute oral toxicity and LD₅₀ determination of Nar-NPs in swiss albino mice: a dose-response study following OECD guidelines The acute oral toxicity of naringenin nanoparticles (Nar-NPs) was systematically assessed in adult Swiss albino mice weighing 25–30 g ( n = 10 per dose group) in accordance with OECD Guideline 423 for acute oral toxicity testing 28 . A geometric dose progression (100, 200, 600, 800, 1200, and 1400 mg/kg body weight) was administered orally via gavage using ball-tipped stainless steel feeding needles. All doses were prepared as fresh suspensions in a 0.5% carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC) vehicle and standardized to a 10 mL/kg administration volume. Animals were fasted for 12 h with unrestricted access to water before dosing to standardize gastrointestinal conditions. Mice were monitored continuously for the first 4 h post-administration to assess immediate toxic responses, followed by twice-daily observations over 14 days. These observations included detailed clinical examinations for signs of toxicity (such as lethargy, tremors, and convulsions), mortality recording, and body weight measurements. Experimental design Main experimental phase: Thirty-six adult male albino mice (average weight 32 ± 3 g) were employed in the study. The number of animals required for investigation ( n = 6 per group) was calculated a priori with the G*Power software, version 3.1.9.7 based on effect sizes (f ≥ 0.8) obtained from our pilot study to achieve &gt; 80% power of detection (α = 0.05) differences in primary outcomes measurements including escape latencies in Morris Water Maze and levels of oxidative stress markers (e.g., MDA). Dose Selection and Justification: Nar-NPs therapeutic dose (37.75 mg/kg) was designed based on a two-tier rational justification. First, the value is 1/20 of the experimental LD₅₀ (755 mg/kg), resulting in &gt; 18-fold safety margin according to OECD guidelines for preclinical toxicology. Secondly, this dose is within the effective range of naringenin (25–50 mg/kg) reported in previous neuropharmacological studies, which demonstrated cognitive and oxidative measures for rodent models of neurodegeneration 20 , 29 . The scopolamine dose (3, i.p.) is widely known for causing a consistent cholinergic dysfunction and learning and memory impairment in mice, as observed previously with other Alzheimer’s disease models 30 , 31 . The dose of donepezil (10 mg/kg, p.o.) selected was in the range expected to be less than maximally effective and regularly used for studies in mice for the detection of potential synergistic effects with Nar-NPs but without ceiling effects 32 , 33 . Animals were randomly divided into six experimental groups by a computer-generated random sequence of numbers (Table 1 ). During the experimental period, mice were housed in individual standard polypropylene cages with controlled conditions (22 ± 2 °C and 60% relative humidity) in a 12-hour light/dark cycle (08:00–20:00), fed ad libitum a standard rodent diet (Dyets Inc., Bethlehem, PA, USA) and tap water. No animals reached these endpoints before the scheduled conclusion of the study. Morris water maze (MWM) test The MWM test was performed to evaluate spatial learning and memory 35 . The apparatus consisted of a circular pool (120 cm diameter, 50 cm deep) filled with opaque water (22 ± 1 °C) and a hidden platform (10 cm diameter, submerged 1 cm below the surface). Mice underwent a 5-day acquisition phase with four daily trials (60 s/trial, 30 min inter-trial interval), during which escape latency (time to locate the platform) was recorded. On day 6, a probe trial was performed by removing the platform and allowing mice to swim freely for 60 s. Memory retention was quantified by measuring the time spent in the target quadrant and the number of platform crossings. Plasma lipid profile estimation On day 15, blood samples were obtained from the retro-orbital plexus and collected in heparinized tubes to inhibit coagulation. The samples were promptly placed on ice and centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C to isolate plasma. The plasma was aliquoted and stored at −80 °C for subsequent biochemical analysis. Plasma lipid parameters, specifically total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) were measured utilizing commercially available enzymatic-colorimetric kits from Asan and Youngdong Pharmaceutical Co., Korea 36 – 38 . Absorbance was measured at specific wavelengths utilizing a UV-Vis spectrophotometer. Brain tissue collection and preparation Mice were anesthetized with sodium thiopental (75 mg/kg, intraperitoneally) before cervical dislocation to reduce distress and ensure humane euthanasia. Post-euthanasia, brains were swiftly removed, immersed in ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, 0.1 M, pH 7.4), and dissected. The meninges and superficial blood vessels were meticulously excised with fine-tipped forceps to guarantee contamination-free tissue samples. The brain tissues were processed, weighed, and partitioned into aliquots for designated biochemical analyses. Brain samples were homogenized at a concentration of 25% w/v in ice-cold saline utilizing a glass homogenizer (Universal Lab. Aid MPW-309, Poland). The homogenization process occurred under refrigerated conditions to maintain enzymatic activity and protein integrity. Brain homogenate preparation Brain tissues were homogenized in a 10:1 (w/v) ratio with ice-cold homogenization buffer consisting of 50 mM Tris-HCl at pH 7.4, supplemented with 1 mM EDTA and 0.1% Triton X-100. Homogenization was conducted with a motor-driven Teflon-glass homogenizer at 1200 rpm for 10 strokes on ice to reduce protein degradation. The crude homogenate was divided into three aliquots for various biochemical assays. Glutathione (GSH) quantification The first aliquot underwent centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C to obtain the supernatant. Levels of reduced glutathione (GSH) were quantified utilizing Ellman’s DTNB (5,5’-dithiobis-2-nitrobenzoic acid) method 39 . The reaction mixture comprised 100 µL of deproteinized supernatant combined with 900 µL of DTNB reagent (0.6 mM in 0.1 M phosphate buffer, pH 8.0). The sample underwent incubation at 25 °C for a duration of 10 min in the absence of light, followed by absorbance measurement at 412 nm. Oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation assays Lipid peroxidation was measured with the TBARS method (Cayman Chemical, Item No. 10009055) 40 . Absorbance was read at 532 nm, and MDA concentration was obtained from a standard curve (0–50 µM). Values were normalized with respect to total protein content as determined by the Bradford assay. Antioxidant enzyme assays The brain antioxidant enzyme activities were determined in the same groups ( n = 6 mice/group) by approved commercial kits. Brain homogenates (10% w/v in PBS, pH 7.4) of each animal were prepared and centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C and superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity was detected according to the kit instructions of SOD Assay Kit (Cat No:19160-1KT-F; Sigma-Aldrich, USA), in which pyrogallol autoxidation inhibition was monitored at 420 nm on a Shimadzu UV-1800 spectrophotometer (Japan). CAT activity was examined by Catalase Activity Assay Kit (Cat: ab83464, Abcam Ltd., UK), measuring the decomposition of H 2 O 2 at 240 nm. Experiments were performed in triplicate reactions for each of the six different biological replicates per group, and enzyme activity was normalized to total protein (Bradford) and expressed as U/mg protein (for SOD), or µmol H 2 O 2 decomposed/min/mg protein (CAT). Inflammatory marker quantification Neuroinflammatory markers in brain tissue, specifically TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β, and nitrites, were quantified using commercial ELISA kits obtained from Immuno-Biological Laboratories (IBL, USA; TNF-α Cat# 27194, IL-6 Cat# 27718, IL-1β Cat# 27298, Nitrite/Nitrate Cat# 27430). Brain homogenates (10% w/v in PBS) were subjected to centrifugation at 12,000×g for 20 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were collected for analysis in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocols. Samples were analyzed in duplicate on pre-coated plates, incubated with specific antibodies, and developed using TMB substrate. Absorbance was quantified at 450 nm utilizing a BioTek Synergy H1 microplate reader. Sample concentrations were derived from standard curves (R² &gt; 0.99) and normalized to total protein content assessed via Bradford assay. The intra-assay coefficient of variation remained under 8% for all markers. Molecular docking methodology The binding interactions of naringenin with GABRA5α (PDB ID: 8BHG) and GSK-3β (PDB IDs: 5K5N, 8AUZ) were assessed using AutoDock Vina v1.2.3 (The Scripps Research Institute, USA). Crystal structures were obtained from the Protein Data Bank (PDB) and subsequently preprocessed to eliminate water molecules, co-crystallized ligands, and heteroatoms. Polar hydrogens and Gasteiger charges were incorporated using AutoDock Tools v1.5.6 to enhance hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions. The 3D structure of naringenin (PubChem CID: 439246) underwent energy minimization using the MMFF94 force field in Avogadro 1.2.0 and was subsequently converted to PDBQT format for docking purposes. Binding sites were predicted utilizing P2Rank, a deep learning algorithm, with grid boxes measuring 50 × 50 × 50 Å³ centered on the anticipated active residues. GABRA5α: x = 22.92, y = 13.99, z = 294.78 (near the benzodiazepine-binding site). GSK-3β: x = 2.71, y = 7.29, z = 23.70 (5K5N, ATP-binding pocket) and x = 22.86, y = − 0.98, z = 15.54 (8AUZ, allosteric site). Docking simulations were conducted using an exhaustiveness parameter set to 8, resulting in the generation of 10 conformational poses for each target. The selection of the lowest-energy poses was conducted based on binding affinity (ΔG) and subsequently validated through root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) analysis, with a threshold of less than 2.0 Å. Protein-ligand interactions were analyzed utilizing BIOVIA Discovery Studio 2019 and LigPlot+, emphasizing hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic contacts, and steric clashes. The proteins were pre-processed using a water exclusion, polar hydrogens addition and assignment of Gasteiger charges. Active sites were predicted and grid boxes of 50 × 50 × 50 Å³ were generated around each one. Validation was carried out by re-docking co-crystallized ligands with RMSD &lt; 2.0 Å. Exhaustiveness value of the model was set at 8, and the best docked pose was chosen on the basis of binding affinity (ΔG) and interaction analysis from Discovery Studio 2019. Such a separation is vital given that augmented effectiveness could result from various actions: (i) pharmacodynamically complementarity with donepezil increasing cholinergic transmission and Nar-NP attenuating oxidative/inflammatory damage and regulating GABAergic/GSK-3β signaling, (ii) shared downstream effects—wherein both agents meet in some antioxidant or anti-inflammatory pathway to give additive control of neurotoxicity, or (iii) pharmacokinetic permissiveness—with nanoparticle delivery modifying naringenin bioavailability or distribution that may incidentally impact donepezil clearance. Conversely, the scopolamine group (G3) showed severe histopathological changes, including marked neuropil vacuolation, neuronal degeneration, microgliosis, necrosis, and architectural disruption, accompanied by disorganized pyramidal neurons, pyknotic nuclei, and dilated blood vessels. Treatment with Nar-NPs (G4) or donepezil (G5) markedly ameliorated these changes, showing only mild vacuolation and minimal neuronal degeneration (+), with improved structural integrity of hippocampal layers. The combined therapy group (G6) demonstrated the greatest neuroprotection, with nearly normal hippocampal architecture and absence of microgliosis or necrosis, correlating with minimal histological scores. These findings confirm that Nar-NPs, particularly in combination with donepezil, significantly mitigated scopolamine-induced neurodegeneration and preserved hippocampal cytoarchitecture. Neuropil vacuolation, neuronal degeneration, microgliosis, necrosis, and architectural disarray were scored semi-quantitatively (0–4). The scopolamine group was 3.2 ± 0.3, while that of Nar-NPs + donepezil group was found to be 0.8 ± 0.2, revealing nearly ~ 85% recovery. The number of neurons in the CA1-region of the hippocampus supported a significantly enhanced neuron viability in treatment groups ( p &lt; 0.001). Molecular docking reveals naringenin’s dual neuroprotective mechanism GABRA5α interactions and therapeutic implications Our molecular docking research reveals naringenin’s significant binding affinity (ΔG = −6.59 kcal/mol) with GABRA5α, an essential component of the GABA-A receptor (Table 6 ; Fig. 16 ). The molecule forms interactions in the orthosteric binding site, establishing hydrogen bonds with polar residues TYR157 and THR160, engaging in π-π stacking with PHE200, and exhibiting van der Waals interactions with LEU204. This complex binding pattern indicates that naringenin may efficiently regulate GABAergic neurotransmission by stabilizing the receptor’s active conformation. The atomic-level depiction of these interactions offers substantial evidence for naringenin’s capacity to modulate neuronal excitability and safeguard against excitotoxicity, presenting a structural foundation for its neuroprotective effects via GABA receptor regulation. GSK-3β binding and kinase inhibition potential Naringenin demonstrates enhanced binding affinity (ΔG = −6.90 kcal/mol) with GSK-3β, effectively occupying the ATP-binding pocket of this essential kinase (Table 6 ; Fig. 17 ). The complex engages in hydrogen bonding with VAL135 and electrostatic interactions with ASP133, while forming π-π stacking with TYR134 and a salt bridge with LYS85. This thorough analysis of both catalytic and regulatory residues indicates that naringenin competes effectively with ATP, perhaps reducing the activity of GSK-3β. Considering GSK-3β’s pivotal role in tau pathology and neuroinflammation, these findings identify naringenin as a potential option for targeting several pathogenic pathways in neurodegenerative disorders via kinase inhibition. The residue-specific interactions predicted by our docking investigations provide a structural hypothesis for naringenin’s potential affinity for both targets. The compound’s capacity to interact with essential amino acids via many interaction modalities (hydrogen bonds, π-π stacking, and electrostatic interactions) underscores its promise as a multi-target neuroprotective agent. These atomic-level insights corroborate naringenin’s therapeutic potential and provide useful information for future structure-based optimization of naringenin derivatives and formulation strategies to boost its bioavailability and efficacy as a neuroprotective medication. Discussion Neurodegenerative disorders present a formidable global health challenge, with current therapies largely offering symptomatic relief without halting disease progression. This study sought to address two critical limitations in the field: the transient efficacy of drugs like donepezil and the poor bioavailability of promising natural compounds such as naringenin. We hypothesized that a dual-therapy approach using nanoformulated naringenin (Nar-NPs) with a reduced dose of donepezil would synergistically target both the symptoms and underlying pathology of neurodegeneration. Our comprehensive characterization confirmed the successful synthesis of Nar-NPs. The spherical morphology and uniform size (~ 95 nm) are conducive to cellular uptake and tissue accumulation via the EPR effect 42 , 43 . The high negative zeta potential (−28.5 mV) and unimodal DLS profile indicated excellent colloidal stability, minimizing aggregation and potential clearance issues 44 , 45 . Spectral analyses (UV-Vis and FTIR) confirmed successful nanoencapsulation, showing an altered electronic environment for naringenin and physical encapsulation without chemical degradation, thereby preserving its bioactivity 46 , 47 . These stability features of Nar-NPs further support their translational potential. The formulation showed good colloidal stability for 1 month of storage at 4 °C with no significant alterations of hydrodynamic diameter, PDI, and zeta potential. This refrigerated shelf-stability is critical to long‐term storage and batch‐to‐batch consistency for pharmaceutical purposes. The increase of the particle size and PDI at 30 °C, although slight, may imply that higher temperature may induce a weak aggregation of particles over time, which is likely to occur in lipid-based nanosystems. Crucially, zeta potential (− 28.5 to − 28.7 mV) remained strongly negative in both conditions, which means electrostatic matching was kept strong, preventing large-scale aggregation or sedimentation. Together, these results confirm that Nar-NPs are physicochemically stable and feasible for additional preclinical development, with the recommendation of refrigerated storage to maintain shelf-life stability. Moreover, the formulation had a high encapsulation efficiency (~ 89%), making efficient use of the active available, in addition to presenting an acceptable short-term stability profile in physiological buffer, essential for predictable in vivo performance. The low inter-batch variability of ζ (− 28.47 to − 28.71 mV; CV% ≈ 0.44%) indicates that the solvent-evaporation method results in nanoparticle batches with similar surface charge, an important factor influencing colloidal stability, interaction with mucus, and biological identity in serum. Along with the small PDI and uniform hydrodynamic size, these data demonstrate a stable, realizable production process and minimize the potential variation of in vivo performance by batch. This reproducibility is especially important for scale-up and translational development, as narrow ζ control ensures predictable protein corona generation and dispersion in physiological conditions. The in-vitro release study indicated there were marked differences in the release rate and mechanism between Nar-NPs and naringenin alone. The relative cumulative release of Nar-NPs showed that 56% was reached in 24 h, whereas free naringenin reached almost up to 69% over the same time frame. This slower and controlled release from Nar-NPs is valuable for the sustenance of therapeutic concentrations over prolonged durations, decreased dosing frequency, and circumvention of peak-related cytotoxicity. Release mechanisms were explained through kinetic modelling. The Korsmeyer–Peppas (KP) model provided the best fit for Nar-NPs with R²=0.93 using a release exponent (n) of 0.556, confirming non-Fickian transport/relaxation as the drug release mechanism indicating that drug diffusion and polymer relaxation control the release process. The excellent Higuchi correlation (R²=0.97) also indicates the importance of diffusion as one of the main mechanisms contributing to the release rate. Free naringenin, instead, exhibited a KP exponent ( n = 0.678) still corresponding to the anomalous region but with a higher release in the first hours, where dissolution and diffusion were minor due to low solubility properties of naringenin in the absence of structured vehicle systems. These results are consistent with previous data on flavonoid-loaded nanoparticles showing that the encapsulation increases solubility and modulates release kinetics. The sustained-release profile of Nar-NPs in intestinal pH conditions indicates its potential as an oral agent for protection in chronic diseases to maintain levels of drugs over therapeutic concentrations. Furthermore, the abnormal transport mechanism implied that formulation parameters (e.g., surfactant ratio and particle size) could be further optimized to fine-tune the release. Taken together, the kinetic study substantiates a controlled release by Nar-NPs and that it is diffusion-relaxation driven, which has implications in treating chronic diseases such as neurodegeneration, where constant drug availability is required rather than instantaneous bursts. In vitro assays demonstrated the multi-target potential of Nar-NPs. The high-affinity inhibition of GABRA5α (IC₅₀ = 20.3 µM) and GSK-3β (IC₅₀ = 15.1 µM) suggests a dual mechanism of action. The sigmoidal kinetics imply potential allosteric modulation of GABRA5α, which could enhance inhibitory neurotransmission and protect against excitotoxicity 48 . The potent inhibition of GSK-3β positions Nar-NPs as a candidate to mitigate tau hyperphosphorylation and neuroinflammation, key pathways in neurodegeneration 49 , 50 . The favorable LD₅₀ (755 mg/kg) established a wide safety margin for the selected therapeutic dose (37.75 mg/kg), which was also aligned with efficacious doses reported in prior literature 8 . The core of our findings lies in the robust in vivo neuroprotection offered by Nar-NPs. In the scopolamine model, Nar-NPs significantly mitigated cognitive deficits, as evidenced by improved performance in the Morris Water Maze. This cognitive restoration is likely underpinned by the observed reversal of scopolamine-induced pathophysiology. Nar-NPs treatment markedly attenuated oxidative stress, as shown by the significant restoration of GSH, SOD, and CAT levels and the reduction in lipid peroxidation (MDA). This potent antioxidant effect likely contributes to the preservation of neuronal integrity 51 , 52 . Furthermore, Nar-NPs exhibited superior anti-inflammatory efficacy compared to donepezil monotherapy, significantly reducing pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, TNF-α, IL-1β) and nitrosative stress. This suggests a capacity to suppress neuroinflammatory pathways, potentially through downregulation of NF-κB and iNOS 53 , 54 . The formulation also counteracted scopolamine-induced lipid dysregulation, normalizing cholesterol and triglyceride profiles while boosting HDL-C, effects that may support cerebrovascular health 55 , 56 . The more potent impact of Nar-NPs co-administered with donepezil compels us to examine the types of interactions between them and underpinning mechanism for higher efficacy achieved. Donepezil can induce acutely enhanced cholinergic neurotransmission through acetylcholinesterase inhibition, which can be associated with rapid symptomatic benefits. Meanwhile, Nar-NPs attenuate oxidative stress and suppress neuroinflammation while modulating GABRA5α/GSK-3β/pERK signaling, which are relevant to neuronal resilience and functional recovery. Such separate, yet converging pathways might result in additively acting neuroprotection without molecular interaction. The drug can both inhibit common oxidative stress and inflammatory mediators (e.g., MDA, IL-6, TNF-α) in the brain independently, towards achieving additive repression of neurotoxicity without pathway-specific synergy. Delivery of naringenin via nanoparticles might modify absorption, distribution, or metabolism; one could speculate on changes in donepezil disposition or bioavailability, a hypothesis that would deserve proper pharmacokinetic testing. The exploratory molecular docking to GABRA5α and GSK-3β, although hypothesis-generating, is mechanistically separate from the identified behavioral rescue. To incorporate such in silico predictions in a comprehensive carrier design framework, one needs: Direct engagement of target validation (competitive binding assays, cellular thermal shift assays, or kinase activity) for confirmation that naringenin effectively engages these targets at therapeutically achievable concentrations in vivo. GABRA5α upregulation, GSK-3β downregulation, and pERK restoration precede and predict cognitive recovery and their blockade would prevent the therapeutic effects. Formal synergy analysis: isobologram analysis or response-surface modelling at several dose ratios to determine if and where interactions are additive versus synergistic. Determine if the acute pathway alterations lead to long-term neuroprotection, decreased proteinopathy, and retention of cognitive function in transgenic AD mice. Pending mechanistic and quantitative analyses, the improvement in antimalarial efficacy by combination would be better described as potentially additive/complementary rather than synergistic, and the molecular docking predictions are preliminary hypotheses that need confirmation. At the molecular level, Nar-NPs treatment led to a marked restoration of key signaling pathways, upregulating GABRA5α, downregulating GSK-3β, and enhancing pERK expression. These changes point to a recovery of inhibitory neurotransmission, a reduction in pro-degenerative kinase activity, and a promotion of pro-survival synaptic plasticity 57 , 58 . It is important to note that these mechanisms, while strongly supported by our gene expression and docking data, are correlative and warrant further investigation to establish direct causal links in vivo. The histopathological evaluation provided compelling evidence of the neuroprotective potential of Nar-NPs. Scopolamine administration induced pronounced structural disruptions, including neuropil vacuolation, neuronal degeneration, microgliosis, and architectural disorganization within the hippocampus. These alterations were markedly attenuated following treatment with Nar-NPs, either alone or in combination with donepezil. Notably, the combined therapy group exhibited hippocampal architecture that closely resembled that of the normal control, with minimal vacuolation and preserved pyramidal and granule cell layers. This structural integrity strongly correlates with the functional improvements observed in behavioral performance and the restoration of oxidative and inflammatory markers, underscoring the multifaceted protective role of Nar-NPs against cholinergic dysfunction and neurodegeneration. Computational studies of molecular docking also offer a structural basis for the observed effects of naringenin, while predicting high-affinity binding to both GABRA5α and GSK-3β. These in silico predictions indicate that naringenin might directly act on these targets, restoring GABAergic signaling and suppressing kinase activity 59 – 61 . Nevertheless, these predictions on target engagement need to be confirmed by experiment in the future. The mechanistic theme that arises from these in silico predictions (GABAergic modulation → GSK-3β inhibition → downstream pERK reactivation) should be considered within the framework of the scopolamine model. Although this concept offers a tenable mechanism by which functional and molecular recovery is seen in response to an acute cholinergic challenge, it does not serve as proof that the chronic modulation of these same systems would act to arrest the progressive proteinopathic cascade of AD. The current study provides correlational evidence linking pathway modulation and cognitive rescue in an acute setting; determination of causality as well as disease-modifying potential will require (i) direct target engagement studies (such as binding assays, kinase activity assays) (ii) chronic intervention in transgenic models, and (iii) temporal analysis of pathway activation relative to proteinopathy progression. Naringenin’s high-affinity for GABRA5α (ΔG = −6.59 kcal/mol) and GSK-3β (ΔG = −6.90 kcal/mol) is consistent with the neuroprotection observed from a structural perspective. Interaction with GABRA5α probably contributes to increased GABAergic inhibition and decreased excitotoxicity, while the GSK-3β inhibition may minimize tau hyperphosphorylation and neuroinflammation. These in silico predictions are in agreement with our biochemical evidence of recovery of GABRA5α expression, down-regulation of GSK-3β activity, and up-regulation of oxidative and inflammatory markers. Across all parameters, the combination of Nar-NPs with a low dose of donepezil consistently produced the most pronounced therapeutic outcomes, often leading to a near-complete reversal of scopolamine-induced deficits. This synergy suggests that Nar-NPs target the underlying pathology (oxidative stress, inflammation), while donepezil provides immediate symptomatic relief by boosting cholinergic tone, together creating a comprehensive therapeutic strategy. Model limitations and translational considerations The scopolamine-induced model, despite being an established and useful tool for studying cholinergic dysfunction and acute cognitive impairment, possesses inherent limitations in reflecting the complex, progressive pathology of Alzheimer’s disease (AD). Scopolamine induces temporary cholinergic hypofunction, oxidative stress, and neuroinflammation—phenomena that overlap with early pathological events in AD but do not recapitulate the chronic proteinopathic hallmarks (amyloid-β plaque accumulation, neurofibrillary tangle formation, progressive synaptic loss) that define AD as a neurodegenerative disease. Consequently, the ‘neuroprotection’ demonstrated herein pertains to acute insult mitigation and functional recovery, not to disease modification in the sense of arresting or reversing chronic neurodegenerative cascades. This distinction is critical for framing the translational implications of our findings. The multidomain molecular endpoints assessed (GABRA5α, GSK-3β, pERK, oxidative/inflammatory markers) serve as mechanistic correlates of functional rescue within this acute paradigm but cannot be extrapolated to predict long-term disease-modifying efficacy. The robust effects observed across behavioural, biochemical, and histopathological domains establish Nar-NPs as a promising candidate for symptom-targeted therapy and for mitigating acute cholinergic crises, but the leap to Alzheimer’s disease modification requires confirmation in chronic, pathology-driven models (e.g., APP/PS1, 3xTg-AD, or tauopathy models) with longitudinal assessment of proteinopathy progression, neuronal survival, and cognitive trajectories. Until such validation is obtained, claims of disease-modifying potential remain speculative and should be tempered accordingly. The above molecular docking findings, while computationally based, are predictive and would need in-vitro testing involving direct binding assays, kinase activity analysis, or structural studies to establish the interaction of naringenin with both GABRA5α and GSK-3β. Moreover, although Nar-NPs in combination with donepezil exhibited better effects than either compound alone, there was not quantification through synergy evaluation test, such as isobolographic and dose–response matrix analysis. Indeed, the observed enhancement can be characterized as additive (or even possibly synergistic), ideal for further pharmacological interaction studies. To move the translational characteristics of Nar-NPs forward, the next studies should aim at: Chronic/transgenic (e.g., APP/PS1, 3xTg-AD) models for the study of Aβ and tau pathology, synaptic loss, and long-term cognitive trajectories. Formal synergy analysis across a wide dose ratios range and by validated mathematical models (e.g., Chou–Talalay, Bliss independence). Safety therapeutic effects of nanoparticles with long-term biodistribution studies to evaluate organ toxicity and immune responses. Confirmatory testing for target engagement using phospho-specific protein assays, immunohistochemistry, and receptor binding assays. Taken together, our study offers a proof-of-concept for the use of Nar-NPs as a potential neuroprotective strategy in an acute injury model. Although disease modification has been forwarded by some reports, clinical relevancy need to be validated in more etiological contexts and using longer treatment times. Conclusion We have successfully formulated naringenin as a stable nanosystem which can efficiently overcome its key pharmacokinetic challenge. Nar-NPs proved to be a powerful, multi-targeted neuroprotectant in an animal model of acute scopolamine-induced cholinergic malfunction by markedly improving cognition, minimizing oxidative stress and neuroinflammation, as well as histopathological insults. Additive/complementary effects of low-dose donepezil with Nar-NPs demonstrate the feasibility of Nar-NPs as a supplementary treatment for symptom-targeted intervention attitude toward diseases associated with acute cholinergic derangement. Importantly, the mechanistic framework proposed from our molecular docking and gene expression assessments, suggesting GABRA5α modulation, inhibition of GSK-3β, and restoration of pERK, is a plausible correlative explanation for functional restitution but remains hypothesis-generating until direct target engagement is tested. The multi-domain therapeutic potential inferred from our surrogates should be viewed within the limitations of the scopolamine model as not replicating synaptic failure, progressive neurodegeneration, and proteinopathic features typical of AD. Future studies should emphasize: (i) formal synergy calculations at different dose ratios; (ii) direct target engagement verification (binding assays, kinases enzymatic activities); (iii) chronic efficacy in pathology-driven, transgenic models of disease presenting with progressive proteinopathy, and evaluation overtime of proteinopathy, neuronal survival and cognitive performance; (iv) interaction PK study to rule out formulation-driven changes in the drug disposition. This comprehensive mechanistic and translational validation is the only way forward for Nar-NPs to progress beyond a promising acute neuroprotective approach to a disease-modifying therapy for chronic neurodegenerative disorders. Significance of the study This research emphasizes the potential of Nar-NPs as an innovative neuroprotective treatment, showing enhanced effectiveness in reducing scopolamine-induced neurotoxicity, a commonly utilized model for neurodegeneration. Nar-NPs offer a multifaceted strategy for neuronal preservation through the modulation of lipid metabolism, suppression of oxidative stress, and reversal of neuroinflammation. Their capacity to restore cholinergic function, modulate GABRA5α signaling, and inhibit GSK-3β activity identifies them as a potential intervention for cognitive decline and neurodegenerative disorders. Histopathological evidence confirms the structural preservation effects, significantly reducing neuronal degeneration, vacuolation, and microgliosis to near-normal levels. The observed synergistic potential of donepezil presents a promising opportunity for combination therapy, enhancing neuroprotection. The findings position Nar-NPs as a strong candidate for clinical translation, facilitating the development of next-generation strategies for Alzheimer’s disease and associated neurological disorders.
