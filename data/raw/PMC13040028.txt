The role of the oxytocinergic system in oral microbiome composition in children with autism: evidence from a randomized controlled trial of intranasal oxytocin

Atypical oxytocinergic functioning and altered microbiome compositions have both been implicated in autism, with growing evidence of interactions between these systems. However, how exogenous oxytocin influences the oral microbiome remains largely unexplored. This exploratory study examined for the first time how oral microbiome alterations link to oxytocinergic signalling in school-aged autistic ( n = 80) and non-autistic children ( n = 40). Additionally, we investigated the effect of four-weeks of intranasal oxytocin administration in autistic children on oral microbiome compositions immediately post-treatment (T1) and at four-weeks follow-up (T2). At baseline, lower endogenous salivary oxytocin levels were linked to greater microbial evenness and diversity, with twelve genera showing significant associations with oxytocin levels. In autistic children, four weeks of oxytocin administration was associated with significant increases in the abundances of Centipeda immediately post-treatment (T0-T1), alongside decreases in Moraxella (T0-T1), and subsequent reductions in Rothia observed at the four-week follow-up (T1-T2). Particularly, the genus Moraxella emerged as relevant, as lower baseline abundance was associated with higher endogenous oxytocin levels, and a stronger oxytocin-induced downregulation of its abundance correlated with greater increases in endogenous oxytocin levels, accompanied by hypomethylation of the oxytocin receptor gene. All results persisted after adjusting for nutrition and dental care. This exploratory study provides initial evidence for a role of the oxytocinergic system in shaping the oral microbiome in autistic children. These results may facilitate the integration of oral microbiome profiling into autism diagnostic criteria and stimulate future studies on the use of oxytocin as a therapeutic option targeting oral microbiome alterations.

Introduction Autism is a neurodevelopmental condition characterized by difficulties with social communication and interaction, combined with expressions of restricted and repetitive behaviors and interests. Aside from these core difficulties, autistic individuals often display a broad range of co-occurring conditions, including gastro-intestinal (GI) problems and poor oral health [ 1 ]. Compared to the general population, autistic individuals are known to display higher risks of periodontal problems and caries [ 2 ]. In line with these observations, previous studies have observed oral microbiome alterations in autistic individuals [ 2 – 8 ]. In particular, in our prior study, the bacterial genera Solobacterium , Stomatobaculum , Ruminococcaceae UCG.014, Tannerella and Campylobacter were shown to be significantly more abundant in autistic compared to non-autistic children (same cohorts as included in the present study), and that higher abundances in the former four bacteria were significantly related to more severe behavioral difficulties [ 9 ]. In addition to alterations in microbiome compositions, there is increasing evidence that also alterations in the oxytocinergic system may be linked to distinct difficulties that are experienced by autistic individuals [ 10 – 12 ]. The nonapeptide oxytocin constitutes an important neuromodulator and has been identified as a key mediator of affiliative and prosocial behaviors [ 13 – 15 ]. The functioning of the circulating oxytocin is dependent on the functioning of the oxytocin receptor [ 16 ], which itself is encoded by the Oxytocin Receptor gene ( OXTR ) [ 17 ]. DNA methylation (DNAm) is one of the most extensively studied epigenetic mechanisms involved in the regulation of gene transcription, such that increased DNAm frequency is generally associated with decreased gene transcription, and therefore less expression of the respective protein [ 18 ]. Initial preclinical studies have demonstrated links between aberrant oxytocinergic signalling and autism symptom expression in rodent models of autism [ 19 – 21 ]. However, investigating oxytocin concentrations in biological samples (mostly plasma and saliva) collected from humans with autism yielded a more mixed pattern of results [ 12 ]. But, most studies reported reduced endogenous salivary oxytocin levels in autistic children, as shown in previous meta-analysis [ 12 ] and replicated in the same cohort examined in the present study [ 22 ]. While evidence is sparse, a review on OXTR DNAm by Moerkerke et al. (2021) [ 11 ] pointed towards a pattern of hypermethylation in adults with autism [ 23 , 24 ], indicative of reduced OXTR expression. Contradictory, also patterns of hypomethylation were identified in autistic children [ 25 , 26 ], suggesting that autism-related differences in OXTR DNAm may be modulated by developmental stage. Given the growing evidence of the role of the endogenous oxytocinergic system in autism, intranasal administration of oxytocin has gained increasing attention as a potential therapeutic option to mitigate the social difficulties and stress-related characteristics of autism. To date, however, mixed patterns of results have emerged, with some studies reporting beneficial clinical-behavioral effects on social or repetitive behaviors, while other studies failed to show significant effects [ 27 – 31 ]. Prior work from our group, based on the same cohort of school-aged autistic children as included in this study, revealed robust evidence of oxytocin’s anxiolytic and autonomic stress-regulatory effects [ 32 , 33 ], despite the absence of significant behavioral improvements or measurable enhancements in social salience [ 34 , 35 ]. While still in its early stages, recent preclinical research has provided evidence of significant bidirectional interactions between the oxytocinergic system and gut microbiome [ 36 ]. For example, initial rodent research provided evidence indicating that the oxytocinergic system, including both endogenous [ 37 – 43 ] and administered oxytocin [ 43 , 44 ], might affect gut microbiome functioning. Also in humans, initial evidence suggested an association between reduced endogenous oxytocin levels and gut microbiome changes in autistic children [ 45 ]. Furthermore, a prior study by Kong et al. (2021) examined the impact of combination therapy using probiotics ( Lactiplantibacillus plantarum ) and oxytocin on the gut microbiome in 35 autistic individuals (aged 3-20 years), as well as the effect of oxytocin alone [ 46 ]. The findings indicated that, although the oxytocin-only group did not show significant improvements on the Clinical Global Impression scale compared to the combination group, notable alterations in gut microbiome compositions were observed in both groups [ 46 ], suggesting that oxytocin may modulate the gut microbiome independently of its clinical efficacy. Likewise, examinations of the impact of four-weeks of daily oxytocin administration on gut microbiome compositions showed a significant increase in the abundance of the genus Fusicatenibacter in school-aged autistic children (subset of the cohort tested in the current study) [ 47 ], which may reflect a role of oxytocin in balancing gut inflammation, considering prior evidence that the genus Fusicatenibacter exerts potential anti-inflammatory effects and was positively associated with the production of short-chain fatty acids [ 48 ]. To date, no study has investigated the effect of intranasal oxytocin administration on oral microbiome compositions. However, initial rodent research has reported exogenously administered oxytocin to prevent the onset of periodontitis [ 49 ] and reverse the upregulation of oxytocin receptor expression in gingival tissue induced by experimental periodontitis [ 50 ]. While preliminary, these initial findings suggest that oxytocin may modulate host-microbiome interactions in the oral cavity, potentially impacting microbiome compositions and promoting a more balanced oral microbiome. While the exact mechanisms by which the oxytocinergic system may impact the oral microbiota remain an active area of research, several non-mutually exclusive links have been proposed. One potential pathway involves oxytocin’s regulatory effect on the autonomic nervous system, which modulates salivary secretion, thereby altering the oral microbiome by creating a more favorable environment for specific oral bacteria [ 51 ]. Furthermore, oxytocin could affect the oral microbiome by creating a less inflammatory environment and stimulating anti-inflammatory bacteria [ 52 ]. Another suggested mechanistic route involves the reduction of cortisol levels by oxytocin [ 53 ], which may mitigate stress-induced changes in the microbiome, such as increased permeability and dysbiosis [ 54 ]. Oxytocin could also act indirectly through its effects on the gut microbiome, given the anatomic connection between the gut and oral cavity. The oral-gut-microbiome axis and the presence of oxytocin receptors in the enteric nervous system have been described earlier [ 55 ]. Here, it is anticipated that nasally administered oxytocin may primarily modulate these processes indirectly via entry in the systemic circulation, as its direct bioavailability in the GI tract is likely limited due to rapid degradation by peptidases. With regard to the oral-gut-microbiome axis, previous research has indicated that at least one in three oral bacteria will inhabit the gut in healthy adults [ 56 ]. Moreover, increased transmission of bacteria from the oral cavity to the gut have been described for different diseases [ 56 – 58 ]. The transmission of bacteria from the oral cavity to the gut may be facilitated by saliva. After all, saliva is composed of water, lipids and proteins, and contains mucus, which can shield bacteria from gastric acid, thereby enhancing their survival in the GI tract [ 58 ]. Besides the enteral route, bacteria are potentially also transferred through the hematogenous route. Oral mechanical injuries resulting from brushing the teeth or from orthodontic treatments/extractions can facilitate the dissemination of oral bacteria into the systemic circulation [ 59 ]. Furthermore, periodontitis can promote vascularization, facilitating the entry of periodontal pathogens into the bloodstream [ 60 ]. Lastly, oral bacteria can also migrate to the gut through their intracellular survival within immune cells. This mechanism facilitates the transmission of bacteria from the oral mucosa to the gut mucosa [ 60 ]. With the present study, we aim to provide an initial investigation into: (i) associations between oral microbiome composition and oxytocinergic signalling in autistic and non-autistic children aged 8–12 years; and (ii) the effect of a four-week course of twice-daily intranasal oxytocin administration on oral microbiome composition in autistic children. The primary outcome measure was the association between the oxytocinergic system and oral microbiome composition, specifically whether the abundance of oral bacteria was associated with endogenous oxytocin levels and/or altered following intranasal oxytocin administration. The secondary outcome measure was the association between changes in oral microbiome composition and changes in behavioral difficulties following intranasal oxytocin administration. As lifestyle variables such as dental care and nutrition can potentially impact oral microbiome composition and thereby influence the results [ 61 ], these confounders were taken into account in all analyses. Methods Participant characteristics and general study design A single-center, double-blind, randomized placebo-controlled study was performed at Leuven University Hospital (Belgium) to assess the effects of four-weeks of twice daily intranasal administration of oxytocin (12 IU in the morning and 12 IU in the afternoon) on oral microbiome compositions in school-aged autistic children. Participants, assisted by their parents, self-administered either oxytocin or a placebo nasal spray at a dose of 12 IU in the morning and 12 IU in the afternoon (total daily dose 24 IU), corresponding to three puffs of 2 IU per nostril per administration. This dosing regimen was consistent with a conservative protocol previously applied in young autistic children [ 62 ]. The four-week treatment duration was in line with prior trials conducted in both children [ 63 ] and adults [ 28 ] with autism. Outcome measures were assessed at baseline ( T0 ), within 24 h after the 4-week administration period ( T1 ), and at a follow-up session, 4 weeks after cessation of the daily administrations ( T2 ). Eighty children with a formal autism diagnosis, aged between 8-12 years with a 4/1 boys/girls’ ratio, were recruited through the Leuven Autism Expertise Centre between July 2019 and January 2021. Alongside, forty age- and sex-matched non-autistic peers were recruited. The autistic children were randomized to receive either oxytocin (Syntocinon®, Sigma-tau) or placebo (all ingredients used in the active solution except the oxytocin compound) nasal sprays using a 1:1 randomization scheme (see Table 1 for the number of participants analyzed and Fig. 1 for the flow diagram). All research staff conducting the trial, participants and their parents were blinded to treatment allocation, as reported before in [ 35 ]. During the course of the treatment, participants were screened for potential side effects. Overall, side effects were minimal and not treatment-specific (see [ 35 ]). To assess potential baseline diagnosis-related differences, 40 neurotypical control children underwent similar assessments at baseline ( T0 ), but did not proceed into the nasal spray administration regimen. Written informed consent from parents and assent from the children were obtained prior to study enrolment. Microbial sample collections were part of a larger clinical study, also including neurophysiological and endocrinological assessments [ 32 – 34 ]. The trial was registered with the European Clinical Trial Registry (EudraCT 2018-000769-35) on June 7th, 2018 ( https://www.clinicaltrialsregister.eu/ctr-search/trial/2018-000769-35/BE ). The clinical diagnosis of autism was established by a multidisciplinary team based on DSM-5 criteria (Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders) [ 64 ]. Non-autistic children were screened to exclude any neuropsychiatric condition. Shared main inclusion criteria comprised intelligence quotient above 70 (Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children [ 65 ]) and native Dutch speaker. Both boys and premenstrual girls were included in the study. Only premenstrual girls were eligible in order to avoid potential confounding effects of pubertal hormonal changes on oxytocin levels and microbiome composition. Main exclusion criteria comprised (a history of) any neurological disorder (stroke, concussion, epilepsy etc.), any significant physical disorder (liver, renal, cardiac pathology) and the use of antibiotics within the last three months. In addition, the Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule second edition was obtained from the autistic participants [ 66 ]. Parents were also asked to report any ongoing therapies or training programs and whether these were aimed at psychosocial stimulation. Based on their open-ended responses, parents indicated that their child participated in the following psychosocial trainings or therapies: theory of mind training, emotion recognition training, social skills training, cognitive behavioral therapy, psychotherapy, self-esteem training, mood regulation programs, music therapy, hippotherapy, and support from an autism coach. Sample size Tongue swabs for assessing oral microbiome composition were collected for the entire sample of 80 autistic and 40 non-autistic children. For the longitudinal analyses, microbiome samples were available for a subsample of 79 autistic children (40 placebo/39 oxytocin) (see Fig. 1 ). Here, a modified intention-to-treat approach was adopted, including all randomized participants who completed the baseline session (T0) and at least one post or follow-up session (T1 and/or T2) (data exclusion rule). Questionnaires assessing behavioral characteristics and dental and nutritional lifestyle variables Core autism characteristics were assessed using the parent-reported versions of the Social Responsiveness Scale, second edition (SRS-2) [ 67 , 68 ] and the Repetitive Behavior Scale-Revised (RBS-R) [ 69 ]. Anxiety was evaluated using both the child- and the parent-reported version of the Screen for Anxiety Related Emotional Disorders (SCARED) [ 70 ] (see Supplementary Methods and [ 35 ] for a detailed description of the adopted clinical-behavioral scales). The children’s parents were asked to fill in an extensive questionnaire, including metadata lifestyle variables previously shown to be associated with gut microbiome compositions [ 71 , 72 ]. In line with prior literature demonstrating the impact of nutrition and dental care on oral microbiome compositions [ 73 , 74 ], we here specifically assessed how these variables could impact oral microbiome compositions. The full list of assessed nutrition and dental care lifestyle variables is provided in Supplementary table S5 - 6 . Oral microbiome DNA extraction and Illumina sequencing Oral samples were collected using a standardized protocol as described earlier [ 9 ]. Isolated DNA was subsequently sent to BaseClear BV (Leiden, The Netherlands) for 16S rRNA gene (V3-V4 region) amplicon sequencing using the 341 F/785 R primers. The amplified DNA was sequenced with the Illumina MiSeq sequencing platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA) to generate 2 ×300 base-pair paired-end reads. Bioinformatics and statistical analyses DADA2 pipeline (version 1.16) was used for data cleaning and trimming of the raw microbial reads, merging of the forward and reverse reads and assigning taxonomy, using the Genome Taxonomy Database (GTDB, version 214.1) [ 75 ]. Prevalence and abundance filters of 5% and 0.01% (at Amplicon Sequence Variant (ASV) level) were applied. To mitigate the impact of extreme outliers, abundance values exceeding the 95th percentile were truncated at the 95th percentile within each group for each ASV. To allow log transformation in a table containing zeros, a pseudocount of 3 was chosen to fit the original data scale. Next, bacterial abundance tables were calculated and subjected to a centered log-transformation (CLR-transformation) (R, version 4.2.1). Microbial alpha-diversity indices were calculated using the Phyloseq package. Specifically in terms of microbial alpha-diversity, microbial richness (Chao1, amount of different kind of bacteria within a sample), evenness (Pielou, homogeneity in abundance of the different bacteria within a sample), observed richness, and diversity (Shannon and Simpson, taking into account both the microbiota richness and evenness) were assessed. Oxytocin levels and OXTR DNA methylation Oxytocin levels were assessed via saliva samples using Salivette cotton swabs (Sarstedt AG &amp; Co., Germany). To assess DNAm of OXTR (hg19, chr3:8,810,729-8,810,845), salivary samples were obtained via the Oragene DNA sample collection kit (DNA Genotek Inc., Canada) at the Leuven University hospital. More detailed information regarding the salivary assessment procedures, data handling and analyses are provided in [ 22 , 76 ]. Statistical analysis Associations at baseline Spearman correlation or MaAsLin2 analyses were adopted to examine whether variations in microbiome compositions (alpha-diversity and bacterial abundances) were related to behavioral characteristics and endogenous oxytocinergic signalling (endogenous oxytocin levels and OXTR DNAm) (R version 4.2.1). These analyses were conducted using T0 baseline data of autistic and non-autistic children. To assess whether identified associations were confounded by diagnosis, diagnosis was included as a covariate. All analyses were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg correction (BH-correction) [ 77 ], resulting in q values, i.e., the p -values after correction for multiple testing, with significance defined as q &lt; 0.25. Analysis of oxytocin administration effects Effects of oxytocin on microbiome compositions (alpha-diversity and bacterial abundances) were assessed using linear mixed-effects models (package lme4, version 1.1-35.1), with nasal spray (oxytocin, placebo) and assessment session included as fixed factors, and subject ID included as random factor. Separate models were constructed for each pair of timepoints (T0-T1, T0-T2 and T1-T2) and BH-correction was applied. Post-hoc analyses were conducted using the R package Emmeans (version 1.10.5). Association with oxytocin-induced changes MaAsLin2 analyses with BH-correction were conducted to examine whether the identified oxytocin-induced changes in bacterial abundances were related to oxytocin-induced changes in behavioral characteristics or oxytocinergic signalling. Confounding lifestyle variables In line with prior literature demonstrating a significant impact of nutrition and oral health on oral microbiome compositions [ 73 , 74 ], we here specifically assessed how these metadata lifestyle variables could impact oral microbiome compositions. More specifically, lifestyle variables related to nutrition and dental care which showed significant associations with overall microbial composition (beta-diversity) at baseline, were identified as potential confounders and therefore included as covariates in all MaAsLin2 analyses and significant linear mixed-effects models (see Supplementary Methods and Results ). For all the analyses mentioned above, the influence of outliers on the results was assessed using Cook’s distance, with values greater than 1 indicating substantial influence on the model [ 78 ]. Results After running the DADA2 pipeline, assigning taxonomic classifications, and removing genera assigned to eukaryotes, mitochondria, and chloroplasts, a total of 49 genera were included in both the baseline analyses and analyses of oxytocin effects. Associations with oxytocinergic signalling at baseline independent of diagnosis In an initial stage, we examined the association between alpha-diversity indices and markers of oxytocinergic signalling to obtain a broad overview of the relationship between overall microbial diversity and the oxytocinergic system. In a subsequent stage, more granular analyses were conducted to identify specific genera that were significantly associated with oxytocinergic signalling. Association with alpha-diversity A significantly negative association between microbial evenness (Pielou: ρ = -0.27; p = 0.003) and microbial diversity (Simpson: ρ = -0.23; p = 0.012; Shannon: ρ = -0.22; p = 0.018) and endogenous oxytocin levels was found, indicating that children with lower levels of oxytocin have more uniform abundances of bacteria as well as a higher microbial diversity (see Fig. 2A ). These associations remained significant when examined in the group of autistic children only ( p &lt; 0.05). Association with microbial abundances A significant positive association was evident between endogenous oxytocin levels and the abundances of Anaeroglobus (Coeff. = 0.015; p &lt;0.001; q = 0.015) , Veillonella (Coeff. = 0.010; p = 0.008; q = 0.080) , Prevotella (Coeff. = 0.010; p = 0.020; q = 0.104) , Granulicatella (Coeff. = 0.008; p = 0.020; q = 0.104) , Actinomyces (Coeff. = 0.008; p = 0.012; q = 0.104) , Streptococcus (Coeff. = 0.006; p = 0.040; q = 0.142) and Atopobium.B (Coeff. = 0.015; p = 0.040; q = 0.142), indicating that children with lower endogenous oxytocin levels have a lower abundance of these genera (MaAsLin2, see Fig. 2B ). Furthermore, a significant negative association was evident between endogenous oxytocin levels and the abundance of Porphyromonas (Coeff. = -0.019; p = 0.001; q = 0.031), Fusobacterium (Coeff. = -0.016; p = 0.003; q = 0.043), Parvimonas (Coeff. = -0.019; p = 0.015; q = 0.104) , Moraxella (Coeff. = -0.012; p = 0.022; q = 0.104) and UBA5124 (Coeff. = -0.015; p = 0.041; q = 0.142), indicating that children with lower endogenous oxytocin levels have a higher abundance of these genera (MaAsLin2, see Fig. 2B ). No significant associations were observed between microbial abundances and OXTR DNAm (all q &gt; 0.298). Effects of oxytocin administration Also here, in a first stage, we investigated the effect of oxytocin administration on overall microbial diversity by analyzing changes in alpha-diversity indices, providing a broad assessment of how oxytocin may influence the general composition of the microbiome. In a second stage, more detailed analyses were performed to identify specific genera whose abundance was significantly altered following oxytocin administration. Alpha-diversity After multiple testing correction, no differential changes in alpha-diversity were observed between the oxytocin and placebo groups at either the post-treatment (T1) or follow-up (T2) assessment (linear mixed-effects models, all p &gt; 0.05). Microbial abundances From baseline (T0) to post-intervention (T1), significant group-by-time interactions were found for Tannerella ( F (1;77.011) = 13.771, p &lt;0.001, q = 0.001), Moraxella ( F (1;77.007) = 5.956; p = 0.017, q = 0.183) and Fusobacterium ( F (1;77.001) = 5.770, p = 0.019, q = 0.183) (lme4, see Fig. 3A-C ), indicating an abundance decrease of these genera in the oxytocin group and a relative increase in the placebo group. Notably, as previously reported, higher baseline abundances of Moraxella and Fusobacterium were associated with lower endogenous oxytocin levels, suggesting that exogenous oxytocin administration may have contributed to the observed relative reductions. Indeed, post-hoc analyses revealed that for Moraxella a significant pre-post reduction was evident in the oxytocin group (Emmeans, see Table 2 ). Significant T0 to T1 group-by-time interactions were also identified for Actinomyces ( F (1;77.001) = 7.613, p = 0.007, q = 0.120), and Centipeda ( F (1;77.009) = 7.586, p = 0.007, q = 0.120) (lme4, see Fig. 3D-E ), indicating a relative increase in their abundance in the oxytocin group and decreased abundances in the placebo group post-intervention. Notably, also for Actinomyces , a baseline association was previously observed, showing a higher abundance in children with higher endogenous oxytocin levels. Therefore, the observed relative increase following oxytocin administration may reflect a normalization or amplification of this endogenous pattern. However, direct post-hoc analyses only revealed a significant abundance increase for Centipeda in the oxytocin group from T0 to T1 (Emmeans, see Table 2 ). For the T0 to T2 comparison, a significant group-by-time interaction was observed for Capnocytophaga ( F (1;69.475) = 11.494, p = 0.001, q = 0.057) reflecting a relative decrease in abundance in the oxytocin group and a relative increase in the placebo group (lme4, see Fig. 3F ). Post-hoc analyses showed that this effect was primarily driven by a significant increase in the placebo group (Emmeans, see Table 2 ). For Rothia , a significant differential change emerged from post-intervention (T1) to follow-up (T2) ( F (1;73.771) = 11.535, p = 0.001, q = 0.054) indicating a relative decrease in the oxytocin group and relative increases in the placebo group (lme4, see Fig. 3G ), with post-hoc analyses confirming these relative changes in both groups (Emmeans, see Table 2 ). Discussion In the current study, we investigated for the first time the associations between oral microbiome composition and oxytocinergic signalling, and to investigate whether chronic oxytocin administration can influence these factors. Consequently, our analyses were not based on specific hypotheses but were mostly of an exploratory, hypothesis-generating nature. While no baseline associations were identified between microbial genera and OXTR DNAm, twelve genera were significantly associated with endogenous oxytocin levels, including Granulicatella and Streptococcus , both of which were positively associated with higher endogenous oxytocin levels and found to be more abundant in non-autistic compared to autistic children. Granulicatella has previously been reported as more abundant in the gut microbiome of non-autistic versus autistic individuals [ 79 ], and is recognized as part of the commensal human oral microbiota [ 80 ]. Similarly, Streptococcus has been reported as more abundant in the tongue and gut microbiome of non-autistic versus autistic children in distinct prior studies [ 5 , 81 – 85 ] (albeit see [ 86 ] reporting opposite results). Overall, these mixed findings are likely due to species-specific differences within the genus Streptococcus , as some Streptococci are known to exhibit immune-protective functions, while other species have been linked to increased social difficulties in autistic children [ 87 , 88 ]. Overall, the here identified associations with endogenous oxytocin levels extend our previous findings of reduced oxytocin in autistic children from the same cohort [ 22 ], and suggest that baseline differences in specific bacterial genera may be paralleled by individual variability in oxytocinergic functioning. In the current cohort of autistic children, four weeks of intranasal oxytocin administration (compared to placebo) led to significant group-by-time interactions, including increases in Actinomyces and Centipeda (T0-T1), and decreases of Moraxella , Tannerella and Fusobacterium (T0-T1), as well as Capnocytophaga (T0-T2), and Rothia (T1-T2). Post-hoc analyses further revealed that in the oxytocin group, only significant pre-to-post increase in Centipeda and pre-to-post decreases in the abundance of Moraxella and Rothia were evident. Especially the oxytocin-induced reductions in Moraxella abundance appear to be of particular interest, given that at baseline, lower Moraxella abundance was associated with higher endogenous oxytocin levels. Moreover, children who exhibited a greater oxytocin-induced decrease in Moraxella abundance at the first follow-up (T1) demonstrated more pronounced increases in endogenous oxytocin levels, accompanied by decreases in DNAm at the OXTR CpG site -924. These findings suggest that oxytocin-driven facilitation of endogenous oxytocin signalling (as previously reported in [ 76 ]) may be accompanied by modulations of microbial compositions, specifically involving the genus Moraxella . In previous studies, early-life colonization of the respiratory tract with Moraxella has been associated with an increased risk of respiratory illnesses [ 89 , 90 ]. Moraxella catarrhalis species in the nasopharynx have also been linked to heightened inflammatory responses, characterized by increased cytokine production [ 91 , 92 ]. In the oral cavity, previous studies comparing the oral microbiome composition in the subgingival plaque of healthy controls versus people with periodontitis and Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease found Moraxella to be more abundant in people with periodontitis [ 93 ]. Overall, the observed reduction in Moraxella abundance following intranasal oxytocin administration may reflect oxytocin’s immunoregulatory effects on the oral microbiome. While speculative, these neuroimmune mechanisms could contribute to shifts in microbiome compositions, favoring certain bacterial taxa while creating a less hospitable environment for others, such as Moraxella . Other changes in microbial composition after oxytocin administration include the observed relative increase in Centipeda , a genus previously associated with dental caries [ 94 ] and reduced overall survival in oral cancer patients [ 95 ]. Additionally, a late-emerging significant decrease in Rothia was observed in the oxytocin group at four-weeks follow-up. At baseline, Rothia was found to be more abundant in non-autistic children and associated with fewer social difficulties and lower anxiety levels (see Supplementary results ). Rothia has also been linked to oral health benefits, being more prevalent in healthy individuals compared to those with oral lesions [ 96 ] or periodontitis [ 93 ]. Given oxytocin’s association with improved social functioning and reduced anxiety (see Supplementary results ), one might have expected an increase rather than a decrease in Rothia . The fact that the effect emerged only at follow-up suggests that it may not result from a direct or immediate action of oxytocin on the microbiome composition. Instead, this finding may reflect secondary or downstream processes, such as oxytocin-induced changes in immune regulation, which may in turn modulate the oral microbial environment over time. Nonetheless, increased Rothia abundance has also been reported in autistic children [ 3 ] and in individuals with blood cancer [ 97 ], Down syndrome [ 98 ], and type 2 diabetes [ 99 ], highlighting its complex and context-dependent role and probable species- and strain-specificity. Further research using higher taxonomic resolution is necessary to clarify the specific roles of these bacteria in the context of autism and oxytocin modulation. These oxytocin-induced changes in oral bacterial abundances were not associated with oxytocin-induced changes in behavioral difficulties. Notably, in this cohort no specific oxytocin effect on behavioral difficulties was observed [ 35 ]. In line with this, a study by Kong et al. (2021) investigated the effects of intranasal oxytocin alone and in combination with probiotics on the gut microbiome in 35 autistic individuals (aged 3–20 years) [ 46 ]. While the oxytocin-only group did not show significant improvements on the Clinical Global Impression scale compared to the combination group, both groups exhibited notable alterations in gut microbiome composition. These findings suggest that oxytocin may influence the microbiome independently of its clinical efficacy. In our cohort, both oxytocin and placebo groups showed similar changes in social difficulties [ 35 ], consistent with previous research in children and adolescents [ 31 ]. Overall, studies investigating the effects of multiple-dose oxytocin administration on behavioral difficulties in autistic children have reported more mixed results compared to adults [ 28 , 100 ], with some showing beneficial effects [ 62 , 63 ] and others finding no significant impact [ 101 – 103 ]. To better interpret some of the observed microbiome shifts, we believe that, in addition to potential behavioral correlations, the reported associations with endogenous oxytocin function may also be of particular relevance. For instance, as noted, the genus Moraxella appeared to have emerged as relevant, with lower baseline abundance associated with higher endogenous oxytocin levels, and a more pronounced oxytocin-induced reduction in its abundance correlated with greater increases in endogenous oxytocin levels, accompanied by hypomethylation of the oxytocin receptor gene. Although differential changes in bacterial abundances were observed between the oxytocin and placebo groups, it is noted that several of the significant shifts occurred in the placebo group, for instance, increases in Tannerella and Fusobacterium (T0–T1), Capnocytophaga (T0–T2), and Rothia (T1–T2), as well as a decrease in Actinomyces (T0–T1). Although speculative, the microbial changes observed in the placebo group may be influenced by several factors. First, it cannot be ruled out that these fluctuations reflect natural temporal variations in the oral microbiome. However, given the limited data on natural variability in the oral microbiome over time, it is challenging to fully interpret these findings. Another possible explanation is that study participation itself, also for children in the placebo group, may have induced nonspecific changes in microbiome composition. This could relate to increased expectations of social or stress-related benefits, which might have led to neuroendocrine alterations (e.g., elevated endogenous oxytocin levels), as previously observed in placebo responders who also display behavioral improvements [ 63 ]. However, in our cohort, the oxytocin group showed a significant increase in oxytocin levels relative to the placebo group [ 76 ]. Conversely, participation in the study and altered expectations (e.g., regarding improved sociability) could have also increased stress levels among participants in the placebo group who did not experience the anticipated anxiolytic and stress-regulating effects of intranasal oxytocin, which could the opposite direction of some of the observed microbial changes observed in this group. Finally, interpretation of oxytocin effects is limited by the resolution of genus-level taxonomic analyses. Future studies with more detailed taxonomic annotation are needed to improve the biological interpretation of oxytocin’s potential impact on the microbiome. The following limitations can be noted. First, the use of amplicon sequencing limited taxonomic resolution to the genus level, rendering uncertainties about the effect of oxytocin at the species/strain level. Second, instead of retrospective reports, future studies could envisage the use of prospective logs of the children’s food habits, which could allow obtaining a more detailed overview. Third, in addition to its influence on nutrition, prior studies have demonstrated that oxytocin can modulate immune and inflammatory processes, including the attenuation of neuroendocrine and cytokine activation [ 52 ]. Follow up studies should therefore include additional (blood) samples to also determine whether these systemic effects of oxytocin contribute to the observed changes in microbiome composition. Fourth, the sample comprised a homogenous group of high-functioning children within a narrow pre-pubertal age range, raising uncertainty about the generalizability of the findings. Fifth, there is a need for further research involving autistic children with intellectual disabilities (ID), given the high occurrence of ID in autism. Sixth, future studies should also aim to investigate these effects in larger cohorts of girls with autism, although the sex distribution in our sample aligns with the previously established four-to-one male-to-female ratio in autism prevalence [ 104 ]. Lastly, food intake may also influence endogenous oxytocin levels. Although our data were not detailed enough to investigate this, future studies should consider this factor. Conclusion This study is the first one providing important insights into the role of the oxytocinergic system in shaping oral microbiome composition in autistic children, along with initial evidence suggesting a modulatory effect of intranasal oxytocin administration on these microbial abundances. In particular, the bacterium Moraxella is highlighted as an important genus, with higher abundance linked to an aberrant endogenous oxytocin system, while exogenous oxytocin administration appears to downregulate its levels. These findings highlight the oral microbiome as a potential target of oxytocinergic (dys)function with diagnostic and therapeutic relevance, paving the way for future studies investigating oral bacterial abundances as biomarkers and oxytocin-based interventions to improve oral health in autism.
