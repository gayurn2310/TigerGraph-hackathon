Understanding the Use of Atypical Antipsychotic Treatment for Major Depressive Disorder

Purpose: The aim of this study was to describe pharmacological prescribing patterns for major depressive disorder (MDD), particularly regarding the use of atypical antipsychotics (AAPs) for the treatment of MDD, and the underlying factors that may influence decision-making. Methods: An electronic cross-sectional survey with 210 health care professionals (HCPs) involved in the treatment of MDD in 4 countries [United States (57), Canada (53), Brazil (39), Japan (61)] was conducted in May 2024 to July 2024. Results: Of the total HCPs, 62.9% (n = 132) were psychiatrists, 31.9% (n = 67) primary care physicians or general practitioners, and 5.2% (n = 11) nurse practitioners or physician assistants. Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) were the most commonly prescribed first-line antidepressants across all countries surveyed. Regardless of disease severity, the first step for patients with inadequate response to the first antidepressant treatment was reported to be switching to another antidepressant (mild MDD: 66.7%, moderate MDD: 56.2%, severe MDD: 41.0%). Overall, SSRIs were the most common treatment class, typically augmented with AAPs, followed by serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors. HCPs across the countries surveyed increasingly utilized augmentation with AAPs as the severity of MDD increased, with the severity of the current depressive episode and the number of prior antidepressants being the most important patient and treatment factors, respectively, in this decision. In addition, symptom domains, particularly underactive noradrenergic/norepinephrinergic activity and anxiety, were deemed crucial by HCPs in later treatment lines. Conclusions: The study highlighted complex treatment paradigms in MDD and the need for alternative therapeutic strategies and more tailored options that specifically target receptor pharmacology.

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is one of the most burdensome psychiatric illnesses globally, impacting many areas of a patient’s functioning including quality of life, cognitive function, employment, and work productivity. 1 – 3 An estimated 21.0 million adults in the United States (8.3% of all adults) had at least one major depressive episode in 2021. 4 The prevalence of MDD is growing over time, with the World Health Organization estimating in 2023 that 5% of adults, equating to ∼280 million people worldwide, suffer from depression. 5 Treatment options available for people with MDD include pharmacological and nonpharmacological therapy and neuromodulation approaches. For patients initiating on pharmacological therapy, there are currently numerous antidepressant medication options available; these include selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs), monoamine oxidase inhibitors (MAOIs), tricyclic agents, and other treatments. 6 , 7 However, these treatments may not be effective in some cases due to the heterogenous nature of MDD, differences in neurobiology that can affect individual’s response, and side effects. 6 Unresolved symptoms such as low energy (affecting up to 80% of patients), sleep disturbances (60% to 80%), behavioral symptoms (40% to 60%), cognitive impairment (30% to 50%), agitation (26% to 64%), irritability (37% to 40%), lack of concentration (25% to 50%), and anxiety (75%) are identified as especially disruptive to global functioning. 8 – 16 For patients who have an inadequate response to first-line antidepressant therapy, there are adjunctive treatments available, such as atypical antipsychotics (AAPs), which can address ongoing and persistent symptoms of depression. 17 , 18 AAPs are used synonymously with second-generation antipsychotics (SGAs) and can broadly be described as dopamine and serotonin receptor modulators, consistent with the Neuroscience-based Nomenclature framework. 19 Understanding which symptoms are most prominent can help in selecting the most appropriate second- or late line of treatment. Modulating various receptors including serotonin, norepinephrine, dopamine, histamine, and glutamate receptors can help address unresolved symptoms of MDD. 20 , 21 A growing body of evidence indicates that hyperactivity of norepinephrine signaling is a key factor in psychiatric disorders. Conversely, there is also significant evidence suggesting that deficits in the noradrenergic system are equally important. 10 By targeting specific neurotransmitter systems, treatments can be more tailored to the specific symptom profiles of patients, potentially leading to better outcomes and fewer unresolved symptoms. Numerous evidence-based clinical practice guidelines (CPGs) have been developed in different countries with the aim of guiding decision-making to positively impact patient outcomes. 22 – 34 However, the extent to which these guidelines are applied in clinical practice is unclear. A systematic review on the implementation of psychiatric guidelines found that changes to clinical practice based on these guidelines were generally moderate and temporary. 35 Another systematic review highlighted that there were few attempts to evaluate the impact of CPGs on clinical practice, and that different CPGs can vary in their effect on prescribing practice. 36 Furthermore, the specific guidance given on pharmaceutical treatment patterns, including the use of AAPs, varies from country to country, and there may be discrepancies in prescribing habits for different regions, which may not match local guidelines. Therefore, a need for better understanding of real-world prescribing decisions by HCPs for MDD and how these adhere to local guidelines is warranted. The aim of this study was to describe pharmacological prescribing patterns of HCPs by line of therapy for MDD within the United States, Canada, Brazil, and Japan; particularly regarding the use of AAPs for the treatment of MDD by line of therapy and symptom domains, and the underlying factors that may influence decision-making. METHODS Study Design and Participants An online cross-sectional survey was administered to HCPs who were involved in the treatment and management of MDD in the United States, Canada, Brazil, and Japan. The survey was implemented in 2 phases: a pilot phase (in which 2 HCPs from the United States took part in an interview to collect feedback on the survey) and the survey implementation phase, in which the survey was administered. The pilot phase was utilized to ensure the appropriateness of the survey questions and response options in relation to clinical practice. Questions were developed in collaboration with country-based medical experts and focused on current management practices across the MDD treatment pathway (Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/JCP/B10 ). The survey included questions on; (1) respondent profile (speciality, practice setting, years of experience, number of patients managed per month and the severity of patients with MDD currently treated), (2) current MDD treatment practices, (3) importance of selected symptom domains [symptoms related to overactive noradrenergic/norepinephrinergic activity ( NA ; eg, irritability, agitation, hyperarousal), 10 , 37 underactive NA (eg, lack of concentration, low energy, lassitude), 10 , 38 and anxiety symptoms 20 ] when selecting optimal treatment, and (4) current AAPs prescribing practices. HCPs were recruited between May 2024 and July 2024. HCPs were eligible to take part in the survey if they: (1) were a licensed HCP currently involved in the treatment and management of MDD in the United States, Canada, Brazil, and Japan, (2) were currently employed as either a primary care physician, psychiatrist, physician assistant, or nurse practitioner, (3) had ≥5 years of experience both diagnosing and prescribing pharmacological treatment for MDD (NB. practitioners may also prescribe nonpharmacological treatment), and (4) managed ≥5 patients with MDD per month. Individuals were excluded from participation if they evaluated patients with MDD exclusively in the inpatient setting, did not prescribe pharmacological treatment for MDD, were full-time employees at a pharmaceutical company, or were unwilling to provide electronic consent to participate in the survey. An estimated total of ∼200 HCPs were planned to be included (target 50 per country). The sample size was pragmatic based on the feasibility of being able to recruit HCPs in the countries of interest. The study was an electronic survey that was programmed and hosted on a secure web-based platform by Global Perspectives in close collaboration with OPEN Health. HCPs were invited and recruited using online panels (by Global Perspectives) based on prespecified criteria. An online panel is a community of preselected individuals interested and willing to participate in market research studies, such as online surveys. When an HCP first shows interest in taking part in a panel, they are required to complete an initial enrollment and verification process whereby their details and medical credentials are submitted and verified against various sources including state license records, directories, publicly available verified health care portals, and official physician databases. Once these HCPs are verified, they are activated within the community and are eligible to be invited to take part in surveys. When the survey was launched, an invite email was randomly sent to HCPs within the panels. Invites were sent until the required sample size targets in each country were met (convenience sampling). HCPs were asked to provide electronic written consent to participate in the survey. Those who provided consent to participate were asked to complete a series of screening questions (aligned to the inclusion and exclusion criteria) accessed through a unique link to determine eligibility for the survey. The HCP participants in the survey (and pilot phase) received a standard stipend for their participation. Pseudonymised raw data were sent to OPEN Health for analysis. The study was determined to be exempt from IRB review, in accordance with 45 CFR 46.104(d)(2(i) and 2(ii)), as assessed by SALUS IRB (ID: 24013 - 01), and since this was an electronic HCP survey, no additional local approvals were needed for the conduct of this study and the sharing of pseudonymised data. The study followed the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Statistical Methods Continuous variables were summarized using mean (SD), median [interquartile range (IQR)], 95% CI, and range, as appropriate. Categorical variables were summarized using frequency and percentages. Ordinal variables (eg, ranking specific treatment options) were evaluated using either frequencies and percentages or medians and IQR or both depending on the number of possible values for the variable. For questions with free-text responses, the answers were translated to English, and the common themes were identified. The proportion of responses aligning with each theme was quantitatively summarized. In this survey, participants could choose to bypass questions, thus the number and percentage of missing responses were reported where relevant. Results of one question were excluded due to the ambiguous nature of the wording. Statistical analyses were carried out using Microsoft Excel 365 and were presented for the overall cohort and separately by country. HCP characteristics are summarized in Table 1 . Of the total respondents, 62.9% (n = 132) were psychiatrists and 31.9% (n = 67) were primary care physicians or general practitioners. Approximately one-third of the HCPs primarily worked in primary care/general practice, one-third in specialist clinics/centers, and one-third in hospital clinics/outpatient settings. The distribution of the respondents’ main work setting varied between countries; the most common setting was primary care/general practice in the United States (61.4%), specialist clinic/center (34.0%) and hospital clinic/outpatient setting (34.0%) in Canada, specialist clinic/centre (59.0%) in Brazil, and hospital clinic/outpatient setting (41.0%) in Japan. First-Line Treatment Overall, the treatment classes typically prescribed as first-line antidepressant therapy for patients with MDD experiencing their first depressive episode were SSRIs [prescribed by 94.3% (n = 198) of respondents], SNRIs [prescribed by 73.8% (n = 155) respondents] and serotonin modulators [prescribed by 45.2% (n = 95) respondents]; 55.2% (n = 116) also prescribed other types of antidepressants (Figure 1 ). HCPs reported the typical treatment they would prescribe to patients experiencing their second or later MDD episode would be an SNRI (81.4%; Figure 1 ). They estimated that a mean of 40.9% (SD 16.2%, 95% CI: 38.7%-43.1%) of patients with MDD have an inadequate response to their first antidepressant therapy; ranging from 36.0% (16.6%, 95% CI: 30.6%-41.4%) in Brazil to 43.7% (17.0%, 95% CI: 39.1%-48.4%) in Canada (Table 2 ). Second-Line Treatment The HCPs reported that of patients prescribed a second line of antidepressant therapy, most were prescribed a monotherapy antidepressant [mean (SD) 39.8% (20.7%), 95% CI: 36.9%-42.6%; Table 2 ]. Regardless of disease severity and if psychotic symptoms were present, the most common next step for patients who did not respond adequately to their first antidepressant treatment was reported to be switching to another antidepressant (Figure 2 ). This approach was reported to be taken by 66.7% of HCPs for mild MDD, 56.2% for moderate MDD, and 41.0% for severe MDD. Similar trends were observed across countries except for Brazil, where the most common typical next step for patients with severe MDD was to combine the current antidepressant with another antidepressant (Figure S2, Supplemental Digital Content 2, http://links.lww.com/JCP/B11 ). The HCPs estimated [mean (SD)] that 26.4% (15.3%, 95% CI: 24.4%-28.5%) of patients with MDD have an inadequate response to second-line antidepressant treatment (Table 2 ). The HCPs ranked the importance of selected symptom domains (underactive noradrenergic/norepinephrinergic activity [ NA ], overactive NA , and anxiety) when selecting optimal second-line treatment [1 (not important) to 10 (most important)]. They rated the following domains as highly important (≥8) for selecting second-line treatment for patients who did not respond adequately to their first antidepressant treatment: 65.7% for underactive NA , 60.0% for overactive NA , and 64.8% for anxiety (Table 2 ). Third-Line Treatment The HCPs rated the following domains as highly important (≥8) for selecting third-line treatment for patients who did not respond adequately to their second antidepressant treatment: 66.7% for underactive NA , 64.3% for overactive NA , and 64.3% for anxiety (Table 2 ). They reported that most patients who were prescribed a third-line antidepressant therapy received a combination of antidepressants [mean (SD) 37.4% (18.8%), 95% CI: 34.8%-40.0%]; with the highest average in the United States [43.5% (20.8%), 95% CI: 38.0%-49.0%] and the lowest in Japan [32.1% (17.0%), 95% CI: 27.8%-36.5%; Table 2 ]. Current Atypical Antipsychotics Prescribing Practices The majority of HCPs (88.1%, n = 185) reported that they prescribe AAPs for the treatment of MDD (United States: 77.2%, Canada: 92.5%, Brazil: 92.3%, Japan: 91.8%; Table 3 ). They reported, overall, a mean of 27.7% (SD 15.4%; 95% CI: 25.5%-29.9%) of patients with MDD receive an AAP treatment, ranging from 22.3% (11.7%; 95% CI: 18.7%-25.8%) in the United States to 34.4% (17.1%; 95% CI: 29.5%-39.4%) in Canada. Per line of treatment, the HCPs estimated that a mean of 13.5% (SD: 19.8%, 95% CI: 10.7%-16.4%), 26.8% (17.4%, 24.3%-29.3%), and 34.3% (17.0%, 31.8%-36.8%) of patients received AAPs in the first, second, and third lines of treatment, respectively. The lowest estimation of patients with MDD receiving an AAP treatment by their first-line was in Canada [6.6% (11.7%); 95% CI: 3.2%-9.9%], while the highest estimation was in Brazil [23.9% (28.1%); 95% CI: 14.4%-33.4%]. Factors Affecting Atypical Antipsychotics Use The severity of the current depressive episode was reported to be the most important patient factor influencing treatment with AAPs (overall: 55.1%, United States: 52.3%, Canada: 59.2%, Brazil: 61.1%, Japan: 50.0%, Figure S4, Supplemental Digital Content 2, http://links.lww.com/JCP/B11 ). Number of prior antidepressants was the most important treatment factor influencing US HCPs’ decisions (38.6%) to prescribe AAPs for MDD, while the nature of prior response to medication was the most important treatment factor for Canadian (42.9%) and Brazilian HCPs (33.3%) to prescribe AAPs (Figure S5, Supplemental Digital Content 2, http://links.lww.com/JCP/B11 ). Relative efficacy and effectiveness of AAP treatment options were the most important treatment factor influencing Japanese HCPs’ treatment with AAPs (33.9%). Among HCPs who reported they do not currently prescribe AAPs (n = 25), safety/tolerability/anticipated side effects was the most common reason reported for not prescribing AAPs, overall (64.0%, n = 16) and by United States (69.2%, n = 9 out of 13) and Brazilian (66.7%, n = 2 out of 3) HCPs while insufficient information/evidence on effectiveness and /or safety was the most common reason reported by HCPs from Canada (100%, n = 4 out of 4) and Japan (60.0%, n = 3 out of 5; Figure S6, Supplemental Digital Content 2, http://links.lww.com/JCP/B11 ). The same proportion of HCPs from Japan (60.0%, n = 3 out of 5) also reported that AAPs are not currently recommended for use in the local setting. Preferred Receptor Target Per Symptom Domain The importance of targeting receptor domains was surveyed in this study. The HCPs were asked to rank potential receptor targets including α1 antagonism (decreases NA ), α2 antagonism (increases NA ), 5-HT2A antagonism, 5-HT1A agonism, and dopaminergic receptor antagonism / partial agonism from most to least likely to address symptom domains. The receptor targets ranked as most likely to address overactive NA domain symptoms (eg, irritability, agitation, hyperarousal) were reported to be dopaminergic receptor antagonism/partial agonism (36.2%) and α1 antagonism (29.0%; Figure 5 ). To address underactive NA domain symptoms (eg, lack of concentration, low energy, lassitude), the most likely receptor targets were reported to be α2 antagonism (39.5%) and dopaminergic receptor antagonism/partial agonism (28.1%). The HCPs ranked 5-HT2A antagonism (27.6%) and 5-HT1A antagonism (23.8%) as the most likely potential receptor targets to address anxiety symptoms. DISCUSSION This study provided insight into real-world prescribing practices and the factors influencing treatment decisions for MDD across different countries and provided an understanding of targeted adjunctive treatments based on symptoms. Due to favorable antidepressant efficacy and tolerability, SSRIs are consistently recommended as first choice for the first line of treatment of MDD in international guidelines; 39 consistent with the study findings demonstrating SSRIs are typically prescribed as first-line antidepressant therapy for patients with MDD experiencing their first depressive episode. Approximately 50% of patients with MDD do not achieve an adequate response to their initial antidepressant treatment according to the Sequenced Treatment Alternatives to Relieve Depression (STAR-D) trial, highlighting the unmet need for patients on first-line treatment. 40 In this real-world study, HCPs estimated that 40.9% (SD: 16.2%) of patients with MDD have an inadequate response to their first antidepressant therapy. This is slightly lower than the STAR-D trial, likely due to observed real-world findings versus the clinical trial setting, further highlighting the true unmet need for patients on first-line treatment. A recent narrative review of literature published from 2018 to 2023 (including clinical trials and observational studies) highlighted that ∼50% of patients with MDD do not achieve an adequate response to their initial antidepressant treatment. 41 Results from an international online survey in patients with MDD also showed that about half of patients fail to respond adequately to their first antidepressant. 42 According to the literature, ∼30% of patients with MDD do not respond adequately to second-line treatment, which is in line with our finding [26.4% (SD: 15.3%)]. 43 – 45 This highlights the challenge of managing treatment-resistant depression and the need for alternative therapeutic strategies. Switching antidepressants is a common strategy when patients have an inadequate response to an initial antidepressant. A US study of an administrative claims database found that among over 130,000 adult and child patients who started antidepressant monotherapy for a new episode of depression, 23.2% had a treatment change, most commonly an antidepressant switch (9.5%) or switch to combination therapy (9.1%). 46 In our study, the majority of HCPs reported switching to another antidepressant for patients who had an inadequate response to the first antidepressant treatment, which was consistent across different disease severities. Combining antidepressants is a recognized step for those failing to respond to monotherapy. 47 Our findings indicated that the majority of patients with MDD were treated with a combination of antidepressants as their third-line treatment. This aligns with a United States claims study that showed a decrease in monotherapy and an increase in adjunctive treatments with subsequent lines of therapy. 48 The HCPs reported the highest proportion of patients received augmentation with AAPs for third-line treatment, which is consistent with the aforementioned US study. 48 The US study also reported that 2 percent of total patients received AAPs, which is lower than the proportion reported in our study [27.7% (SD: 15.4%)]. However, the US study was based on claims data limited to patients within a specific employer health plan, and the majority of patients were seen by primary care physicians who were less likely to prescribe adjunctive therapy and AAPs. Studies have shown that MDD patients typically cycle through 5 to 6 different treatments before considering AAPs. This cycling is associated with increased HCRU and costs. 49 , 50 Our findings indicate that HCPs across the countries utilize augmentation with AAPs more frequently as the severity of MDD increases. Consistent with a noninterventional study conducted in 60 community mental health centers throughout Spain showed that AAPs were the most commonly used agents for augmenting the antidepressant effect. 51 The most commonly reported reasons for not prescribing AAPs in this study were their lack of recommendation for use in the local setting, concerns about safety, tolerability, and anticipated side effects, as well as insufficient information or evidence regarding their effectiveness and/or safety. The use of antipsychotic medications in non-psychotic patients warrants careful consideration. Evidence supporting such off-label use is limited, with observed benefits often attributable to nonspecific effects like sedation rather than targeted therapeutic action. Moreover, the risk of adverse outcomes—including metabolic disturbances, extrapyramidal symptoms, and cognitive impairment—raises concerns about the overall benefit–risk balance. Ethical and regulatory frameworks also emphasize the need for informed consent and close monitoring in these cases. Therefore, antipsychotic prescribing in non-psychotic populations should remain exceptional and reserved for situations where evidence-based alternatives have been exhausted. 52 – 55 Unlike other surveys, this study inquired on addressing monoaminergic symptom domains and how this may impact treatment decision-making when selecting an adjunctive treatment. AAPs are often used when depressive symptoms are severe and resistant to first-line treatments. 56 However, AAPs can be considered earlier in the treatment of MDD as they can target a broad range of symptoms, enhance the overall treatment efficacy, have relatively rapid onset of action in alleviating depressive symptoms, and modulate multiple neurotransmitter systems. In our study, 65.7% and 66.7% of HCPs rated the lack of concentration, low energy, and lassitude (underactive NA symptom domain) as highly important (≥8) for treating patients who did not respond adequately to first and second antidepressant treatment, respectively. HCPs also recognized the importance of targeting symptom domains related to overactive NA or underactive NA when treating MDD. HCPs’ selections varied widely in this study, highlighting the need for education on receptor pharmacology. This study suggests that patients may require more tailored treatment options that specifically target the most relevant receptor pharmacology for their current clinical status. The study showed a discrepancy between the guidelines and treatment practice, which may be driven by regional differences in prescribing habits that do not align with local guidelines. For example, HCPs reported that 6.6% and 14.2% of Canadian and Japanese patients with MDD, respectively, receive an AAP as a first-line treatment, which is not recommended by CANMAT 30 and the Japanese Society of Depression Treatment Guidelines. Moreover, there did not appear to be a single “gold standard” CPG. The use of various CPGs both within and between countries may have introduced variations in practices, potentially impacting the results. A retrospective analysis of linked primary and secondary care data in the UK examined the real-world application of treatment guidelines for depression and treatment-resistant depression. It highlighted the long-term use of single antidepressants, poor remission rates, and high relapse rates in primary care settings, emphasizing the need for optimized treatment pathways. 57 The latest CANMAT guidelines, 58 updated in 2023, provide significant revisions from the previous 2016 version. These updates include more robust scientific evidence and revised recommendations for health care professionals, as well as incorporating the perspectives of patients with lived experience. The guidelines emphasize the use of adjunctive treatments, including AAPs, stimulants, and mood stabilizers, to enhance the effectiveness of primary antidepressants. There are several limitations to our study. Firstly, results may be subject to selection bias since HCPs who formed part of the recruitment panels and/or opted to participate in the survey may not represent the views of wider HCPs involved in the treatment of MDD who were not enrolled in the panels or chose not to respond. However, the planned recruitment targets were chosen to reflect existing knowledge and data on the specialties involved in the diagnosis and management of MDD, as well as variations in health care systems in different countries, and so are expected to be representative of the treating HCPs in those countries. For example, the health care systems in Canada, Brazil, and Japan are primarily public (government-funded), with the state acting as the major provider of health services, health promotion, prevention, diagnosis, and treatment. In these countries, internal data shows that MDD is primarily diagnosed by a general practitioner or a primary care physician, and they provide a referral to other specialized practitioners such as the psychiatrist. In the United States, where the health care system is mainly private, additional specialties, for example, physician assistants and nurse practitioners, can also diagnose MDD. Since physician assistants and nurse practitioners do not commonly diagnose and treat patients with MDD in the other countries (Canada, Brazil, and Japan), these HCPs will be recruited for the United States only. Therefore, the sample of HCPs selected for each country reflects the distribution of HCPs in the respective country that commonly diagnose/treat patients with MDD to maximize generalizability. Secondly, the data relied on the completeness of the answers provided by respondents, which were not queried or otherwise followed up to clarify inconsistencies or omissions, and may be subject to reporting bias. Thirdly, due to survey constraints, not all pertinent factors related to prescribing decisions were accounted for in the survey questions; for example, treatment decisions for initiation of next-line therapy based on comorbidities were not addressed. Fourthly, in real-world practice, clinicians may also make decisions based on their own clinical judgment and prior experiences. No prior definition for disease severity was provided, as we aimed to capture how health care professionals manage their patients in real-world practice. Therefore, their approach would be based on their own experience, judgment, and the guidelines they were using. In addition, there may be differences in approved treatment options, timing of approvals, and market access across countries. Finally, we acknowledge that compensating participants for their time may have influenced their behavior or responses, introducing potential bias. In this survey, SSRIs were consistently the most commonly prescribed first-line antidepressants across all countries surveyed. In general, SSRIs were the most common treatment class typically augmented with AAPs, followed by SNRIs. Generally, HCPs across the countries surveyed increasingly utilize augmentation with AAPs as the severity of MDD increases, with the severity of the current depressive episode being the most important factor in this decision. Although some variability was observed across countries, the findings indicate that AAPs are sometimes being used earlier in the treatment pathway than recommended by guidelines. In addition, the selected symptom domains deemed crucial by HCPs when treating a patient with an inadequate response to first or second-line of antidepressant treatment widely varied, suggesting unmet need for education on receptor pharmacology.
