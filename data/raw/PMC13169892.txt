AI-enabled digital phenotyping for Alzheimer’s disease: a review of multimodal sensor integration and symptom trajectories

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is characterized by progressive cognitive impairment accompanied by behavioral disturbances and neuropsychiatric manifestations. Conventional clinic-based assessments and biomarkers provide essential diagnostic information, but these episodic measurements are limited in capturing longitudinal AD-related symptoms. Digital phenotyping has emerged as a complementary approach that addresses this limitation by enabling continuous monitoring of cognitive and functional changes in everyday life. This narrative review defines digital phenotyping as a longitudinal monitoring approach that complements episodic clinical evaluations rather than replacing diagnostic assessment. Building on this, we propose a novel, stage-specific digital phenotyping framework that integrates passive and active data streams with artificial intelligence (AI) and non-AI-driven analytics to generate personalized AD symptom profiles aligned with disease progression. AI enhances the interpretability of subtle cognitive and behavioral changes observed in daily life by transforming continuously collected real-world data into clinically actionable insights across different stages of disease progression. In addition, we address three strategic priorities for advancing AI-driven digital phenotyping in AD: the development of standardized phenotyping protocols, the implementation of ambient sensing systems for later disease stages, and AI-enabled longitudinal multimodal data fusion. Moreover, we describe how increased variability and subtle disruptions in daily routines may reflect early AD progression and outline key considerations for real-world implementation, including data integration, interpretability, and clinical workflow alignment. Collectively, this review provides new insights into digital phenotyping as a scalable monitoring infrastructure that complements biomarker frameworks and enables continuous assessment across the AD continuum.

Introduction Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is the most prevalent neurodegenerative disorder. AD is characterized by progressive memory decline accompanied by cognitive, behavioral, motor, and neuropsychiatric symptoms. In addition to memory impairment, individuals with AD frequently exhibit language dysfunction, visuospatial deficits, motor slowing, mood disturbances, and sleep disruption [ 1 – 3 ]. These multidomain manifestations, which contribute to functional decline and caregiver burden, reflect the heterogeneous and dynamic pathophysiology of AD [ 4 ]. AD symptoms may differ not only across individuals but also temporally in the same individual. Consequently, episodic clinic-based evaluations may not fully capture the longitudinal expression of AD in daily life. AD diagnosis currently relies on two clinically distinct dimensions: biological evidence of AD pathology and clinical syndromic staging. AD pathology may be identified by measuring biomarkers of AD, such as β-amyloid and tau, by PET imaging or biofluid-based assays, whereas clinical syndromic staging reflects AD symptom severity and functional status, ranging from cognitively unimpaired to mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and dementia [ 5 , 6 ]. Recent diagnostic frameworks highlight the challenges of this distinction. The revised Alzheimer’s Association criteria emphasize a biomarker-based definition of AD regardless of clinical symptom stage [ 6 ]. By contrast, the International Working Group framework conceptualizes AD as a clinical–biological construct and specifies that amyloid and tau biomarker positivity in cognitively normal individuals should be interpreted as an at-risk state rather than a diagnosis of AD [ 7 ]. The limitations of conventional clinic-based assessments have spurred interest in digital biomarkers and digital phenotyping as complementary approaches for monitoring the longitudinal, real-world expression of cognitive, behavioral, and functional changes in AD progression [ 8 ]. Digital biomarkers are defined as quantifiable physiological, behavioral, or functional metrics derived from digital device data [ 9 ]. In contrast to these measurable indicators, digital phenotyping refers to the broader methodological approach capturing high-frequency behavioral and functional data in real-world environments [ 10 ]. Accordingly, digital biomarkers are produced by applying quantitative analytical methods to data collected through digital phenotyping [ 11 ]. Digital phenotyping approaches in AD are commonly conducted using either active or passive approaches, depending on whether they involve structured task-based data collection or continuous monitoring during daily life [ 12 ]. Digital cognitive assessments in AD have conventionally focused on active digital phenotyping, in which individuals perform structured cognitive tasks under predefined conditions during clinic-based visits [ 13 ]. Although task-based approaches cannot be used for pathological diagnosis in AD, recent findings indicate that they are useful as syndromic screening tools. For example, Brain OK, a smartphone-based cognitive assessment tool, can differentiate older adults with cognitive impairment from cognitively normal individuals with high discriminative accuracy [ 14 ]. Yet the use of task-based approaches to assess cognitive function within structured testing environments at isolated time points cannot reflect symptom variability in daily life [ 15 ]. Passive digital phenotyping enables unobtrusive, repeated assessment of behavior and function in naturalistic settings, making it particularly well suited for tracking symptom dynamics over time [ 12 , 16 ]. Passive tools are frequently overlooked in standard clinical assessments, despite their capacity to quantify motor, affective, and behavioral domains and to generate ecologically valid profiles of AD symptomatology [ 12 , 17 ]. Passive digital phenotyping enables continuous monitoring of real-world behavior and physiology through ubiquitous digital devices such as smartphones, wearables, and ambient sensors [ 8 ]. These devices capture multidimensional real-world parameters that represent cognitive and functional health [ 12 ], including mobility patterns, speech and typing dynamics, physiological signals, and daily activity rhythms, offering opportunities to detect early and heterogeneous manifestations of AD. For example, features such as gait variability and vocal flattening may signal prodromal symptoms before clinical diagnosis [ 18 , 19 ]. Lifelogging data encompassing movement, social interaction, and sleep patterns are also promising digital [ 12 ]. Indeed, a recent study confirmed that longitudinal tracking further improves sensitivity in monitoring AD progression and treatment [ 20 ]. However, studies of digital phenotyping in MCI remain limited in scope, focusing largely on cognitive measures while underutilizing multimodal sensor data [ 21 , 22 ]. Recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI) offer the ability to integrate multimodal data streams, enhancing diagnostic precision and enabling the discovery of latent AD-specific phenotypes. Although these AI-based systems show considerable promise, they remain at a proof-of-concept stage, with limited clinical validation and no regulatory approval for routine use [ 23 ]. In addition, regulatory-approved digital therapeutic products primarily target cognitive remediation, with minimal focus on non-cognitive pathologies, such as motor and neuropsychological symptoms [ 21 ]. This review aims to consolidate knowledge on current digital phenotyping approaches for AD, focusing on sensor-based modalities and AI- and non-AI-based analytics. Specifically, this review 1) examines digital phenotyping approaches in AD within a longitudinal monitoring framework rather than a diagnostic paradigm; 2) clarifies how digital phenotyping captures cognitive and non-cognitive symptoms as continuous, real-world behavioral and functional signals, distinct from episodic, task-based digital cognitive assessments; 3) delineates how these digital phenotypes should be interpreted across stages of the AD continuum, from preclinical and prodromal phases to established dementia; 4) outlines the clinical, methodological, and regulatory considerations required for real-world implementation; 5) explains how smart technologies facilitate lifelog data acquisition to operationalize digital phenotyping in real-world settings; and 6) lays out limitations and future directions for digital phenotyping in AD. Methods Review design and scope This review was conducted as a narrative review aimed at synthesizing emerging evidence on digital phenotyping approaches in Alzheimer’s disease (AD). Rather than comprehensively cataloging all available studies or performing a formal systematic review, the primary objective was to conceptually map the evolving landscape of digital phenotyping in AD and to identify recurring methodological patterns, analytic strategies, and symptom domains. Given the relatively limited number of eligible studies and the substantial heterogeneity in study design, data modalities, analytic frameworks, and outcome measures, a narrative synthesis approach was considered more appropriate than a formal systematic review or meta-analysis. The emphasis of this review is therefore on domain-level integration and conceptual interpretation rather than quantitative aggregation or comparative effect estimation. Literature search strategy In December 2025, a literature search was conducted using four electronic databases: PubMed, Scopus, IEEE Xplore, and Web of Science. The search focused on studies explicitly investigating digital phenotyping or digital phenotyping-based approaches in individuals with Alzheimer’s disease. The search strategy was designed to capture studies employing digital device-derived data to assess behavioral, cognitive, or functional characteristics in real-world or naturalistic contexts. The literature included in this review reflects studies published up to December 2025. Eligibility criteria Eligible studies were original research articles published in English that investigated digital phenotyping approaches in human participants with Alzheimer’s disease. For the purposes of this review, digital phenotyping was defined as the use of digital device–derived data to capture behavioral, cognitive, or functional characteristics in naturalistic or real-world settings. To maintain conceptual clarity, studies were excluded if they relied exclusively on conventional biomarkers, such as neuroimaging or biofluid measures, without incorporating digitally derived behavioral data. Research based primarily on structured electronic health records (EHRs) was also excluded, as EHRs represent formal clinical documentation rather than naturalistic digital phenotypes. In addition, review articles, conference proceedings, research protocols, case reports, books, and other non-original publications were not considered. Screening and narrative synthesis Titles and abstracts were independently screened by three authors (SK, JK, and JJ), and full-text articles were assessed when eligibility was uncertain. Given the limited number of eligible studies and substantial methodological heterogeneity, formal quality appraisal and risk-of-bias assessment were not conducted, consistent with the narrative review design. Included studies were organized using a domain-based narrative framework. Studies were grouped by digital modality (e.g., wearable sensors, smartphone-based assessments), data type (active vs. passive), target symptom domain, analytic approach (AI-based vs. non-AI methods), and AD stage. This framework enabled the identification of methodological trends, recurring analytic strategies, and conceptual patterns across heterogeneous study designs. This narrative synthesis aimed to highlight overarching trends and emerging directions in digital phenotyping for AD, rather than to provide a quantitative or statistically pooled evaluation of study outcomes. Results This review examines behavioral digital phenotypes derived from an individual’s everyday activities, with a focus on passive, real-world indicators rather than explicit or task-based cognitive testing. The conceptual transition from clinic-based cognitive assessment to daily-life digital phenotyping highlights how established neuropsychological constructs can be mapped onto observable behavioral signals in naturalistic settings. This framework organizes everyday-life digital phenotypes into communication-level interactions, person-worn mobility and activity patterns, and context-aware passive signals derived from the home environment (Fig. 1 ). The review encompasses diverse sensing modalities, symptom dimensions, and stages of AD progression, which are summarized according to representative data sources, primary symptom targets, and their relevance across the AD continuum (Table 1 ). Computerized and smart device-based cognitive assessment Smartphone- and tablet-based cognitive platforms Smartphone- and tablet-based platforms enable repeated, remote cognitive assessment, allowing cognitive function in AD to be characterized as a dynamic digital phenotype rather than a single-point test outcome. Digital platforms enable self-administration and unsupervised collection of data on cognitive interactions in everyday life, generating high-resolution behavioral data over time. For example, BrainCheck Memory delivers brief cognitive tasks via mobile devices and can differentiate cognitively unimpaired older adults from those with MCI or dementia [ 28 , 29 ]. Beyond providing classification accuracy, digital platforms capture response latency and interaction-level metrics that extend conventional total scores by offering continuous and behavioral representations of cognitive function. More recent mobile approaches emphasize high-frequency, repeated assessment to capture intra-individual cognitive performance. Short-duration tasks administered multiple times per day produce stable estimates of cognitive performance trajectories in individuals at risk for AD [ 30 ]. When combined with high-frequency sampling approaches, multi-day unsupervised protocols generate reliable learning-based indices comparable to in-clinic cognitive performance [ 31 , 32 ]. Repeated cognitive measures from these smartphone- and tablet-based platforms can be used to generate longitudinal digital phenotypes across stages of AD. Gamified and virtual cognitive tasks Gamified and virtual cognitive tasks extend digital phenotyping by capturing cognitive function through complex, goal-directed behavioral interactions that more closely reflect real-world demands. These approaches assess domains such as spatial navigation, executive control, and planning, which are affected early in AD but are less represented in standard memory-focused assessments. For example, in Sea Hero Quest, a smartphone-based navigation game that quantifies wayfinding behavior on a scale, the gameplay-derived navigation performance of cognitively unimpaired individuals carrying genetic risk factors for AD (e.g., APOE ε4) differs from that of cognitively unimpaired individuals without genetic risk factors [ 33 , 34 ]. Metrics in gamified and virtual cognitive tasks include navigation efficiency, path selection, and error patterns, which are used as behavioral digital measures rather than simple summary scores. Immersive virtual reality (VR)-based assessments further enhance this paradigm by embedding cognitive challenges within ecologically valid, simulated environments. Performance on an AI-assisted VR cognitive test was recently shown to have strong associations with amyloid and tau, which are biomarkers for AD, and achieved high accuracy in distinguishing individuals with MCI or amyloid positivity from healthy controls [ 35 ]. Similarly, the Smart Aging platform employs a VR-based serious game that simulates an individual’s everyday activities within a virtual home, allowing memory, executive function, and navigation to be inferred from task completion strategies, timing, and movement trajectories [ 36 ]. These gamified and VR-based approaches generate multidimensional behavioral data derived from complex task interactions rather than isolated test responses. Regulatory-approved tools for digital phenotyping in AD Most digital applications that have received regulatory approval for cognitive assessment in AD focus on structured cognitive testing and remediation [ 37 , 38 ]. These tools capture structured behavioral data through smart devices, including tablets, wearable interfaces, and AI-integrated platforms [ 39 , 40 ]. For example, CANTAB Mobile® [ 41 ] and Cognivue Clarity® [ 42 ] deliver structured visual memory and sensorimotor tasks via tablet or computer interfaces (Table 1 ). The resulting metrics, including reaction time, visuospatial accuracy, and executive response speed, provide indirect but standardized markers of early cognitive decline. In addition to collecting structured cognitive metrics, touchscreen interactions and motion-based responses capture fine motor slowing and attentional variability in prodromal AD. Beyond behavioral and motor signals, EEG-integrated platforms such as Cognision® quantify task-related neurophysiological activity during cognitive performance [ 43 ]. At a multimodal level, AI-based systems such as BrainSee® combine MRI-derived anatomical features with cognitive metrics to estimate the risk of MCI-to-AD conversion [ 44 ]. Although these technologies are often framed as digital screening tools, their structured behavioral and physiological data streams are relevant to digital phenotyping. Reaction time patterns, sensorimotor coordination metrics, neural signal changes, and AI-derived risk estimates constitute quantifiable components that can be tracked longitudinally as elements of individual digital phenotypes. These platforms therefore provide multidimensional data relevant to symptom monitoring. Stage-dependent digital phenotyping in Alzheimer’s disease Digital phenotyping in prodromal Alzheimer’s disease and mild cognitive impairment AD progresses through a continuum of clinical stages, from preclinical phases to MCI and, ultimately, severe dementia [ 45 – 47 ]. Digital phenotyping approaches are therefore aligned with disease stage, as the type and relevance of digital signals differ across progression. Digital monitoring strategies shift across stages of AD progression, reflecting a transition from active, high-engagement sensing approaches in earlier AD stages to increasingly passive, environment-based monitoring in later AD stages (Fig. 2 ). Accordingly, the utility of specific digital phenotyping modalities varies by clinical stage, and no single modality is uniformly optimal across the disease course (Table 2 ). AI-driven analyses of wearable-derived gait signals show diagnostic utility for cognitive impairment [ 55 ] and are being integrated into AD staging frameworks, including the CDR, to quantify symptom severity and stage transitions [ 56 , 57 ]. Despite these advances, the absence of standardized digital feature definitions and metadata protocols limit reproducibility and direct comparisons of findings. Mapping AD symptoms to smart sensor modalities Non-AI-based digital phenotyping To address the heterogeneity of AD symptoms, it is critical to identify smart sensing modalities capable of capturing fine-grained functional impairments in real-world contexts. Recent advances in digital health have enabled multimodal sensor-based phenotyping across multiple domains, including motor behavior, mobility, speech, attention, and daily function. These modalities generate domain-specific behavioral data that support longitudinal monitoring and detection of subtle functional changes not captured by episodic clinical assessment (Table 1 ). Speech and language-based digital phenotyping Spoken language reflects how cognitive impairment manifests during everyday communication, and its analysis can provide quantifiable behavioral features relevant to digital phenotyping in AD. Across both naturalistic and task-elicited speech, individuals with AD exhibit characteristic linguistic changes, including reduced lexical diversity, increased reliance on generic words or pronouns, and diminished semantic specificity [ 58 ]. Analyses of conversational speech show that AD is associated with altered distributions of nouns and verbs, consistent with progressive semantic degradation rather than isolated word-finding difficulty. Complementary findings from structured speech elicitation paradigms (i.e., picture description tasks) indicate that early-stage AD is marked by simpler syntactic constructions, reduced informativeness, and increased disfluencies compared with the speech of cognitively healthy older adults [ 59 ]. In addition to lexical–semantic changes, acoustic alterations, including slower speech rate and longer or more frequent pauses, have been reported, giving rise to multidimensional linguistic and temporal profiles of AD [ 60 ]. These convergent observations suggest that language impairment in AD is not confined to a single linguistic level but instead emerges as a distributed pattern across lexical, syntactic, and temporal dimensions of speech. Speech-based digital phenotyping shows associations with cognitive status and disease-related biomarkers in AD. Automated analyses of linguistic and acoustic features are correlated with global cognitive measures, indicating that speech-derived markers scale with overall cognitive impairment. Associations of pause-related metrics and lexical usage patterns with AD-related neuropathology, including tau burden, have also been reported [ 61 ]. Importantly, the integration of linguistic features with other biomarkers improves detection sensitivity compared with cognitive testing alone, positioning speech as a complementary digital modality [ 62 ]. Because speech can be collected repeatedly, remotely, and with minimal burden, longitudinal changes in an individual’s language provide measurable indicators of disease progression in real-world contexts. Typing, touch, and interaction dynamics of digital phenotyping Typing on physical or virtual keyboards and interacting with touchscreen devices provide measurable behavioral signals relevant to digital phenotyping in AD. Changes in keystroke dynamics, including typing speed, inter-key latency, key hold duration, and error-related behaviors, have been reported in individuals with MCI and early AD compared with cognitively unimpaired older adults. Individuals with cognitive impairment exhibit slower and more variable typing patterns, with increased hesitation and irregular input rhythms [ 63 , 64 ]. These typing-derived metrics are typically captured during naturalistic, unsupervised device use rather than during formal testing, allowing cognitive function to be inferred from habitual behavior. Fine-grained keyboard interaction patterns have been shown to differentiate early cognitive impairment from healthy aging [ 65 ]. Touchscreen interaction dynamics provide a complementary window into AD-related change by quantifying how individuals engage with smartphones and tablets in everyday contexts. Metrics such as tap latency, swipe timing, reaction time, and error frequency during routine touchscreen interactions are associated with cognitive status in older adults; specifically, slower and more variable interaction patterns are observed in those with cognitive impairment [ 66 , 67 ]. These alterations are associated with impairments in psychomotor speed, attentional control, and implicit learning, which are affected early in AD. Notably, large-scale longitudinal studies using passive smartphone and wearable data have demonstrated that changes in input behavior can track cognitive slowing over time and distinguish individuals with MCI from cognitively healthy peers [ 21 ]. Because typing and touch interactions frequently occur during daily device use, they enable longitudinal monitoring of behavioral patterns without structured testing. Accelerometer-based monitoring of motor activity for digital phenotyping Wearable accelerometers and inertial sensors quantify gait pace, rhythm, and variability during daily-life walking, providing motor-related digital measures in AD [ 68 , 69 ]. Longitudinal data indicate that slowing of usual gait speed can precede clinical diagnosis of MCI by more than a decade, suggesting that motor slowing may represent an early functional manifestation of neurodegenerative change relevant to the trajectory of AD [ 70 ]. Cross-sectional and real-world monitoring studies have shown that individuals with MCI or AD exhibit decreases in gait velocity and stride length and greater variability in step timing and cadence compared with cognitively healthy older adults, highlighting gait variability as a sensitive marker detectable outside the clinic [ 68 , 71 ]. Continuous gait monitoring has further demonstrated discriminative performance for mild AD in community-dwelling cohorts [ 69 ]. These motor patterns are interpreted in relation to cognitive vulnerability and require clinical context for attribution [ 72 ]. Accelerometer-derived activity phenotypes capture overall functional engagement through measures of total daily activity, intensity distribution, and sedentary behavior [ 73 , 74 ]. Compared with cognitively unimpaired older adults, individuals with MCI and AD accumulate fewer daily steps, spend less time in moderate-intensity activity, and exhibit longer sedentary periods [ 71 , 73 ]. Temporal organization of activity, assessed using actigraphy-derived rest–activity rhythm metrics (e.g., intra-daily variability, inter-daily stability, relative amplitude), has been associated with cognitive impairment [ 75 , 76 ]. Actigraphy-based classification studies have further suggested multivariate accelerometry features that differentiate AD from control groups [ 77 ]. In longitudinal cohorts, greater fragmentation and reduced amplitude of rest–activity rhythms have been associated with higher risk of incident MCI or dementia and with accelerated cognitive decline in AD cohorts [ 78 , 79 ]. Taken together, accelerometer-based motor, activity, and rhythm phenotypes represent complementary digital measures of functional and cognitive motor change in AD [ 80 , 81 ]. GPS-based spatial mobility in digital phenotyping GPS-based sensing quantifies spatial mobility patterns reflecting autonomy and community engagement in AD by measuring movement range and travel behavior in everyday environments [ 82 , 83 ]. Geolocation studies report that constriction of life-space, defined as reduced travel distance, smaller roaming area, and less time spent away from home, occurs among individuals with MCI and AD compared with cognitively intact older adults [ 82 , 83 ]. This constriction reflects cognitive impairments because spatial mobility depends on intact navigation and executive planning [ 84 ]. Importantly, life-space phenotypes derived from GPS data show prognostic associations with cognitive outcomes, supporting their relevance as digital markers of disease vulnerability and progression [ 83 , 85 ]. Longitudinal analyses show that progressive constriction of life-space is associated with accelerated cognitive decline and increased risk of incident neurodegenerative disease [ 85 ]. Continuous GPS monitoring also quantifies mobility routines, including outing frequency and regularity, as indicators of functional autonomy [ 82 , 83 ]. Collectively, GPS-based spatial mobility phenotypes offer a scalable approach to capturing real-world functional restriction in AD, extending digital phenotyping beyond body-centric movement signals to the broader domain of community life-space and independent living [ 84 , 85 ]. Home-based functional digital phenotyping in Alzheimer’s disease Home-based functional digital phenotyping utilizes non-wearable sensors embedded in domestic environments to quantify everyday functioning in AD [ 86 , 87 ]. These approaches rely on ambient sensing systems, including motion detectors, door and appliance sensors, bed sensors, power monitors, and computer-use logs, to continuously capture daily routines without requiring active user participation [ 86 , 87 ]. Passive monitoring of behavior in naturalistic settings generates objective and ecologically valid measures of functional activity. Early longitudinal studies demonstrated that subtle alterations in in-home activity patterns, including increased variability in movement and changes in walking speed measured passively within the home, are associated with MCI and may precede clinical diagnosis [ 88 , 89 ]. These findings establish the feasibility of environmental sensing as an approach for detecting early functional changes along the AD continuum. Home-based digital phenotyping has expanded beyond mobility to encompass a broader range of functional behaviors and daily routines. Studies of continuous monitoring of instrumental activities of daily living (IADLs), such as computer use, have shown that older adults with MCI exhibit greater variability and accelerated decline in daily computer engagement compared to cognitively normal peers [ 86 ]. Integrated ambient sensor systems that can detect changes in the timing and regularity of daily behaviors in individuals with MCI or AD are advancing mobility-focused and single-activity monitoring [ 88 , 90 ]. Home-based monitoring studies have shown that individuals with MCI or AD exhibit increased nocturnal activity, disrupted sleep, and irregular daily activity timing [ 78 , 91 ]. These alterations are associated with established vulnerabilities in circadian and homeostatic regulation in AD, suggesting that ambient sensors capture biologically meaningful disruptions in behavioral rhythms rather than nonspecific variability. The captured functional phenotypes reflect not only motor decline but also impairments in planning, attention, and temporal organization of behavior. Home-based digital phenotyping also offers clinically meaningful insights into safety, independence, and care needs, extending its value beyond simple detection of functional decline. Because daily activities typically follow stable and individualized routines, deviations from established patterns (e.g., prolonged inactivity or atypical nocturnal movement) may signal acute functional deterioration or intercurrent health events [ 87 , 92 ]. In addition to identifying these event-level abnormalities, ambient systems can derive indirect environmental markers with discriminatory value for cognitive impairment, such as household electricity consumption and appliance-use patterns [ 93 ]. However, the interpretation of home-based phenotypes requires careful contextualization. Living arrangements, caregiver support, and environmental constraints may substantially influence observed behaviors independent of cognitive status, potentially confounding inferences about disease progression [ 94 , 95 ]. In summary, home-based functional digital phenotyping complements wearable and mobile sensing by capturing real-world manifestations of cognitive decline, supporting longitudinal monitoring across the AD spectrum. Comparative strengths and limitations of digital phenotyping modalities in Alzheimer’s disease Digital phenotyping modalities capture different symptom domains and vary in their methodological reliability and suitability for clinical implementation. These differences have practical consequences: each modality offers distinct advantages while possessing specific limitations that affect real-world applicability. Speech-based measures are often highly sensitive to early cognitive change, particularly in language production and semantic processing [ 42 – 45 ], but their performance is influenced by educational level, linguistic background, and recording context, which can reduce specificity and limit comparability across populations. By contrast, wearable-derived gait and activity metrics provide objective and continuous indicators of motor and functional decline [ 27 , 38 – 41 ], but their reliability depends on consistent use. When patients do not wear the device regularly, the resulting gaps in data can distort assessments of functional decline. GPS-based mobility phenotypes quantify autonomy and life-space restriction, domains not easily assessed in clinic-based evaluations. However, mobility behavior is strongly shaped by environmental structure, transportation access, and social context, complicating causal attribution to neurodegenerative change and raising privacy concerns that affect implementation feasibility [ 46 – 49 ]. Home-based ambient sensing enables passive and longitudinal monitoring of daily routine stability, but installation requirements, housing heterogeneity, and infrastructure demands limit standardization and broad deployment across clinical sites [ 27 ]. Collectively, these contrasts indicate that no single modality provides comprehensive coverage of AD-related cognitive, motor, and functional change. Instead, each modality balances sensitivity, interpretability, burden, and implementation feasibility in different ways (Table 3 ). Clinical utility therefore depends on aligning modality selection with specific objectives (e.g., early detection, functional monitoring, autonomy assessment, or longitudinal stability tracking) rather than attempting to improve diagnostic precision by increasing data volume alone. AI-assisted phenotypic stratification AI-assisted phenotypic stratification in AD aims to distinguish AD-related cognitive impairment from normal aging and to discern clinically meaningful digital phenotypes from high-dimensional behavioral signals. Classification approaches span feature-engineered machine learning models, sequence-based neural networks, and transformer-based language architectures, depending on data structure and temporal complexity. Large-scale digital behavior modeling has shown that combining motor interaction patterns with semantic search behavior improves detection accuracy compared with using either domain alone [ 96 ]. Similar advantages are observed in speech-based phenotyping, as integrated modeling of acoustic and linguistic features yields higher classification accuracy than unimodal speech markers [ 97 ]. This improvement occurs because acoustic and linguistic features capture different aspects of cognitive impairment. Acoustic features quantify changes in speech timing and pause patterns, whereas linguistic features reflect alterations in lexical selection and syntactic organization associated with AD [ 98 , 99 ]. The greatest performance gains arise when temporally dynamic acoustic signals and lexical content are jointly modeled rather than analyzed independently. Importantly, stratification models achieve greater clinical relevance when digital features demonstrate biological plausibility and align with known AD pathology [ 61 ]. Accordingly, predictive accuracy alone is insufficient; clinically meaningful stratification requires interpretable outputs that link model-derived features to established cognitive and neuropathological constructs. AI for multimodal integration AI for multimodal integration in AD seeks to combine heterogeneous data streams, ranging from biological biomarkers to real-world behavioral measures, into unified analytic frameworks that capture complementary dimensions of neurodegeneration. Empirical studies have implemented multimodal fusion through multi-kernel machine learning models, deep representation learning (e.g., autoencoder-based architectures), graph-structured integration, and hierarchical modeling approaches, depending on data topology and scale. In neuroimaging-driven diagnosis, integrating structural MRI with PET or cerebrospinal fluid biomarkers consistently improves classification performance relative to unimodal models, demonstrating the additive value of combining anatomical and molecular markers [ 100 – 102 ]. This improvement reflects the complementary information provided by structural and molecular biomarkers, which together enhance disease discrimination relative to either modality alone. Graph-based fusion strategies model inter-modality relationships and distributed biomarker interactions, enhancing discrimination when disease signatures are network-based rather than localized [ 103 ]. Furthermore, when digital behavioral patterns are analyzed alongside clinical phenotypes, multimodal modeling can reveal latent behavioral structures that are not detectable within a single modality [ 104 ]. Similarly, joint analysis of physiological variability and ecological cognitive measures strengthens associations between real-world fluctuations and neurodegenerative processes [ 105 ]. Overall, multimodal AI frameworks enhance clinical interpretability when latent components can be mapped onto known neurobiological substrates or clinically meaningful functional domains, thereby shifting integration from performance optimization to mechanistically grounded and translationally credible phenotype construction. The strongest added predictive value emerges when functionally complementary domains are jointly modeled rather than analyzed independently. AI-assisted longitudinal phenotyping AI-assisted longitudinal phenotyping in AD analyzes repeated measurements to identify deviations from an individual’s usual behavioral or cognitive patterns, thereby enabling early detection of progression. To achieve this, temporal modeling approaches use recurrent neural networks for sequential data and mixed-effects or state-space models for structured longitudinal signals. Recurrent sequence models have demonstrated utility in forecasting future diagnostic status, cognitive decline, and structural brain changes from longitudinal ADNI-derived data, highlighting the predictive value of modeling temporal dependencies rather than relying on cross-sectional snapshots [ 106 , 107 ]. Complementing deep sequence learning, Hidden Markov Models (HMMs) applied to passive smartphone-derived behavioral data can identify latent social states and quantify dwell-time alterations across healthy and clinical populations, providing interpretable state-transition metrics that differentiate disease-related behavioral dynamics [ 108 ]. Similarly, combining contactless in-home monitoring with mixed-effects longitudinal modeling can enable the characterization of night-time physiological and behavioral trajectories to detect both gradual decline and acute perturbations relative to personalized baselines [ 91 ]. These findings indicate that AI-based longitudinal models can improve detection of early progression by capturing intra-individual variability and temporal state transitions that are not observable in cross-sectional analyses. However, the clinical value of these AI models depends on the interpretability of their temporal outputs. Clinical interpretability is enhanced when AI-generated predictions are expressed as deviation-from-baseline indices, state-transition probabilities, or trajectory slopes rather than opaque latent representations. Accordingly, effective AI-assisted longitudinal phenotyping requires individualized baselines and transparent temporal metrics that directly support clinical monitoring and decision-making. Challenges and clinical integration in AI-driven digital phenotyping for AD Technical and methodological challenges in AI-based AD phenotyping The implementation of AI-based digital phenotyping protocols for AD requires standardized sampling frequencies, harmonized feature extraction, and consistent signal normalization across diverse data acquisition platforms (e.g., smartphones, wearables, and home IoT sensors) [ 109 , 110 ]. Standardized harmonization of the outputs from different platforms facilitates replication across studies and supports clinical translation. However, temporal misalignment and data dependency present additional challenges for AI-driven multimodal modeling. Heterogeneous sensors generate asynchronous outputs, and precise synchronization of heterogeneous signals is required to ensure valid model training and interpretation. Temporal alignment is particularly important when differentiating pathological wandering from routine mobility patterns, which requires accurate alignment of GPS trajectories with wearable-derived gait features [ 49 ]. GPS traces summarize mobility over minutes, whereas accelerometers record gait cycles at sub-second intervals. If these signals are not temporally synchronized, AI models may capture spurious correlations rather than clinically meaningful patterns. Therefore, the development of robust time-aligned architecture and metadata standards is critical for clinical validity and reproducibility. After addressing synchronization issues, context-aware modeling is a critical step for enhancing the interpretability of digital phenotyping outputs. Algorithms must distinguish pathological symptoms from benign behaviors (e.g., differentiating falls from rapid sitting) by incorporating contextual information, including time of day, activity type, and location [ 49 ]. However, the implementation of context-aware models is frequently limited by the scarcity of high-quality labeled datasets, which restricts the reliability of training and validation. Federated learning and transfer learning offer potential solutions by enabling model generalization while preserving patient privacy [ 111 , 112 ]. In federated learning, raw patient data remain on local devices or institutional servers, and only model updates are shared across sites, thereby reducing the risk of exposing sensitive health information. Federated learning with differential privacy has been shown to preserve data security while enabling effective collaborative model training in medical image analysis [ 111 , 113 ]. Ethical, regulatory, and accessibility challenges in AI-based AD monitoring AI-based digital phenotyping, including computational analysis of speech, mobility, and behavioral patterns, offers substantial opportunities for advancing early detection and monitoring in AD. However, these opportunities also pose ethical and regulatory challenges regarding data governance, privacy protection, and the equitable deployment of digital health technologies. Behavioral and physiological data are continuously collected from mobile and wearable devices without standardized governance frameworks, resulting in uncertainty regarding ownership, access rights, and accountability when AI-generated predictions are incorporated into clinical decision-making [ 114 ]. Privacy-preserving computational strategies (e.g., edge computing and differential privacy) can strengthen security by minimizing raw data transfer, keeping sensitive data locally processed [ 115 ]. Continuous monitoring raises substantial privacy and consent challenges that cannot be sufficiently managed by static and one-time consent frameworks. A more adaptive alternative is dynamic consent models, which are characterized by ongoing and flexible user permissions [ 115 ]. A recent study demonstrated that a ten-year implementation of dynamic consent in the CHRIS cohort improved participant trust and engagement, providing longitudinal evidence for the feasibility of this model in large-scale digital health research [ 116 ]. However, AI-specific regulatory frameworks have not been fully developed. While existing legal standards (e.g., HIPAA and GDPR) establish baseline privacy protections, they fail to address risks related to continuous behavioral monitoring, multimodal data integration, and AI-driven decision-making. Current policies provide insufficient guidance on the secondary use of high-frequency behavioral data, raising concerns that information originally collected for clinical monitoring could be repurposed for unrelated research or commercial applications without explicit patient awareness or consent [ 114 ]. Legal frameworks also provide limited clarity on accountability when AI-generated outputs (e.g., diagnostic classifications or risk scores) are incorporated into clinical decision-making, leaving clinicians and patients uncertain regarding responsibility and liability [ 117 ]. Moreover, inconsistencies in the regulation of cross-border data-sharing across jurisdictions hinder secure international collaboration [ 118 ]. In real-world settings, limited digital access and literacy may restrict equitable implementation of AI-based AD monitoring. Older individuals with cognitive impairment frequently face difficulties using digital interfaces, limiting adherence in long-term monitoring studies [ 119 ]. For instance, a cohort study (n = 1860, mean age = 65) reported that individuals with cognitive impairment exhibited significantly reduced digital device access and literacy regardless of education level, indicating that addressing disparities in digital access is essential to ensure fair and inclusive implementation of AI-driven AD monitoring systems [ 120 ]. Approaches such as passive sensing, caregiver-assisted technologies, and simplified user interface design have been shown to enhance usability and reduce attrition, addressing these barriers [ 119 ]. In addition, effective clinical integration requires translation of AI outputs into clinician-oriented dashboards that summarize continuous predictions in interpretable formats aligned with clinical workflows. Without such interfaces, AI-generated risk scores remain difficult to operate in routine care. In summary, successful AI deployment depends not only on algorithmic performance but also on adherence, privacy safeguards, and workflow compatibility. Collectively, governance gaps, access disparities, and regulatory ambiguity highlight the need for AD-specific frameworks that clarify data rights, accountability standards, and equitable implementation requirements. Discussion Regulatory positioning of mobile-based digital phenotyping Mobile-based digital phenotyping is not currently defined as an independent regulatory category by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) or under the European CE Medical Device Regulation (MDR); instead, its regulatory status is determined by the intended use of the software [ 38 , 121 ]. In this review, digital phenotyping in AD is positioned as a longitudinal monitoring infrastructure rather than a diagnostic modality. Therefore, regulatory evaluation requires demonstration of analytical validity, measurement reliability, data integrity, and clinically interpretable outputs rather than diagnostic accuracy [ 122 ]. Given that digital phenotyping often involves highly sensitive data such as location, behavior, and usage patterns, adherence to principles of data minimization and transparent data governance is essential for ensuring both regulatory acceptability and user trust. Clinical responsibility, user acceptance, and liability considerations In clinical practice, medical decision-making remains the responsibility of the treating clinician, even when AI-enabled digital phenotyping outputs are used to support monitoring and prompt further evaluation [ 121 , 123 ]. Digital phenotyping should therefore function as an adjunctive longitudinal monitoring tool that informs clinical assessment without determining diagnostic or therapeutic actions. Successful clinical adoption of digital phenotyping in AD depends on effective integration into routine clinical workflows. Outputs that increase time burden or generate excessive alerts are unlikely to achieve sustained physician adoption. For sustained clinical use, high-frequency data must be transformed into concise, clinically meaningful, and interpretable summaries without increasing the cognitive or operational burden on clinicians [ 124 , 125 ]. Excessive dashboard complexity and frequent alerts increase cognitive and operational burden for clinicians, substantially undermining usability. Therefore, integration requires seamless linkage with electronic medical record systems, predefined alert thresholds, and user interfaces that present summary indicators aligned with existing documentation practices. For patients and caregivers, long-term adherence is primarily determined by ease of use and minimal required effort. These implementation and usability constraints—independent of algorithmic performance—often determine whether mobile-based digital phenotyping tools are adopted or abandoned in real-world clinical settings. Mobile-based measurements are inherently reliant on long-term engagement, sustained patient and caregiver participation, and usability directly influence data quality and clinical validity. When algorithmic outputs are opaque or effectively direct diagnostic or therapeutic decisions, legal and ethical liability risks increase substantially [ 126 – 128 ]. Therefore, mobile-based digital phenotyping should be consistently defined and deployed as an observational and monitoring-oriented tool rather than as a substitute for clinical diagnosis (Fig. 4 ). Fig. 4 Integration of digital phenotyping into the Alzheimer’s disease clinical pathway. Digital phenotyping enables continuous monitoring of real-world behavior prior to symptom recognition, allowing early deviations from individual baselines to be detected outside the clinic. Once symptoms are recognized, conventional screening and diagnostic evaluations are performed, with clinicians informed by longitudinal digital phenotyping data collected before and between visits. In this framework, digital phenotyping functions as a monitoring layer that complements episodic clinical assessments and supports decision-making across the diagnostic and post-diagnostic stages. In addition, protection of continuously collected behavioral data remains a fundamental requirement for broader implementation. To maintain analytical performance while minimizing the use of sensitive data such as location, behavioral traces, and usage patterns, privacy-preserving learning approaches, most notably federated learning, are emerging as viable solutions [ 129 , 130 ]. These approaches enable model training through distributed parameter updates without centralized storage of raw data, improving data protection while preserving model performance. Overall, workflow integration and privacy-preserving modeling are essential for responsibly expanding digital phenotyping as a longitudinal monitoring infrastructure rather than a diagnostic substitute. Methodological and operational limitations in real-world deployment Mobile-based digital phenotyping holds substantial clinical potential; however, several limitations must be considered for successful real-world deployment and broader adoption. First, AI models trained on earlier-stage behavioral data may lose accuracy as AD progression alters daily activity patterns. Given that model parameters are estimated from historical data, shifts in behavioral distributions over time (data drift) can reduce predictive validity if periodic recalibration is not performed. Second, AI models may yield inconsistent predictions when behavioral data are collected using devices with different hardware specifications or sensor configurations. Variations in sensor properties can lead to systematically different signal values for identical behaviors. Without device harmonization, the model may misinterpret technical variation as disease-related change, reducing reproducibility and limiting generalizability in real-world deployment. Third, label noise arising from inaccurate or temporally misaligned clinical diagnoses can distort model training and reduce predictive validity. Clinical labels derived from episodic assessments may not align with continuously collected behavioral data. When diagnostic categories do not reflect the behavioral state at the time of data capture, models may learn misleading associations, resulting in overestimated development performance and reduced robustness in real-world deployment. Finally, reduced device use among patients with worsening cognition can create systematic data gaps that distort longitudinal monitoring. Because engagement often declines as functional impairment increases, the most clinically relevant periods may be underrepresented in the dataset. As a result, AI models may underestimate AD progression or produce unstable predictions when applied in real-world settings. Standardization and longitudinal validation as prerequisites for scale Clinical implementation of mobile-based digital phenotyping in AD requires methodological standardization and longitudinal validation. Digital phenotyping data vary substantially depending on device type, operating system, sensor specifications, and individual user behavior, which limits comparability and reproducibility across studies [ 131 , 132 ]. This heterogeneity constrains both cross-study synthesis and clinical interpretability, as identical behaviors may yield non-equivalent digital features under different technical conditions. Accordingly, shared frameworks are required to ensure consistent feature definition, data structuring, and quality control. Without harmonized feature definitions and reporting standards, the accumulation of reproducible evidence remains limited. Beyond short-term observational designs, the clinical relevance of digital phenotyping will further depend on longitudinal study frameworks that emphasize within-person baseline changes over time [ 133 – 135 ]. Long-term data are crucial for distinguishing transient fluctuations from disease-related change signals, which is necessary to refine the role of digital phenotyping as an early warning and disease monitoring tool. Future directions Recurring failure modes in real-world clinical translation Real-world clinical translation of digital phenotyping in AD is constrained by four recurring implementation failures. (1) missing data that is systematic rather than random (non-wear, battery, connectivity, non-carry); (2) adoption and workflow friction (older adults, caregiver dependency, installation requirements); (3) noise and non-AD confounding (device heterogeneity, environment, comorbidities); and (4) limited interpretability and actionability (clinicians receive high-frequency streams without standardized summaries, thresholds, or linkage to decisions). These recurring failure modes provide an essential context for interpreting the current evidence base in AD digital phenotyping. Addressing these failure points requires modality-specific quality control, missingness modeling, and development of clinician-oriented reporting structures that translate digital signals into actionable summaries. Evidence gaps and priorities for AD–specific digital phenotyping A critical synthesis emerging from this review is that AD-specific evidence is currently strongest for low-burden, ubiquitous modalities (e.g., speech capture and passive digital traces) and weakest for biomarker-inspired, physiology-forward sensors (e.g., home EEG, scalable eye tracking, multi-site inertial sensing with robust daily-living validation). In addition, multimodal integration is often proposed but rarely validated longitudinally in diverse AD cohorts with reproducible pipelines. Therefore, the biggest evidence gaps are: (i) longitudinal validation across disease stages, (ii) harmonized feature definitions and preprocessing across devices, (iii) clinical interpretability standards (what constitutes meaningful change), and (iv) pragmatic deployment studies that evaluate adherence, missingness patterns, and workflow integration as primary outcomes—not afterthoughts. Conclusions Digital phenotyping enables longitudinal characterization of real-world cognitive, behavioral, and functional changes in AD that are not captured by episodic clinical assessments. Across disease stages, within-person monitoring of everyday digital signals provides sensitivity to early deviations and functional progression when interpreted relative to individualized baselines. Therefore, digital phenotyping may complement biomarker-based diagnosis and contribute to more continuous, stage-aware management of AD when positioned as a monitoring infrastructure supported by interpretable AI and integrated into clinical workflows.
