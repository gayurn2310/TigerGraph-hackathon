Glymphatic dysfunction links vascular pathology to Alzheimer’s biomarkers and cognitive decline

Background Vascular damage, including cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA) and non-amyloid cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD), has been linked to glymphatic dysfunction, which may contribute to Alzheimer’s disease (AD) pathology and cognitive decline. We investigated the associations among vascular damage, glymphatic function measured by the DTI-ALPS (Diffusion Tensor Imaging-Analysis Along the Perivascular Space) index, AD plasma biomarkers, and cognitive decline. Methods This study includes 1,249 participants recruited from Samsung Medical Center. We performed linear regression analysis to identify factors associated with the DTI-ALPS index. Further, linear regression analysis with vascular imaging markers, including CAA and CSVD summary scores, as predictors and DTI-ALPS index as an outcome was performed to investigate the effect of vascular pathology on glymphatic function. We conducted mediation analyses to investigate whether the DTI-ALPS index mediates the effect of vascular imaging markers on plasma biomarkers (phosphorylated tau 217 [p-tau 217], glial fibrillary acidic protein [GFAP], and neurofilament light chain [NFL]). Additionally, mediation analyses with the DTI-ALPS index as a predictor, each plasma biomarker as a mediator, and annual MMSE or CDR-SOB change as an outcome to investigate whether plasma biomarkers mediate the effect of the DTI-ALPS index on longitudinal cognitive decline. Results First, the DTI-ALPS index was negatively associated with both CAA ( β [95% CI] = -0.163 [-0.214, -0.112], p &lt; 0.0001) and CSVD ( β [95% CI] = -0.195 [-0.247, -0.143], p &lt; 0.0001) summary scores after controlling for age, sex, BMI status, and APOE genotype. Second, the DTI-ALPS index fully mediated the relationship between these vascular markers and p-tau 217 (CSVD summary score, indirect effect β [95% CI] = 0.016 [0.010, 0.023], p &lt; 0.001; CAA summary score, indirect effect β [95% CI] = 0.013 [0.008, 0.020], p &lt; 0.001) and GFAP (CSVD summary score, indirect effect β [95% CI] = 0.015 [0.008, 0.022], p &lt; 0.001; CAA summary score, indirect effect β [95% CI] = 0.012 [0.007, 0.019], p &lt; 0.001), while partially mediating the relationship for NFL, regardless of Aβ uptake on PET. Finally, the DTI-ALPS index was significantly associated with cognitive decline and this association was partially mediated by plasma biomarkers. Conclusions These findings highlight glymphatic dysfunction as a key mechanism linking vascular pathology with tau, inflammation and neurodegeneration, independent of Aβ uptakes. Background Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is characterized by the accumulation of amyloid-beta (Aβ) plaques and neurofibrillary tangles, which contribute to progressive cognitive decline and neurodegeneration [ 1 , 2 ]. Vascular pathologies, such as cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA) and non-amyloid cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD), have been increasingly recognized for their role in exacerbating AD progression [ 3 , 4 ]. These vascular changes may disrupt brain homeostasis, decrease clearance mechanisms, and promote the accumulation of neurotoxic proteins [ 5 ]. Plasma biomarkers such as p-tau 217, glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), and neurofilament light chain (NFL) have emerged as promising tools for monitoring AD progression [ 6 , 7 ]. P-tau 217 is closely associated with amyloid deposition and neurofibrillary tangle formation [ 8 ]. GFAP and NFL reflect neuroinflammation and neuroaxonal degeneration, respectively which are prominent processes in both AD and vascular brain injury [ 9 , 10 ]. Previous evidence, including our own prior work, indicates that vascular pathologies such as CAA and CSVD can influence tau-related, inflammatory, and neurodegenerative biomarkers independently of parenchymal Aβ pathology [ 11 , 12 ]. However, the potential mediating role of glymphatic dysfunction in these amyloid-independent associations has not been systematically investigated. Vascular damage has been proposed as an upstream factor that may influence glymphatic function, which in turn mediates the clearance of metabolic waste from the brain. The glymphatic system, a recently discovered waste clearance pathway in the brain, has been suggested as a key player in removing interstitial waste, including AD pathologies [ 13 ]. Operating along the perivascular spaces, the glymphatic system facilitates the exchange of cerebrospinal and interstitial fluid, maintaining brain homeostasis [ 14 ]. Impairments in glymphatic function have been linked to a reduced clearance of neurotoxic proteins, contributing to accumulation of AD pathologies, which accelerates AD progression [ 15 ]. The Diffusion Tensor Imaging Along the Perivascular Space (DTI-ALPS) index has been suggested as a marker to evaluate glymphatic function in vivo, with lower values indicating glymphatic dysfunction [ 16 ]. Recent research has demonstrated that reductions in the DTI-ALPS index predict increased brain atrophy and faster cognitive decline, underscoring the critical role of early glymphatic impairment in the progression of AD pathologies [ 17 ]. Similarly, a study on CSVD found that lower DTI-ALPS values were associated with greater white matter hyperintensity (WMH) volumes, enlarged perivascular spaces (EPVS), and cognitive deterioration over time [ 18 ]. While these findings underscore the connection between glymphatic dysfunction and vascular pathology, they did not fully examine how glymphatic impairment interacts with plasma biomarkers such as p-tau 217, GFAP, and NFL, which are critical for understanding the mechanisms underlying AD and vascular pathologies. Therefore, the relationship among vascular damage, glymphatic dysfunction, and these key plasma biomarkers remains unclear and warrants further investigation. In this study, we aimed to examine the relationship between vascular imaging markers, the DTI-ALPS index, and AD plasma biomarkers in a large cohort of 1,249 participants with varying stages of cognitive impairment. Specifically, we sought to understand how vascular pathologies, including CAA and CSVD, influence glymphatic function, as measured by the DTI-ALPS index, and how this dysfunction affects plasma biomarkers such as p-tau 217, GFAP, and NFL. Furthermore, we explored the role of the DTI-ALPS index in mediating the effects of vascular damage on these biomarkers and cognitive decline. By elucidating the links among vascular pathology, glymphatic dysfunction, and AD biomarkers, our study provides new insights into disease mechanisms and potential therapeutic targets for both vascular and neurodegenerative processes in AD. Methods Study population Participants were recruited from Samsung Medical Center between 2016 and 2023 (flow chart of study population is shown in supplementary Fig. 1). We enrolled 1,249 participants aged ≥ 45 years who underwent Aβ PET, brain MRI including diffusion tensor image (DTI) and plasma AD biomarkers (262 with cognitively unimpaired [CU], 489 with mild cognitive impairment [MCI], 352 with dementia of the Alzheimer’s type [DAT], and 146 with subcortical vascular cognitive impairment [SVCI]). All participants with CU status satisfied the following criteria: (1) no medical history that was likely to affect cognitive function based on Christensen’s health screening criteria; [ 19 ] (2) no objective cognitive impairment in any cognitive domain on a comprehensive neuropsychological test battery (above at least − 1.0 SD of age-adjusted norms on any cognitive test); and (3) independence in activities of daily living. All participants with amnestic MCI (aMCI) satisfied the criteria for aMCI with the following modifications: [ 20 , 21 ] (1) subjective cognitive complaints by the participants or caregivers; (2) objective memory impairment below − 1·0 SD on verbal or visual memory tests, (3) no significant impairment in activities of daily living, and (4) non-demented status. All participants with DAT satisfied the diagnostic criteria of the National Institute on Aging and Alzheimer’s Association (NIA-AA). All participants met the following criteria for SVCI diagnosis: (1) a subjective cognitive complaint from either the patient or caregiver; (2) objective cognitive impairment below the 16th percentile in any domain, including attention, language, visuospatial, memory, and frontal/executive functions, on detailed neuropsychological tests; (3) significant ischemia on brain MRI, defined as periventricular WMH ≥ 10 mm and deep WMH ≥ 25 mm, modified from the Fazekas ischemia criteria, as described in previous studies [ 2 ]; and (4) focal neurologic symptoms or signs. We excluded participants with structural lesions including territorial infarction, lobar hemorrhage, brain tumors, WMH due to demyelinating disorders, vasculitis, leukodystrophy, radiation injury, and hydrocephalus on MRI. Additionally, participants with abnormal laboratory results for complete blood count, electrolyte, vitamin B12, folate levels, syphilis serology, and liver/kidney/thyroid function tests were excluded. Aβ PET acquisition and quantification using centiloid values All the participants underwent Aβ PET ( 18 F-florbetaben [FBB] PET or 18 F-flutemetamol [FMM] PET using a Discovery STe PET/CT scanner (GE Medical Systems, Milwaukee, WI, USA). For 18 F-florbetaben PET or 18 F-flutemetamol PET, a 20 min emission PET scan in dynamic mode (consisting of 4 × 5 min frames) was performed 90 min after injection of a mean dose of 311.5 MBq 18 F-florbetaben or 197.7 MBq 18 F-flutemetamol, respectively. Three-dimensional PET images were reconstructed in a 128 × 128 × 48 matrix with 2 mm × 2 mm × 3·27 mm voxel size using the ordered-subsets expectation maximization algorithm ( 18 F-florbetaben, iteration = 4 and subset = 20; 18 F-flutemetamol, iteration = 4 and subset = 20). We used the Klunk Centiloid (CL) scale, which has been increasingly used in various cohort studies and clinical trials, to harmonize the quantitative analysis of Aβ PET across different ligands [ 22 – 25 ]. Data processing was conducted utilizing SPM version 5 in Matlab 6.5 (Mathworks, Natick, MA) for PiB PET images and SPM8 in Matlab 2014b (Mathworks, Natick, MA, USA) for FBB/FMM PET images. The acquired PET images were co-registered with the individual MRI scans. Subsequently, the native PET images were normalized to the Montreal Neurological Institute-152 template using a transformation matrix. After normalization, the brain was segmented into 116 grey matter regions using an automated anatomical labeling (AAL) atlas. The whole cerebellum was used as a reference region to calculate the standardized uptake value ratio (SUVR) using regional masks obtained from the GAAIN website ( http://www.GAAIN.org ). Thereafter, the global cortical SUVR was computed as the volume-weighted mean of the 56 cortical regions in the AAL template. To quantify the CL scale, we calculated the FBB and FMM CL standard values using the CL transformation equation derived from previous studies on FBB (FBB CL standard = 153.4 × FBB SUVR standard − 154.9) [ 26 ] and FMM (FMM CL standard = 121.42 × FMM SUVR standard − 121.16) [ 27 ]. Aβ PET scans were quantified using the Klunk CL scale, as analyzed by the BeauBrain Amylo software. Aβ positivity was defined as a CL value ≥ 20, which has been widely adopted as a threshold in previous studies. Participants with CL values below 20 were classified as Aβ -negative, while those with values equal to or greater than 20 were classified as Aβ -positive. DTI MRI acquisition and calculation of DTI-ALPS index Noncontrast MRI scans were performed using a 3T scanner (Achieva, Ingenia and Ingenia CX, Philips) with a 32-channel SENSE head coil (Philips Healthcare). The cognitive impairment dedicated MRI sequences used at our institution include 3D T1-weighted imaging, axial thin-cut fluid-attenuated inversion recovery imaging, axial diffusion MRI, axial T2-weighted imaging, axial T2*-weighted imaging, and diffusion weighted imaging. Among them, the diffusion MRI was utilized for the DTI-ALPS. Diffusion MRI was performed based on a single-shot echo-planar imaging sequence in the axial plane with b-values of 0 and 600 s/mm2. We applied 45 directions of motion probing gradients, and other imaging parameters for the diffusion MRI were as follows: repetition time (TR), 9300 ms; echo time (TE), 91 ms; section thickness, 2 mm; flip angle, 90°; field-of-view, 220 × 220 mm2; acquisition matrix, 128 × 128; scan time, 7 min and 26 s. The DTI-ALPS index was calculated by measuring diffusivity along the x, y, and z axes which highlighted water diffusion along the perivascular space. Briefly, anatomical 3D T1-weighted images were preprocessed in the following order: bias field correction using ANTs N4 algorithm, registration-based brain extraction, and registration to the MNI template. Diffusion MRI data were first denoised by Marcenko-Pastur PCA algorithm [ 28 ]. Next, b = 0 image was used for brain extraction to get brain mask using FSL BET tool [ 29 ]. Noise-removed diffusion MRI data was corrected for eddy-current induced distortions and subject movements using FSL eddy tool [ 30 ]. However, it should be noted that we were not able to correct susceptibility-induced geometric distortion as phase-encoding reversed DTI data was not available. DTI model was fitted to the preprocessed data using python library for the analysis of diffusion MRI (DIPY, https://dipy.org/ ) [ 31 ], and maps for fractional anisotropy (FA), mean diffusivity (MD), axial and radial diffusivity (AD and RD) were generated from the eigenvalues of the tensor. Diffusion MRI were then registered to T1-weighed image resampled to 1.5 mm isotropic resolution followed by registration to MNI 2 mm template. This approach merged the affine transformation matrix for DTI to T1-weighted registration with the non-linear transformation matrix for T1-weighted to MNI template registration, allowing direct DTI to MNI template registration. The Symmetric Normalization method in ANTs was utilized, with B-spline interpolation applied. To calculate the DTI-ALPS index, we adopted the method for modified DTI-ALPS (mDTI-ALPS) index for automatic region-of-interest (ROIs) placement [ 32 ]. We placed the 5-mm diameter spherical ROIs on the bilateral projection fibers and association fibers on the color-coded FA map calculated in the MNI space. The coordinates of the centers of the left and right ROIs were (24, − 12, 24) and (− 28, − 12, 24) in projection fibers, respectively, and (36, − 12, 24) and (− 40, − 12, 24) in association fibers, respectively. We measured the mean diffusivities along x-axis (Dx), y-axis (Dy) and z-axis (Dz) in the ROIs of the projection (Dxproj, Dyproj, and Dzproj) and association fibers (Dxassoc, Dyassoc, and Dzassoc). Finally, mDTI-ALPS index was calculated using the following equation: [(Dxproj + Dxassoc) / (Dyproj + Dzassoc)] [ 16 ]. Visual assessment of CAA and vascular imaging markers on MRI Four experienced neurologists blinded to patient information visually assessed the CAA and vascular imaging markers on MRI in line with STRIVE criteria [ 33 ]. Specifically, the number of microbleeds (MBs) was evaluated using the following criteria: ≤10 mm in diameter on 20 axial slices of T2 GRE MRI [ 34 ]. The distribution of MBs was classified as lobar or deep regions according to the criteria proposed by Gregoire et al. [ 35 ]. The presence of cSS was defined as linear chronic blood residues in the superficial layers of the cerebral cortex [ 36 ]. WMH burdens in the deep subcortical (D) and periventricular (P) regions in FLAIR images were evaluated according to the modified Fazekas scale as described in previous literature [ 2 ]. The presence of WMH was defined as severe dependent on WMH severity. EPVS were rated on T2-weighted images according to a previously validated 4-point visual rating scale in the BG and CSO: 0 (no EPVS), 1 (≤ 10 EPVSs), 2 (11–20 EPVSs), 3 (21–40 EPVSs), and 4 (&gt; 40 EPVSs) [ 37 ]. The presence of EPVS was defined as EPVS points of 3 and 4. The number of lacunes was evaluated according to the consensus criteria proposed by Wardlaw et al. [ 38 ]: small lesions (≤ 15 mm and ≥ 3 mm in diameter) with low signal intensity on T1-weighted images, high signal intensity on T2-weighted images, and perilesional halo on 80 axial slices of FLAIR images. We calculated CAA and CSVD summary scores using these vascular imaging markers by modification of previously reported rating systems [ 39 , 40 ]. Specifically, CAA summary score (0–5) awards 1 point each for lobar MBs (if 2–4), 2 points for lobar MBs (if ≥ 5), and 1 point for cSS (if ≥ 1), CSO-EPVS (if ≥ 3), and presence of severe WMH, while CSVD summary score (0–4) wards 1 point each for lacunes (if ≥ 1), deep MBs (if ≥ 1), BG-EPVS (if ≥ 3), and presence of severe WMH. Plasma collection and processing We obtained 8 mL of blood from each participant and placed in a 0.5 M EDTA-containing tube and mixed it for 5 min. Plasma was extracted from the blood sample after a 10-min centrifuge (1300 g) and dispensed into 5 or 10 vials at a volume of 0.3 mL each. All plasma samples were kept frozen at -75 °C until analysis. This process complied with the manual for human resource collection and registration of the National Biobank of the Republic of Korea. The median interval between plasma collection and Aβ PET scans was 4 days (interquartile range [IQR], 0–69 days). Plasma samples were shipped in a frozen state at -70 degrees to the Department of Psychiatry and Neurochemistry, University of Gothenburg for analysis. Plasma samples were thawed on wet ice, centrifuged at 500× g for 5 min at 4 °C. Plasma GFAP and NFL were quantified using the commercial Neurology 4-Plex E kit (Quanterix). Plasma p-tau 217 was analyzed using an immunoassay on the Meso Scale Discovery (MSD) platform by Lilly Research Laboratories. Longitudinal cognitive evaluation Of the 1,249 participants, 1,032 underwent follow-up Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) and 900 underwent follow-up Clinical Dementia Rating scale-Sum of Boxes (CDR-SOB) assessments. The mean follow-up period (standard deviation [SD]) was 2.8 (2.5) years for MMSE and 2.7 (2.6) years for CDR-SOB, and the mean number of MMSE and CDR assessments (SD) were 3.9 (2.5) and 3.5 (2.0). Annual changes in MMSE and CDR-SOB scores were calculated using linear regressions. Statistical analyses Clinical characteristics of the study participants are presented as mean ± standard deviation for continuous variables and as frequency (%) for categorical variables. The level of p-tau 217, GFAP, and NFL were transformed to log-transformed to satisfy the assumption of linearity. To identify the factors associated with the DTI-ALPS index, we performed a linear regression analysis with age, sex, BMI status, APOE genotype and CL scale as predictors and the DTI-ALPS index as the outcome. To investigate the effect of vascular imaging markers on the DTI-ALPS index, we performed a linear regression analysis with CAA summary score, lobar MBs, cSS, CSO-EPVS, WMH, CSVD summary score, lacunes, deep MBs, and BG-EPVS as predictors and the DTI-ALPS index as an outcome after controlling for age, sex, BMI status, APOE genotype, hypertension, and diabetes. To account for multiple comparisons, a Bonferroni correction was applied, yielding an adjusted significance threshold of α = 0.05/9. To investigate the effect of DTI-ALPS index on plasma biomarkers, we performed a linear regression analysis with the DTI-ALPS index as a predictor and each plasma biomarker as an outcome after controlling for age, sex, BMI status, APOE genotype, hypertension, and diabetes. To account for multiple comparisons, a Bonferroni correction was applied, yielding an adjusted significance threshold of α = 0.05/3. To investigate whether the DTI-ALPS index mediates the effect of vascular imaging markers on plasma biomarkers, we conducted mediation analyses with CAA or CSVD summary score as a predictor, the DTI-ALPS index as a mediator, and each plasma biomarker as an outcome after controlling for age, sex, BMI status, APOE genotype, hypertension, and diabetes mellitus (Model 1). Although the CAA and CSVD scores are ordinal variables, mediation analysis can still be applied using continuous or ordinal predictors. We treated these scores as ordinal variables with underlying continuous properties, which is a common approach in epidemiological and neuroimaging studies, particularly when the number of categories is relatively large. This method has been previously applied in similar contexts [ 41 ]. To determine whether these associations could be affected by Aβ uptakes, we added the CL scales to the covariates in the mediation analyses (Model 2). Additionally, to examine whether a similar trend was observed in ADCI alone, we conducted the same mediation analyses after controlling for age, sex, BMI status, APOE genotype, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and CL as sensitivity analyses, excluding SVCI. Bootstrapping was used to verify the significance of the indirect effects. To determine whether each plasma biomarker (p-tau 217, GFAP or NFL) mediated the effect of the DTI-ALPS index on longitudinal clinical progression, we conducted mediation analyses with the DTI-ALPS index as a predictor, each plasma biomarker as a mediator, and annual MMSE change or annual CDR-SOB change as the outcome after controlling for age, sex, years of education, BMI status, APOE genotype, hypertension, diabetes, and CL value. Additionally, to examine whether a similar trend was observed in ADCI alone, we conducted the same mediation analyses after controlling for age, sex, BMI status, APOE genotype, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and CL as sensitivity analyses, excluding SVCI. For the mediation analysis, three plasma biomarkers were included. To adjust for multiple comparisons, a Bonferroni correction was applied, resulting in an adjusted significance threshold of α = 0.05/3. All reported p -values were two-sided and the significance levels were set as described above for each analysis. All analyses were performed using the R 4.3.2 (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Discussion In this study, we explored the relationship between vascular imaging markers, the DTI-ALPS index, and plasma biomarkers in 1,249 participants with varying stages of cognitive impairment. Our major findings are as follows. First, the DTI-ALPS index was negatively associated with the CAA and CSVD markers. Second, the DTI-ALPS index fully mediated the relationship between these vascular markers and p-tau 217 and GFAP, while partially mediating the relationship for NFL, regardless of Aβ uptake on PET. Finally, the DTI-ALPS index was significantly associated with cognitive decline and this association was partially mediated by plasma biomarkers. Taken together, our findings highlight the role of glymphatic dysfunction in linking vascular pathology with both AD biomarkers and cognitive decline, thus providing further insights into the disease mechanisms. Older age, male sex, and obesity were significantly associated with a lower DTI-ALPS index, indicating reduced glymphatic function. These findings are consistent with those of previous studies demonstrating that aging is associated with decreased glymphatic clearance, possibly due to age-related changes in the perivascular space integrity and vascular function [ 42 ]. Similar trends have been observed in obesity, where increased vascular inflammation and metabolic dysfunction are thought to impair glymphatic function [ 43 ]. Conversely, our study did not find a significant association between the APOE genotype (e2 and e4 carriers) and the DTI-ALPS index, which aligns with some research suggesting that the primary impact of APOE4 may be more related to amyloid processing rather than to glymphatic clearance directly [ 44 ]. Our first major finding was that the DTI-ALPS index was significantly and negatively associated with the CAA and CSVD markers. In terms of CAA markers, the number of lobar MBs, presence of CAA and CAA summary score were associated with a decreased DTI-ALPS index, indicating that amyloid-related vascular damage impaired glymphatic function. CSVD markers such as WMH, lacunes, deep MBs, and BG-EPVS were also significantly associated with lower DTI-ALPS index values. This suggests that glymphatic dysfunction is closely associated with both amyloid-related and non-amyloid vascular pathologies. Notably, markers such as cSS and CSO-EPVS were not significantly associated with the DTI-ALPS index, indicating that the effects of vascular damage on glymphatic function may vary depending on the specific type and location of the pathology. These findings highlight the critical roles played by different types of vascular pathologies play in impairing glymphatic function. Our second major finding was that the DTI-ALPS index mediated the relationship between CAA and CSVD summary scores and plasma biomarkers, including p-tau 217, GFAP, and NFL, linking glymphatic dysfunction to the pathophysiological processes of AD throughout its progression. This suggests that vascular pathology, represented by CAA and CSVD summary scores, impacts glymphatic clearance, which is reflected in the accumulation of key AD biomarkers. Given that p-tau 217 is strongly associated with both Aβ deposition and tau pathology, [ 8 ] our findings imply that the DTI-ALPS index reflects the clearance of soluble forms of Aβ and tau. Additionally, the DTI-ALPS index’s association with downstream markers like GFAP and NFL suggests a broader role in neuroinflammatory and neurodegenerative processes. Importantly, previous evidence has shown that lobar CMBs and the presence of CAA are linked to tau pathology, neuroinflammation, and neurodegeneration independently of parenchymal amyloid burden. Such vascular insults may alter vessel wall integrity and perivascular space morphology, thereby impairing glymphatic transport. Given that soluble tau, GFAP, and NFL are more readily cleared through interstitial fluid pathways, these structural changes could facilitate their accumulation via reduced glymphatic outflow. This provides a plausible mechanistic basis for the mediation effect observed in our study, where vascular pathologies appear to promote tau accumulation and other downstream processes through glymphatic dysfunction. Interestingly, the DTI-ALPS index showed no association with Aβ uptake on PET, suggesting that glymphatic clearance may preferentially affect soluble biomarkers over insoluble Aβ aggregates; indeed, the association between DTI-ALPS and Aβ PET uptake remains a subject of ongoing debate [ 45 – 47 ]. The glymphatic system is thought to be more efficient at clearing smaller, soluble molecules such as p-tau217, which can diffuse more readily through interstitial spaces, whereas larger, aggregated forms of Aβ are less accessible and may rely on alternative clearance mechanisms, including microglial activity or blood-brain barrier transport [ 48 , 49 ]. Consistent with this, DTI-ALPS fully mediated the association between vascular markers (CAA and CSVD scores) and soluble plasma biomarkers, but not Aβ PET uptake, highlighting a potential mechanistic pathway by which vascular pathology accelerates AD progression through impaired glymphatic clearance of soluble proteins. These findings underscore the clinical importance of addressing vascular contributions to glymphatic dysfunction in order to slow the accumulation of pathogenic biomarkers. Our final major finding was that the DTI-ALPS index was significantly associated with cognitive decline, and that this association was partially mediated by plasma biomarkers, including p-tau 217, GFAP, and NFL. Specifically, while impaired glymphatic function contributes to the accumulation of toxic tau and inflammation related proteins, which in turn drive neurodegeneration and cognitive impairment, the DTI-ALPS index also has a direct effect on cognitive decline that is not entirely explained by these biomarkers. Unlike previous studies, which primarily focused on the direct effects of glymphatic dysfunction on cognition, our study revealed a more nuanced relationship in which glymphatic impairment affects cognitive decline through both biomarker mediation and independent mechanisms. This distinction suggests that glymphatic dysfunction may accelerate neurodegeneration by disrupting protein clearance and contributing to broader brain homeostasis issues, such as impaired waste clearance and neuroinflammation. Previous animal studies support this finding, showing that glymphatic failure can lead to the accumulation of interstitial fluid and toxic proteins, that disrupt neuronal function and cognitive performance [ 50 ]. Furthermore, the observed direct effect of the DTI-ALPS index on cognition may point to the involvement of vascular and metabolic factors linked to glymphatic impairment, which could further exacerbate brain dysfunction beyond protein aggregation. While SVCI represents a distinct clinical and pathological subtype, these patients frequently exhibit coexisting AD-related pathology, as evidenced by a higher prevalence of amyloid positivity compared to cognitively unimpaired individuals in older age groups (41.5% vs. 27.4%) [ 51 ]. Including SVCI thus allowed us to capture the full spectrum of overlapping vascular and AD pathologies. Importantly, even after excluding SVCI patients, the mediation effects of the DTI-ALPS index between vascular scores and plasma biomarkers remained consistent, reinforcing the robustness of our findings. The key strength of our study is the large, well-characterized cohort with comprehensive imaging and biomarker data, which allowed for a robust analysis of the role of glymphatic system in cognitive decline. However, this study had several limitations to consider. First, the cross-sectional nature of the study limited our ability to establish causality between glymphatic dysfunction and cognitive decline. Second, although the DTI-ALPS index may reflect SVD-related tissue changes rather than glymphatic function alone [ 52 ], especially in CAA and SVD. Minimal differences in ALPS values between patients with and without WMHs suggest that WMHs may not significantly impact its validity [ 53 ]. Further studies are needed to confirm the specific contributions of glymphatic function versus small vessel diseases. Third, because CAA and CSVD scores are ordinal rather than continuous variables, the interpretation of mediation effects should be made with caution, as the linearity assumption may not be fully met. Fourth, MRI scans were acquired using different scanners but with identical sequence parameters and spatial resolutions across all systems (3T Philips Achieva, Ingenia, and Ingenia CX), minimizing potential variability in CSVD visual assessments. Another limitation is that the DTI-ALPS index was computed from diffusion data acquired at b = 600 s/mm² instead of the more commonly used 1000 s/mm². The choice of b = 600 s/mm² balanced SNR, motion robustness, and scan time in this elderly cohort. Although lower b-values may increase CSF contamination, our denoising, eddy/motion correction, and atlas-based ROI placement minimized this risk, and identical parameters across all participants preserved internal consistency. Finally, the study population was predominantly of a single ethnicity, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to more diverse populations. Nevertheless, despite these limitations, our study provides important evidence for the role of the glymphatic system in linking vascular pathology with AD biomarkers and cognitive decline, contributing valuable insights into disease mechanisms and potential therapeutic targets. In conclusion, our study demonstrated that glymphatic dysfunction plays a critical role in linking vascular pathology with AD biomarkers and cognitive decline. These findings provide novel insights into the mechanisms underlying AD progression and suggest that targeting glymphatic function may be a potential therapeutic approach. Despite certain limitations, our research highlights the importance of the glymphatic system in both vascular and neurodegenerative pathways. Supplementary Information Supplementary Material 1.
