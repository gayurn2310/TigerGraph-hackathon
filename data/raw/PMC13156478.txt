Circulating Sphingomyelins Correlate With Plasma T‐Tau in Cognitively Unimpaired Older Adults at Risk of Developing Alzheimer's Disease

ABSTRACT Alterations in plasma sphingomyelin (SM) levels have been reported in Alzheimer's disease (AD), pointing to disturbances in lipid metabolism that may contribute to disease pathogenesis. Neuronal damage in early AD triggers tau release into central and peripheral systems. Despite influence from peripheral contributions, alterations in plasma total‐tau (T‐tau) remain valuable in indicating AD‐related neurodegeneration. Investigating relationships between SM metabolism and tau release during preclinical AD may uncover important biochemical processes and support advancing early non‐invasive detection and treatment approaches. This cross‐sectional study investigated cognitively unimpaired (CU) older adults from the KARVIAH cohort, grouped by cortical amyloid‐β (Aβ) status through positron emission tomography (PET) imaging (CU Aβ− and CU Aβ+) and utilised a Biocrates‐targeted metabolomic platform and Single‐molecule array (Simoa) technology to quantify plasma levels of SMs and T‐tau, respectively. Associations between circulating SMs and T‐tau were examined within each group, with T‐tau‐associated SMs further evaluated for their association with cognitive performance and cortical Aβ burden and their potential to discriminate CU Aβ+ from CU Aβ− individuals. Significant positive correlations were observed between SMs and T‐tau levels exclusively in CU Aβ+ individuals, suggesting connections between SM‐mediated biochemical pathways and tau release from early neurodegeneration in preclinical AD. Lower SM levels were associated with weaker working memory and executive function, as well as poorer global cognition, indicating their potential predictive value for weaker cognitive performance. Moreover, SMs were also inversely associated with cortical Aβ load in CU Aβ+ individuals, possibly reflecting early SM‐mediated neuroprotective responses against AD pathogenesis. Receiver operating characteristic analysis further revealed the significant potential of the SM panel in distinguishing cortical PET‐Aβ status and enhancing the predictive performance of plasma T‐tau in CU individuals. Therefore, circulating T‐tau‐associated SMs may serve as promising early biomarkers of lipid‐mediated processes in CU older adults with cortical amyloid pathology and tau‐related neurodegeneration.

Identifying early peripheral biomarkers that reflect underlying neurodegenerative processes is critical for enhancing the diagnosis, risk stratification and monitoring of AD. Numerous studies highlight the critical role of lipid metabolism in AD, with increasing evidence pointing toward sphingomyelins (SMs) as significant modulators of neuroinflammation, neuronal membrane integrity and Aβ aggregation (Chan et al. 2012 ; Grimm et al. 2005 ; Han et al. 2011 ; Haughey et al. 2010 ). SMs, the predominant sphingolipids, are highly enriched in neuronal membranes and lipid rafts, where they play essential roles in maintaining membrane architecture, facilitating synaptic function, regulating signal transduction, supporting cellular homeostasis and preserving the integrity and functionality of the myelin sheath (Breslow and Weissman 2010 ; Don et al. 2014 ; Jiang and Li 2022 ). In a longitudinal study, Mielke et al. ( 2011 ) demonstrated that alterations in plasma SM levels were associated with cognitive decline in AD individuals, with higher concentrations of specific SM species significantly correlated with less cognitive impairment and a slower progression of clinical symptoms. Further, several key studies have supported these findings by showing that dysregulated SM metabolism contributes to neuronal vulnerability, inflammation and amyloid plaque formation—central features of AD pathogenesis (Grimm et al. 2005 ; Han et al. 2011 ; Haughey et al. 2010 ; Jana et al. 2008 ; Xing et al. 2023 ). Thus, circulating SMs represent a promising area for further investigation and potential therapeutic intervention strategies targeting SM‐mediated pathways in cognitively unimpaired older adults. Concurrently, plasma total tau (T‐tau) has gained interest as a peripheral biomarker reflecting tau‐related neuronal damage and neurodegeneration (Cavedo et al. 2020 ; Dage et al. 2016 ; Holper et al. 2022 ). Although cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) measures of tau are well established in AD diagnostics, blood‐based tau assays have attracted attention due to their accessibility and potential utility in large‐scale screening. Plasma T‐tau levels typically remain low under cognitively unimpaired conditions; for instance, Chen et al. ( 2023 ) reported that plasma T‐tau levels range from 0.20 to 3.12 pg/mL in healthy elderly Chinese individuals. Nevertheless, significantly elevated T‐tau levels have been observed in individuals with AD compared to those with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and healthy controls (Mattsson et al. 2016 ; Mielke et al. 2017 , 2018 ; Zetterberg et al. 2013 ). Additionally, although multiple studies have consistently reported plasma T‐tau as an indicator of neurodegeneration and cognitive impairment, substantial overlap in T‐tau levels among diagnostic groups limits its specificity as an early or standalone diagnostic biomarker for AD (Dage et al. 2016 ; Mattsson et al. 2016 ; Mielke et al. 2017 , 2018 ; Verde 2022 ; Zetterberg et al. 2013 ). Furthermore, Fossati et al. ( 2019 ) reported that incorporating plasma T‐tau to CSF T‐tau or phosphorylated tau (P‐tau) significantly enhanced their diagnostic accuracy for AD, suggesting that plasma T‐tau may serve as a valuable complementary biomarker in multimodal diagnostic frameworks. Therefore, exploring the interplay between plasma SMs and T‐tau concentrations in cognitively unimpaired older adults may provide novel insights into the biochemical pathways involved in the earliest stages of AD pathogenesis and help identify lipid‐related mechanisms influencing or being influenced by tau‐related early neurodegeneration. Such findings could advance the development of blood‐based multimodal biomarker panels for preclinical AD detection and pave the way for personalised therapeutic interventions targeting lipid metabolism. The present study aimed to investigate the associations between circulating SMs and T‐tau levels in cognitively unimpaired (CU) older adults, stratified by the cortical Aβ deposition as determined by PET imaging—to explore whether alterations in SM metabolism are linked to early tau release, potentially before the onset of clinical symptoms. In this study, cross‐sectional analyses were conducted separately in individuals with negative (CU Aβ−) and positive (CU Aβ+) cortical PET‐Aβ status to examine group‐specific associations between SMs and T‐tau levels. Further, the T‐tau‐associated SMs were examined for associations with cognitive performance and the extent of cortical amyloid burden to assess their potential clinical significance. Furthermore, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was conducted to evaluate the diagnostic potential of a panel of T‐tau‐associated SMs in classifying cortical PET‐Aβ positivity, as well as to investigate whether this SM panel could improve the discriminative accuracy of plasma T‐tau in identifying early cortical Aβ accumulation. 2 Methods 2.1 Participants The study cohort was sourced from the Kerr Anglican Retirement Village Initiative in Ageing Health (KARVIAH) population, consisting of residents from Anglicare facilities in New South Wales (NSW), Australia. Out of 200 initial volunteers, 134 participants satisfied the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, as specified previously (Chatterjee et al. 2019 ; Sharmin et al. 2025 ). The primary assessment for the absence of dementia or cognitive impairment was conducted using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) score of 26 or above. For individuals with scores between 18 and 25, further assessments were conducted by the study neuropsychologist, taking into account age and educational background in line with Rossetti et al. ( 2011 ). A total of 105 individuals proceeded to undergo comprehensive neuropsychological testing, brain imaging and blood sample collection. Of these, 100 participants who achieved an MMSE score of 26 or higher (Folstein et al. 1975 ) were confirmed as cognitively unimpaired and included in the final analysis. All research participants provided written informed consent. Ethical approvals were granted by the Bellberry Human Research Ethics Committee (Approval No. 2012‐09‐1086) and Macquarie University Human Research Ethics Committee (Approval No. 520231431450224). This study was not pre‐registered, and all eligible individuals who passed the screening process were included. 2.2 Blood Sample Collection, APOE Allele Analysis and Quantification of Plasma T‐Tau Blood samples (80 mL) were collected from all participants in the morning following an overnight fast of at least 10 h. The samples were then processed and stored at −80°C, following the protocol outlined by Goozee et al. ( 2018 ). APOE allele analysis was conducted using genomic DNA purified from 0.5 mL of whole blood, employing the TaqMan SNP genotyping technique as previously described (Goozee et al. 2018 ). Plasma T‐tau levels were measured using the Single‐molecule array (Simoa) platform employing the Neurology 4‐Plex A (QTX‐102153, Quanterix, Billerica, Massachusetts, United States) kit, which simultaneously measures glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), neurofilament light chain (NFL) and ubiquitin carboxy‐terminal hydrolase L1 (UCH‐L1) (Chatterjee et al. 2022 ). All 100 participants were included in this cross‐sectional analysis, and all assays included duplicate runs for calibrators, controls and participant samples. The lowest limit of quantification (LLOQ) for the plasma T‐tau measurement was 0.053 pg/mL, with an average coefficient of variation (CV) of 7.48%. The other analytes, which were successfully quantified but not included in the present investigation, comprised GFAP (LLOQ: 0.467 pg/mL; average %CV: 2.72%) and NFL (LLOQ: 0.241 pg/mL; average %CV: 3.65%). In contrast, UCH‐L1 values exceeded the acceptable variability threshold (&gt; 20% CV) and, therefore, failed quality control (Chatterjee et al. 2022 ). 2.3 Quantification of Plasma Sphingomyelins To quantify plasma sphingomyelin (SM) levels, targeted metabolomic profiling was conducted on the eligible KARVIAH samples using the AbsoluteIDQ p400 HR kit (Biocrates Life Sciences AG) via flow injection analysis‐high resolution accurate mass‐mass spectrometry (FIA‐HRAM‐MS). In this approach, the autosampler was directly introduced into the ion source of the high‐resolution accurate mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific Q Exactive Plus), enabling direct sample infusion without chromatographic separation. In accordance with the manufacturer's guidelines, all samples were de‐identified, randomised and processed in a blinded manner prior to the analysis. Test samples were analysed in singlicate, and to ensure analytical consistency, five quality control level 2 (QC2) samples provided by the manufacturer were included per plate (positioned every 19–20 test samples). These QC2 samples enabled the normalisation of intra‐ and interplate variability, as previously described in Chatterjee et al. ( 2021 ) and Sharmin et al. ( 2025 ). While the kit can target up to 31 SM species, those with more than 40% of values falling below the lower limit of detection (&lt; LOD) were excluded from the study, consistent with Toledo et al. ( 2017 ), resulting in 26 SM species being retained for subsequent investigation, aligning with findings from Thompson et al. ( 2019 ). 2.4 Brain Imaging Assessments The study participants underwent brain imaging assessments at Macquarie Medical Imaging (NSW, Australia), including positron emission tomography (PET) with 18 F‐florbetaben (FBB) to assess cortical Aβ burden (Goozee et al. 2018 ; Sabri et al. 2015 ) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) to evaluate hippocampal volume (Goozee et al. 2018 ; Schuff et al. 2009 ), all within a three‐month window of blood sample collection. The FBB‐PET scans consisted of a 20‐min static acquisition (4 × 5‐min dynamic frames), initiated 50 min following intravenous FBB administration. Cortical Aβ load was quantified using CapAIBL image processing software, which calculated the mean standard uptake value ratio (SUVR) across key neocortical regions—specifically the frontal, superior parietal, lateral temporal, lateral occipital and anterior and posterior cingulate cortices—following protocols outlined by Bourgeat et al. ( 2015 ) and Zhou et al. ( 2014 ). An SUVR threshold of 1.35 was applied to categorise participants: CU individuals with SUVR &lt; 1.35 were considered PET‐Aβ negative (CU Aβ–), while those with SUVR ≥ 1.35 were classified as PET‐Aβ positive (CU Aβ+). MRI scans were acquired using a 3‐Tesla General Electric scanner (Model 750 W), as detailed by Goozee et al. ( 2018 ). T1‐weighted MRI data were normalised for total intracranial volume—comprising grey matter, white matter and cerebrospinal fluid—prior to calculating hippocampal volumes. 2.5 Neuropsychological Assessments Study participants completed a comprehensive battery of neuropsychological assessments, as detailed previously in Chatterjee et al. ( 2018 ) and Sharmin et al. ( 2025 ). To derive summary cognitive metrics, composite scores were calculated for specific domains such as (a) the verbal and visual episodic memory, (b) the working memory and executive function and (c) the global cognitive composite by averaging z ‐scores from relevant tests, as described previously (Chatterjee et al. 2018 ; Sharmin et al. 2025 ). To assess how cognitive performance varied with alterations in plasma SM levels, participants were grouped into tertiles based on their composite z ‐scores for each cognitive measure (De Anda‐Duran et al. 2024 ; Nuño et al. 2019 ). Those in the lowest tertile represented poorer cognitive function, while those in the highest tertile exhibited stronger cognitive performance. 2.6 Statistical Analyses The participant characteristics were summarised as mean ± standard deviation (s.d.) for numerical measures and as counts ( n ) with corresponding percentages (%) for categorical measures. To compare demographic and clinical variables between the CU Aβ– and CU Aβ+ groups, independent samples t ‐tests and chi‐square tests were performed as appropriate. The distribution of dependent variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Shapiro–Wilk tests for normality. When necessary, data were transformed (using logarithmic or square root functions) to better approximate a normal distribution. All participants included in this study met the inclusion and exclusion criteria of the KARVIAH cohort and had previously been involved in similar research (Chatterjee et al. 2021 ; Sharmin et al. 2025 ). As the entire eligible cohort was used and the study employed a screening design aimed at identifying AD‐related underlying biochemical pathways, no formal sample size estimation or outlier exclusion was undertaken. Spearman correlation coefficients ( ρ ) were used to explore correlations between plasma T‐tau levels and plasma SM levels separately in the CU Aβ– and CU Aβ+ groups. To account for the AD‐related confounding variables—namely age, sex, APOE ε4 allele status and body mass index (BMI)—generalised linear models (GLMs) were employed, and the analyses were followed by false discovery rate (FDR) correction using the Benjamini–Hochberg method to adjust for multiple comparisons. Only Spearman correlations with p ‐values less than 0.05 that remained statistically significant after adjusting for confounding variables and applying FDR correction were considered robust and reported in this study. Plasma SMs that showed significant associations with plasma T‐tau were further examined for their cross‐sectional relationships across varying levels of cognitive performance, as defined by tertiles of the verbal and visual episodic memory, the working memory and executive function, and the global composite scores. These analyses were conducted using Kruskal–Wallis tests, followed by pairwise multiple comparisons, with FDR adjustments applied using Benjamini–Hochberg corrections. Furthermore, the plasma T‐tau‐associated SMs were examined for their relationships with cortical PET‐Aβ burden, using both Spearman correlations and adjusted linear models with FDR correction. Additionally, logistic regression–based predicted models were used to generate receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated to assess classification performance. Comparative analyses of predictive performance between the explored AD‐related biomarker models were conducted using DeLong's test. All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 29), GraphPad Prism (version 10.5.0), and the R statistical environment (version 4.4.0). Visualisations and graphs were produced using GraphPad Prism or the R statistical environment, depending on the figure type and formatting requirements. 3 Results 3.1 Participant Characteristics The demographic details, cognitive assessments, brain imaging measures and plasma measure T‐tau of the study participants are summarised in Table 1 . Among the demographic characteristics—sex, APOE ε4 allele status, age, body mass index (BMI) and education level—only APOE ε4 allele status showed a significant difference between CU Aβ− and CU Aβ+ individuals. While MMSE scores did not differ between groups, the verbal and visual episodic memory, the working memory and executive function, and the global composite scores showed trends toward poorer performance in the amyloid‐positive individuals ( p ‐values≈0.08–0.09). Although these trends did not reach statistical significance, the markedly lower scores—approximately half of those in amyloid‐negative individuals—may reflect subtle emerging cognitive vulnerability. As expected, hippocampal volume, as measured by MRI, did not differ significantly. Plasma T‐tau levels did not differ significantly between the groups; however, a trend toward higher concentrations was observed in CU Aβ+ individuals ( p = 0.06). None of the circulating SMs differed significantly between CU Aβ− and CU Aβ+ individuals (Table S1 ). While significant bivariate correlations were detected, subsequent GLMs were applied to adjust for sex, APOE ε4 allele status, age and BMI, along with FDR corrections to account for multiple comparisons. No statistically significant associations were found between plasma SMs and T‐tau levels in CU Aβ− individuals. However, in CU Aβ+ individuals, plasma T‐tau showed significant positive associations with SM(34:1), SM(36:1), SM(38:2), SM(39:1), SM(40:2), SM(40:4), SM(41:1), SM(42:1), SM(42:2), SM(43:1) and SM(44:1) (Table 2 , Figure 1 ). These associations remained statistically significant both prior to and following adjustment for AD‐associated confounding variables and applying FDR corrections (Table 2 ). While investigating a linear combination of these SMs with plasma T‐tau and Base, the predictive performance (AUC = 85.6% with 95% CI: 78%–93%) was significantly better than T‐tau by itself (AUC = 60% with 95% CI: 48%–72%, n = 100, Delong's test p = 0.00002009 and Z = −4.264) and noticeably higher than T‐tau + Base (AUC = 80.2% with 95% CI: 71%–89%, n = 100, Delong's test p = 0.05638 and Z = −1.908) for cortical PET‐Aβ burden classification (Table 4 , Figure 4B ). In this investigation, statistically significant positive correlations of plasma T‐tau were observed with SM(34:1), SM(36:1), SM(38:2), SM(39:1), SM(40:2), SM(40:4), SM(41:1), SM(42:1), SM(42:2), SM(43:1) and SM(44:1) exclusively in CU Aβ+ individuals and the associations remained significant even after adjusting for AD‐associated confounding variables and applying the FDR corrections for multiple comparisons. However, no significant correlations were found between plasma T‐tau and any SM species in CU Aβ− individuals. These findings indicate linear associations between early neuronal damage and SM‐mediated biochemical pathways in CU older adults at higher risk for AD. Interestingly, several lipidomic studies have demonstrated reduced levels of specific SM species in the plasma of individuals with AD or those at risk of developing the disease. For example, studies by Orešič et al. ( 2011 ), Han et al. ( 2011 ) and Liu et al. ( 2021 ) found significantly reduced SM levels in the plasma of late‐onset AD individuals, suggesting systemic lipid dysregulation associated with disease pathology. Similarly, Fote et al. ( 2021 ) reported reduced levels of one specific SM species in individuals with preconverter and mild cognitive impairment (MCI) (based on a composite memory Z score adjusted for AD‐related covariates) compared to cognitively healthy controls, highlighting their potential as early biomarkers for AD detection. More recently, in a study of erythrocyte metabolite profiling for AD detection, Mill et al. ( 2022 ) identified a consistent reduction in certain SM species in AD individuals compared to controls. Furthermore, Mielke et al. ( 2011 ) reported that higher plasma SM levels were associated with better cognitive performance and a lower risk of AD development, suggesting a potential neuroprotective or compensatory role. Taken together with prior reports, the current findings may represent early plasma SMs‐related mechanisms occurring in CU Aβ+ individuals to compensate for tau release during the initial stages of neurodegeneration. Given that SM is a key structural component of neuronal membranes, its fluctuations in peripheral circulation could indicate early adaptive responses to emerging neurodegenerative processes, potentially driven by membrane remodelling or lipid homeostasis regulation before significant neuronal loss manifests. Given the exploratory nature of this study, replication in larger cohorts is required. Further, upon analysing the circulating T‐tau‐associated SMs across the spectrum of cognitive domains—as defined by tertiles of the verbal and visual episodic memory, the working memory and executive function and the global composite scores—significant cross‐sectional associations were observed with the working memory and executive function, as well as the global cognition. These associations continued to remain statistically significant even after FDR corrections. Across the spectrum, SM(40:4) displayed significantly lower plasma levels accompanying weak working memory and executive function; additionally, SM(39:1) and SM(40:4) had significantly lower plasma levels accompanying poor global cognitive performance. In a study of AD individuals, Mielke et al. ( 2011 ) described relationships between higher SM levels and slower progression of cognitive decline, suggesting a potential protective role of SM in neurodegeneration. Recently, Guo et al. ( 2025 ) reported higher plasma SM levels accompanying better verbal memory performance in non‐demented older adults. Therefore, consistent with previous research, the current observations further strengthen the connection between SM homeostasis and cognitive resilience. Additionally, while examining the associations with cortical amyloid burden, significant correlations were observed with some T‐tau‐associated SM species only in CU Aβ+ individuals—a novel observation, as per available research. Specifically, SM(40:2), SM(41:1) and SM(42:1) had significant negative associations with cortical PET‐Aβ load only in CU Aβ+ individuals, both before and after adjusting for AD‐related confounding variables in linear model testing and following FDR corrections for multiple comparisons. While absent in CU Aβ− individuals, these findings suggest that circulating T‐tau‐associated SMs are inversely associated with cortical‐Aβ aggregation in CU older adults at risk for AD, and thus, underscore a potential link between early AD pathogenesis and SM‐mediated biochemical pathways in the initial presymptomatic stage of the disease. Moreover, to evaluate the ability to discriminate CU Aβ+ from CU Aβ− individuals, we employed receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis. A panel of these T‐tau‐associated circulating SMs significantly classified cortical PET‐Aβ status with an area under the curve (AUC) of 71.2%, which increased to 83.7% when integrated with a base model; additionally, following a linear combination with the base model and circulating T‐tau, the panel's predictive potential improved significantly, achieving an AUC of 85.6% (DeLong's test: p = 0.00002009, Z = −4.264) compared to T‐tau alone (AUC = 60%, n = 100). Eventually, a stepwise logistic regression was performed to identify a refined model (MFF) incorporating sex, APOE ε4 allele status, age, T‐tau, SM(40:2), SM(42:1) and SM(44:1), which represents another novel finding based on current knowledge. Although the SMs + Base + T‐tau model showed a slightly higher AUC than the MFF model (85.6% vs. 84.8%), overall accuracy was comparable (78% vs. 77%). The MFF model exhibited higher sensitivity, which may be advantageous for early detection in preclinical settings. Both models showed high negative predictive values (&gt; 89%), supporting strong rule‐out capability. Given its comparable performance with potentially lower complexity, the MFF model may represent a more parsimonious and clinically scalable approach. Sphingomyelins (SMs) are the most abundant sphingolipids in the plasma membranes of mammalian cells and tissues (Slotte 2013 ). This lipid class plays a vital role in forming membrane microdomains known as lipid rafts. These specialised regions are highly dynamic and heterogeneous, exist in a non‐equilibrium state and can undergo significant reorganisation in response to various biological stimuli (Grassi et al. 2020 ; Mielke et al. 2011 ; Olsen and Færgeman 2017 ). Notably, as essential constituents of lipid rafts, SMs contribute significantly to maintaining cholesterol homeostasis (Slotte and Ramstedt 2007 ), facilitating signal transduction, modulating inflammatory responses and regulating oxidative stress (Kao et al. 2020 ; Lista et al. 2023 ; Schneider et al. 2019 ). Additionally, SMs are crucial in amyloid precursor protein (APP) processing. Altered SM levels can compromise the structural integrity of lipid rafts—membrane microdomains enriched in cholesterol and sphingolipids—that spatially organise key proteins involved in APP processing, such as β‐secretase (BACE1, β‐site amyloid precursor protein cleaving enzyme 1) and γ‐secretase. This disruption can, in turn, affect the production of Aβ peptides (Ehehalt et al. 2003 ; Grimm et al. 2005 ; Kakio et al. 2001 ; Vetrivel et al. 2004 ). Evidence suggests that increased sphingomyelinase (SMase) activity or SM depletion disrupts raft composition, facilitating aberrant colocalisation of APP with β‐secretase, thereby contributing to amyloidogenic pathways (Di Paolo and Kim 2011 ; Grimm et al. 2005 ). Additionally, the accumulation of Aβ, in both its soluble and fibrillar forms, can promote reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, which in turn activates both neutral and acidic SMase, further accelerating SM depletion and promoting lipid membrane alterations that may exacerbate neurodegenerative processes (Czubowicz et al. 2019 ; Di Paolo and Kim 2011 ; Grimm et al. 2005 ; He et al. 2010 ; Jana and Pahan 2004 ; Jazvinšćak Jembrek et al. 2015 ; Xing et al. 2023 ). Furthermore, beyond many damaging effects via ROS generation, Aβ‐induced oxidative stress dysregulates the p38 MAPK signalling pathway, ultimately resulting in tau hyperphosphorylation (Giraldo et al. 2014 ). In parallel, Aβ accumulation and the resulting oxidative stress also contribute to elevated ceramide levels, which subsequently trigger pro‐inflammatory pathways and promote neuronal apoptosis (Cutler et al. 2004 ; Czubowicz and Strosznajder 2014 ; Jazvinšćak Jembrek et al. 2015 ). Moreover, in the current study, the T‐tau‐associated circulating SMs were observed to have associations with early AD‐related markers and measures, that is, cognitive performance and cortical PET‐Aβ, only in CU Aβ+ individuals, highlighting their potential role in counteracting amyloid‐driven dysregulation. Therefore, in conjunction with previous reports, the current findings most likely reflect circulating SM‐mediated protective mechanisms in CU Aβ+ individuals. This protective effect may be mediated through the preservation of lipid raft integrity, regulation of APP processing, attenuation of pro‐inflammatory signalling and stabilisation of neuronal membrane—all of which may help delay the onset or slow the progression of neurodegenerative processes in AD. However, plasma SM levels are also influenced by multiple systemic factors, including diet, hepatic function, metabolic status, dyslipidaemia and broader lifestyle determinants. These variables may alter circulating SM levels independently of tau release. Therefore, alterations in SM levels may not solely reflect neuroprotective processes but could also be partially driven by peripheral metabolic influences. In this study, plasma T‐tau was selected as the primary tau‐related biomarker because it reflects broader neuronal damage and neurodegeneration processes that we hypothesised could be associated with early SM‐mediated dysregulation in CU older adults. In our previous study (Sharmin et al. 2025 ), although P‐tau181–an early marker for growing Aβ pathology that precedes tau tangle formation–differed significantly between study groups ( p = 0.001) and no significant associations were found with SMs, suggesting that SM‐related lipid alterations may be more closely aligned with general neuronal injury rather than phosphorylation‐specific tau pathology at this early stage. Similarly, an earlier study from our team (Chatterjee et al. 2018 ) reported that neurofilament light (NfL), a marker of axonal degeneration, showed no between‐group differences ( p = 0.384) and no associations with SMs. Further, glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), which predominantly reflects astroglial activation related to Aβ positivity and inflammation, represents a distinct biological pathway from neuron‐derived tau. Consistent with this rationale, only SM(38:2) had a significant positive correlation with NfL, and exclusively in CU Aβ+ individuals (Table S3 ). This association remained significant after adjustment for sex, age, APOE ε4 status and BMI, and after FDR correction, suggesting it is unlikely to be explained by major AD‐related confounders. The absence of similar findings in CU Aβ− individuals indicates that the relationship between SM(38:2) and axonal injury may emerge preferentially in the presence of cerebral amyloid burden. Further, no significant correlations were observed between T‐tau–associated SMs and GFAP in any group, supporting a closer link between SM alterations and neuronal injury rather than astroglial activation or inflammatory pathways. Based on the above rationale, the present study focused on investigating associations of circulating SMs with early neuronal‐injury–related tau release, examining their AD‐related clinical relevance, and evaluating their potential utility for classifying cortical PET‐Aβ burden. Several strengths support the findings. First, circulating SMs were quantified on high‐resolution mass spectrometry integrated with the flow injection analysis (FIA) platform employing the AbsoluteIDQ p400 HR kit (Biocrates Life Sciences ag). Second, circulating T‐tau levels were measured with the ultrasensitive single‐molecule array (Simoa) technology. Third, all identified T‐tau‐associated SMs were analysed for associations with AD‐related key clinical measures and markers, including cognitive and memory function assessments as well as cortical PET‐Aβ imaging. Fourth, statistical analyses accounted for major AD risk factors, including sex, APOE ε4 allele status, age and BMI. Fifth, FDR adjustments were applied to control for multiple comparisons. Sixth, ROC curves and AUC analyses were generated to assess the predictive potential of each plasma biomarker. Seventh, DeLong's tests were performed to compare the predictive performance of various AD biomarkers. Finally, a refined model was developed using a stepwise logistic regression approach to prevent overfitting while preserving optimal predictive accuracy. Despite these strengths, the study has several limitations. It was cross‐sectional and focused on presymptomatic individuals, limiting its ability to assess longitudinal changes. The sample size was modest and circulating T‐tau was not analysed alongside cerebrospinal fluid T‐tau, restricting direct comparisons. Additionally, the study did not fully account for potential confounders such as medication use, dietary factors, or hepatic and metabolic influences. Another limitation was the lack of adjustment for racial and ethnic diversity, which may affect the generalisability of the findings. In conclusion, this study identified circulating sphingomyelins associated with T‐tau, providing insights into SM‐mediated biochemical pathways associated with early neuronal damage in CU older adults at higher risk for AD. Furthermore, lower sphingomyelin levels were associated with poor working memory and executive function as well as global cognition, highlighting their potential prognostic value accompanying poor cognitive performance. Additionally, this study revealed inverse cross‐sectional associations between circulating T‐tau‐associated SMs and cortical PET‐Aβ load exclusively in CU older adults at higher risk for AD. This finding suggests a potential connection between early AD pathogenesis and SM‐mediated biochemical pathways during the early preclinical stage of the disease. Moreover, a panel of these circulating SMs demonstrated significant predictive potential in classifying cortical PET‐Aβ status and improving the diagnostic performance of plasma T‐tau, indicating their viability as early predictive markers for neuropathological changes in ageing populations. However, further validation in independent cross‐sectional and longitudinal cohorts, incorporating parallel assessments of CSF and plasma T‐tau in well‐characterised AD populations along with rigorous cross‐validation, calibration and evaluation of multicollinearity, is necessary to confirm these findings.
