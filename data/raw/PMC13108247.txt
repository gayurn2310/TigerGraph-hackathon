Life course neighborhood conditions and change: a 20‐year examination of neighborhood context and associations with Alzheimer's disease biomarkers in late life

Abstract INTRODUCTION Life course models of Alzheimer's disease (AD) suggest that both long‐term exposure to neighborhood conditions and changes in neighborhood context may shape dementia risk. This study examined the impact of mid‐ and late‐life levels of neighborhood segregation, as well as longitudinal change in segregation, on plasma AD biomarkers. METHODS Participants were 119 racially/ethnically diverse (60% Black/Latino) adults. Residential addresses were geocoded to temporally harmonized US Census tracts, and the dissimilarity index quantified segregation. Plasma neurofilament light chain (NfL), glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), and amyloid beta (Aβ) 42/40 were measured. RESULTS Generalized estimating equations adjusting for individual‐level sociodemographic and health characteristics revealed mid‐life, but not late‐life, levels of segregation were significantly associated with NfL. Increasing segregation across 10‐ and 20‐year periods was associated with higher levels of GFAP and NfL, respectively. DISCUSSION Mid‐life levels of segregation and longitudinal change in segregation were linked to plasma markers of neurodegeneration and astroglial activation.

1 BACKGROUND The neighborhood context has been recognized as an important environmental determinant of Alzheimer's disease (AD), and research has established that where people live, work, and play fundamentally influences their exposure to protective resources and harmful risk factors across the life span. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 Life course models of AD have emphasized that neighborhood context may shape dementia risk through three key pathways: (1) cumulative exposure to adverse neighborhood conditions that accrue over time, (2) exposure to adverse neighborhood conditions during sensitive or critical periods that may heighten vulnerability to adverse outcomes, and (3) neighborhood transitions that can alter exposure patterns and influence disease trajectories. 5 , 6 Research centered on understanding the timing, duration, and evolution of neighborhood exposures is essential for elucidating how environments shape dementia risk. RESEARCH IN CONTEXT Systematic review : We reviewed prior literature on neighborhood context, residential segregation, and AD. Existing work focuses primarily on associations between late‐life neighborhood characteristics and cognition outcomes and rarely employs a life course approach framework to examine biological markers of AD. Interpretation : This study extends existing evidence by demonstrating that both historical exposure to segregation and increasing levels of segregation over time are associated with plasma markers of neurodegeneration and astroglial activation, but not amyloid pathology. Findings suggest that the biological consequences of structural inequality unfold across decades and that characterizing historical neighborhood conditions and neighborhood change is critical for understanding neighborhood effects on AD risk. Future directions : Future research will examine these associations in larger, diverse, longitudinal cohorts, incorporate additional neighborhood factors, and evaluate whether changes in neighborhood conditions relate to neuroimaging markers of brain structure and pathology. Studies employing a life course approach to examining AD remain limited, and most existing work relies on static, single‐time‐point assessments of neighborhood conditions in late life. 7 , 8 , 9 This approach overlooks historical exposures during mid‐life, a period when dementia risk may be modifiable, and ignores how neighborhood change may influence vulnerability over time. Importantly, neighborhood environments are not fixed – they are dynamic spaces that evolve through processes such as gentrification, economic restructuring, and population turnover. 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 These shifts can either expand access to health‐promoting resources or exacerbate inequities through resource erosion and displacement. 14 , 15 Residents of neighborhoods that experience sustained investment may have more favorable health outcomes and trajectories than those facing persistent disinvestment and adversity. 13 Focusing solely on late‐life neighborhood conditions without considering how neighborhoods have evolved over time limits our ability to identify prevention windows and is ineffective for developing place‐based interventions 1 . Racial residential segregation is a powerful structural force that has produced unequal neighborhood conditions over time, 16 , 17 and national trends underscore the need to examine residential segregation as a potential determinant of AD. 18 Although residential segregation has declined in most metropolitan areas, average levels remain persistently high and are increasing among Hispanic/Latino populations. 19 , 20 , 21 These structural shifts signal growing divergences in opportunity that may be important for dementia risk, and emerging evidence has further linked changes in neighborhood trajectories to late‐life cognition. 22 Leveraging data from the National Neighborhood Archive Data, one recent study characterized changes in neighborhood disadvantage from 2000 to 2017 in a large sample of older adults enrolled in the Michigan Cognitive Aging Project and identified three distinct patterns of neighborhood change: (1) persistently high disadvantage, (2) moderate and increasing disadvantage, and (3) low and mildly increasing disadvantage. 22 Notably, individuals living in neighborhoods with moderate and increasing disadvantage exhibited poorer cognitive functioning, and this pattern of change partially explained racial disparities in cognition. Most studies have focused on the association between late‐life neighborhood factors and cognitive outcomes, 23 , 24 while investigations linking neighborhoods to AD biomarkers and neuroimaging outcomes are more limited. 25 However, emerging evidence suggests that neighborhood conditions may shape upstream biological pathways that are central to AD pathogenesis. 26 , 27 Segregated neighborhoods may concentrate disadvantage and limit resources, increasing exposure to chronic stress and contributing to hypothalamic‐pituitary‐adrenal axis dysregulation, cardiovascular disease, and systemic inflammation. 28 , 29 Segregation may also limit opportunities for social engagement, thereby reducing cognitive reserve and resilience to AD pathology. 16 , 30 Additional research is needed to clarify when and how life course exposure to higher levels of segregation, and changes in segregation, shape AD‐related disease pathways and vulnerability. To address these gaps, this study adopted a life course approach to examine how neighborhood conditions and change in residential segregation over a 20‐year period related to AD biomarkers. We leveraged baseline residential address data from long‐term residents of a metropolitan area (Austin, TX, USA) that has experienced rapid growth, economic restructuring, and gentrification. 14 , 25 , 27 , 28 , 30 , 31 We harmonized US Census tracts across sampling periods (2000, 2010, and 2020) to capture historical neighborhood context from mid‐life, current neighborhood conditions in late life, and neighborhood change. This design allowed us to disentangle enduring structural inequities from neighborhood transitions, identify sensitive or critical exposure periods, and clarify biosocial pathways linking place to AD risk. 2 METHODS 2.1 Study sample and participants Participants ( N = 150) were recruited for the Mechanisms Underlying Neurocognitive Aging Laboratory's Brain Aging Study at the University of Texas (UT) at Austin between 2022 and 2025. Recruitment occurred through study advertisements, targeted mailers sent to Census tracts predominantly occupied by racial/ethnic minority residents, and community outreach events (e.g., food drives, senior center psychoeducation events, and health screening fairs). Study participation involved the completion of a blood draw, neuropsychological testing, medical history and background questionnaires, physical health assessments (e.g., blood pressure and waist circumference), and/or brain and renal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans. All procedures involved in this study were in accordance with the ethical standards of the responsible committee on human experimentation (institutional and national) and with the Helsinki Declaration of 1975. Each participant gave written informed consent upon enrollment in the study, and the protocol for the study was approved by the UT Institutional Review Board (IRB # STUDY00002434). 2.2 Inclusion/exclusion criteria Participants were community‐dwelling adults aged 50 years or older residing in the larger Austin metropolitan and Central Texas area who were able to complete cognitive testing in English. Exclusion criteria were current diagnosis of dementia or neurologic diseases (e.g., multiple sclerosis), history of severe psychiatric illness (e.g., schizophrenia and bipolar disorder), current alcohol or substance abuse, endorsement of a medical condition that could impact cognition (type 1 diabetes; current cancer diagnosis; traumatic brain injury; HIV/AIDS). The present analysis included a subset of individuals ( n = 119) who completed blood draws, had biomarker and demographic data, and provided valid residential address information for linkage of neighborhood‐level data. 2.3 Residential history, geocoding, and harmonization of census data Participants provided their residential address and length of residence at this address during their baseline study visit. Addresses were geocoded to 2010 Census boundaries using ArcGIS Pro and linked with US decennial census data (2000, 2010, and 2020) at the block and tract level using the Integrated Public Use Microseries (IPUMS) National Historical Geographic System 32 time‐series tables. These data are geographically standardized to 2010 Census boundaries using areal interpolation methods. 33 This harmonization procedure ensured consistent geographic units across census sampling periods and allowed for valid estimation of neighborhood change while minimizing measurement error due to shifts in Census tract boundaries that may occur over time. Length of residence was collective to characterize residential stability within the sample but was not incorporated into exposure modeling. 2.4 Residential segregation levels and longitudinal change Segregation was measured using the dissimilarity index, which represents the proportion of one group that would need to relocate to achieve an even population distribution across areas. The dissimilarity index captures the spatial distribution of groups across neighborhoods and therefore reflects structural separation between these groups independent of the overall racial or ethnic composition of any single census tract. The dissimilarity index was calculated at the census tract level using the following formula: Tract dissimilarity = 0.5 × ∑ i n | a i A − b i B | , where a and b represent the non‐Hispanic White and minoritized populations (e.g., Black/African American, Hispanic, and other, quantified by subtracting the non‐Hispanic White population from the total population within the tract), respectively, at the block group level, whereas A and B represent the group populations at the tract level. Values for the dissimilarity index range from 0 to 1, with higher levels representing a greater degree of segregation. 34 , 35 Levels of segregation were calculated for each decennial census year (2000, 2010, and 2020). Longitudinal changes in segregation were calculated as the difference between 2020 and 2000 values (20‐year change) and as the difference between 2020 and 2010 (10‐year change), with positive values indicating increasing segregation over time. Because complete residential histories across the 20 years were not available, change measures reflect the historical characteristics and trajectories of census tracts corresponding to participants geocoded enrollment addresses rather than time‐weighted individual exposure histories. Accordingly, segregation trajectories are interpreted as indicators of the structural evolution of participant‐observed residential contexts that may shape present‐day neighborhood conditions. Prior work showed that minoritized households tended to move to neighborhoods of similar economic and social characteristics 36 and therefore indicate baseline address may also be used to approximate patterns of exposure to residential segregation over 20 years. 2.5 Other neighborhood factors: tract‐level neighborhood disadvantage and racial/ethnic composition Neighborhood disadvantage was calculated for each decennial census year (2000, 2010, and 2020) using data from the National Neighborhood Data Archive (NaNDA) to capture structural economic conditions at the tract level. 37 Neighborhood disadvantage was operationalized as the mean of two tract‐level indicators that have been demonstrated to consistently reflect a lack of economic resources at the census tract level across the 20‐year period: proportion of residents with income below the federal poverty level and proportion of households receiving public assistance income. 38 Tract‐level racial and ethnic composition was also derived for each decennial census year and consisted of the proportion of residents identifying as non‐Hispanic White, Hispanic/Latino, and non‐Hispanic Black. These measures were included to characterize neighborhood socioeconomic and demographic context and are distinct from segregation indices, which capture the spatial organization of groups across tracts rather than their relative concentration within a single tract. 2.6 Blood draw procedures, APOE genotyping, and health status Blood samples were collected in tubes containing sodium EDTA as an anticoagulant. Samples were sent to Quest Diagnostic for apolipoprotein E (APOE) genotyping, complete blood count, comprehensive metabolic panel, and hemoglobin A1c analyses. APOE ε4 positivity was determined by the possession of at least one ε4 allele. Cardiometabolic risk was characterized using criteria from the National Cholesterol Education Program Adult Treatment Panel III (ATP III) Guidelines for metabolic syndrome (MetS). 39 Participants were classified as meeting MetS criteria if they exhibited three or more of the following: (1) abdominal obesity (≥102 cm for men and ≥88 cm for women), (2) triglyceride level ≥ 150 mg/dL, (3) high‐density lipoprotein &lt; 40 mg/dL for men and &lt; 50 mg/dL in women, (4) blood pressure ≥ 130/85 mg Hg, (5) fasting glucose &gt; 100 mg/dL. 2.7 AD plasma biomarker quantification The remaining blood samples were centrifuged, and plasma was aliquoted into polypropylene tubes and stored at −80°C. Plasma biomarkers were quantified using the Bio‐Techne ELLA platform (Protein Simple, Minneapolis, MN, USA), which employs automatic microfluid immunoassay technology. Concentrations of amyloid beta (Aβ) 1‐42, Aβ1‐40, glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), and neurofilament light chain (NfL) were measured using multiplex ELLA kits. Sample sizes differed slightly for biomarker data given these data were processed in batches and some subjects may not have had available data at the time of analysis (Nfl: n = 119 Aβ42/40: n = 113, GFAP: n = 111). All assays were performed in triplicate according to the manufacturer's protocols, and observations with intra‐ and inter‐assay coefficients above 15% were excluded from analyses. A small proportion of biomarker data fell below the lower limit of detection (NfL: n = 1, Aβ42/40: n = 3, GFAP: n = 7) and were left censored to the lower limit in order to retain the observations. Lower plasma Aβ42/40, but higher GFAP and NfL have been linked to more severe AD pathology and poorer clinical outcomes. 40 , 41 , 42 , 43 2.8 Cognitive status, everyday functioning, and psychiatric symptomatology The Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) 44 total score was used to characterize global objective cognition and subjective cognitive changes were evaluated using the Everyday Cognitive (ECog) Scale. 45 Symptoms of anxiety were assessed using the Beck Anxiety Inventory, 46 and symptoms of depression were assessed using the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES‐D). 47 2.9 Statistical analyses All analyses were performed in R version 4.5.1. Data were screened to ensure basic assumptions were met. Continuous variables were standardized ( z ‐scored) to improve interpretability and facilitate comparison of estimates across models. Generalized estimating equations (GEEs) were used to determine whether levels of segregation (2000, 2010, and 2020) and longitudinal changes in segregation (10‐ and 20‐year) were associated with plasma AD biomarkers (GFAP, NfL, Aβ42:40). Primary models adjusted for individual‐level covariates including age, sex, race/ethnicity, years of education, APOE ε4 positivity, and MetS status. Models examining longitudinal changes additionally adjusted for baseline level of segregation to isolate the effects of change over time. GEE models were selected given they account for non‐independence of observations due to geographic clustering, as some participants resided in the same census tract. In total, 72 tracts were represented in the sample (mean = 1.65 participants per tract; maximum of five participants). To obtain stable variance estimates given the small number of observations within some clusters, parameter estimates were supplemented using non‐parametric bootstrapping procedures (5000 resamples) conducted at the census tract level. 48 Bias‐corrected 95% confidence intervals are reported alongside model‐based estimates as an assessment of estimate stability. For models demonstrating significant associations, secondary sensitivity analyses additionally adjusted for the appropriate neighborhood disadvantage level to evaluate whether associations were independent of broader neighborhood socioeconomic conditions. Given the primary aim of the study was to evaluate the potential timing and latency of neighborhood effects across multiple exposure windows, adjustments for multiple comparisons were not applied. 49 3 RESULTS 3.1 Participant characteristics Table 1 summarizes sample demographic and neighborhood characteristics for the full sample. Our results reveal that while racial residential segregation has generally declined for the sample, roughly 35% of participants resided in census tracts that experienced increases in segregation during the study period. Importantly, segregation levels and changes demonstrated distinct associations with AD biomarkers of interest. Historical mid‐life, but not contemporaneous late‐life, levels of racial residential segregation were linked to plasma NfL. Increasing levels of residential segregation were associated with plasma NfL and GFAP, although there were no associations between segregation and Aβ42/40. Taken together, these findings suggest that static neighborhood conditions and dynamic neighborhood change represent separable environmental exposures with potentially different impacts on dementia‐related pathology. Patterns of residential segregation across the 20‐year study align with national US trends indicating that residential segregation has generally declined, but these declines have not been uniform across racial and ethnic groups. 20 , 50 Within this regional sample, mean levels of residential segregation were relatively stable between 2000 and 2010, with more pronounced declines observed between the 2010 and 2020 sampling period. However, as illustrated in the segregation change map (Figure 3 ), these declines were not spatially uniform and several peripheral areas of the metropolitan region observed increases in segregation over the same period. These localized increases appear to coincide with broader shifts in the racial and ethnic composition of the metropolitan area (Figure 4 ). Notably, concentrations of non‐Hispanic White residents increased in centrally located tracts, while concentrations of non‐Hispanic Black and Hispanic/Latino residents declined in many of these central areas and increased in more peripheral tracts. The collective spatial patterning of these demographic shifts across neighborhoods reflects a reorganization of segregation and is consistent with rapid population growth, economic restructuring, and rising housing costs in expanding metropolitan areas, which may redistribute racially/minoritized populations from inner‐city neighborhoods to more affordable surrounding areas. 14 , 51 , 52 Taken together, these dynamics suggest that metropolitan growth and expansion in Austin may have reorganized across parts of the city, even as region‐wide averages declined. Although other research has established late‐life residential segregation is associated with cognitive outcomes, 9 , 24 , 53 we did not observe any significant associations between late‐life 2020 estimates of segregation and our AD biomarkers in the present study. However, we did observe that higher levels of mid‐life segregation in 2000 were associated with lower levels of plasma neurodegeneration. This inverse association is consistent with theory that suggests segregated spaces can also function as important sources of social, cultural, and political capital and resistance that temporarily support cognitive health in later life. 54 Consistent with these findings, we previously found that racially minoritized older adults living in highly segregated neighborhoods exhibited higher baseline levels of cognition in another independent sample. 9 However, at the same time, faster rates of cognitive decline were observed, potentially due to cumulative adversity that often accompanies living in a highly segregated residential environment. 9 Notably, in this study, the association between mid‐life segregation and NfL was attenuated after accounting for neighborhood disadvantage, suggesting that broader economic conditions partially accounted for this relationship and underscore the importance of distinguishing segregation from co‐occurring socioeconomic context. Finally, there were no associations between segregation levels, change, and Aβ42:40, suggesting that segregation‐related exposures may be more closely linked to neurodegeneration and astrogliosis rather than amyloid pathology. Collectively these findings support a life‐course interpretation in which historical, mid‐life neighborhood exposures may exert differential effects compared to contemporaneous late‐life exposures and highlight mid‐life as a potential period during which structural conditions may shape later vulnerability or resilience. Increasing segregation across both the 10‐ and 20‐year periods was associated with higher levels of plasma NfL, whereas increases in segregation during the more recent 10‐year period were associated with higher levels of GFAP. These findings suggest a temporal process in which long‐term worsening of segregation relates to neurodegeneration, and more proximal changes are associated with astroglial activation. These different associations observed across the 10‐ and 20‐year intervals further indicate that the timing and duration of neighborhood change may engage distinct pathological processes that could be overlooked in studies focused solely on static environmental measures. Although the association between 10‐year segregation on GFAP were imprecise (as evidenced by bootstrapped confidence intervals that included zero), the direction and magnitude of the bootstrapped estimates were consistent. Importantly, the effects of contextual exposures are often modest in size, but still may be biologically meaningful. 55 For example, a one standard deviation increase in segregation (i.e., 0.06 or 6% change) was associated with an estimated 1.15 pg/mL increase in plasma GFAP, comparable to an increase in GFAP associated with approximately 2.5 additional years of age after adjusting for individual‐level covariates. Importantly, the effects of change in segregation on plasma biomarkers were also independent of both historic segregation levels and neighborhood disadvantage, indicating that worsening segregation itself – regardless of baseline conditions – was associated with higher NfL and GFAP levels. This suggests that changes in neighborhood structure may constitute a distinct exposure from static neighborhood conditions. Such vulnerability may reflect the cumulative biological toll of increasing spatial inequality, the concentration of at‐risk individuals into underresourced areas, or continued patterns of resource loss and disinvestment in affected communities. 14 , 51 Although neighborhood change has been methodologically difficult to characterize, resources like the Longitudinal Tract Database 50 and IPUMS National Historical Geographic System 32 make it possible to standardize census tract boundaries across time, which allows for valid interpretations of the impact of neighborhood change on health. Taken together, neighborhoods are dynamic spaces that evolve over time, and the degree and magnitude of change on certain neighborhood indicators like segregation need to be further considered in future research investigations. There are several strengths to the current study's life course approach to examining neighborhood exposures. We leveraged geographically standardized decennial census data across three time points to estimate both mid‐life and late‐life neighborhood exposures and compared their relative effects on current levels of plasma‐based biomarkers. We also measured residential segregation using the formal index of dissimilarity, which more precisely captures unevenness in the spatial distribution of economic and social resources compared to informal segregation measures (e.g., proportion of non‐White residents). This study implemented a novel approach to examining neighborhood change by utilizing difference scores and adjusting for historic levels of residential segregation, which helped isolate the effects of neighborhood change. The use of plasma‐based biomarkers that are sensitive to AD‐related neurodegeneration and pathology allowed us to detect neighborhood effects in a relatively young sample of adults in mid to late life who are not yet experiencing notable cognitive impairments. Finally, our analysis was conducted in a regionally based sample of racially/ethnically diverse adults who were largely residentially stable, which permitted deeper contextualization of our findings in Austin's history and the examination of the effects of neighborhood change on populations who may be particularly impacted by recent urban growth. While we believe this study represents an initial step toward characterizing the effects of neighborhood change on risk for AD, there are several limitations that should be acknowledged and addressed by future research. We did not collect residential history data at baseline and thus could not verify that participants lived at their address for the entire duration of the 20‐year period examined. However, the median length of residence was 15 years, and prior work suggested that minoritized households tend to move to neighborhoods of similar economic and social characteristics. 36 There was also a gap of 2 to 5 years between 2020, when levels of segregation were measured, and 2022 to 2025, when participants completed their baseline biomarker testing. We decided to utilize 2020 decennial census data to reduce bias in estimates that would have been introduced by the different sampling methods employed in the American Community Survey from 2022 to 2025. Future studies should also examine other measures of segregation (e.g., interaction, index of concentration at extremes) that may capture additional mechanisms through which patterns of change in the distribution of neighborhood resources may influence AD risk. 9 , 56 A smaller sample size may have contributed to reduced precision in our estimates, although the observed effect sizes were consistent with or somewhat larger than those reported in the existing literature. Larger cohorts may be leveraged in the future to replicate our findings and improve precision. Lastly, our regionally specific sample may have limited generalizability to other cities with more Black community members and higher historic levels of segregation. Results highlight the importance of examining neighborhood context across the life course and underscore that neighborhoods represent a critical target for dementia prevention efforts. By incorporating measures of historical mid‐life, current late‐life, and longitudinal changes in segregation, the present study helped disentangle the impact of enduring structural inequities from more contemporary neighborhood transitions and clarified potential biosocial pathways that increased risk for pathologic aging outcomes in late life. As researchers continue to characterize important structural determinants of AD in research studies, efforts that leverage residential history data may help enhance life course approaches and clarify important pathways underlying the development of AD.
