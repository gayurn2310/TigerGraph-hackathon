Perivascular pathology, not macrovascular complexity, governs glymphatic-related dysfunction in preclinical cerebral small vessel disease

The glymphatic system facilitates brain waste clearance, and its dysfunction has been linked to aging, neurodegeneration, and cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD). Enlarged perivascular spaces (ePVS) are established imaging markers of impaired clearance, while the role of macrovascular architecture, particularly the complexity of the circle of Willis (CoW), remains uncertain. In this cross-sectional study of 60 asymptomatic adults with low-to-moderate vascular risk, CoW complexity was quantified using fractal dimension (D f ) analysis, and perivascular diffusivity, an indirect proxy of glymphatic-related activity, was estimated using diffusion tensor image analysis along perivascular space (DTI-ALPS) index, and ePVS burden was stratified as a CSVD marker. CoW complexity was correlated with higher DTI-ALPS and lower ePVS burden, but these relationships did not remain significant after adjusting for age, sex, and vascular risk. In contrast, the presence of ePVS was the strongest and most consistent predictor of reduced DTI-ALPS values, highlighting its central role in impaired fluid dynamics. Thus, ePVS remains the dominant and clinically relevant imaging biomarker of glymphatic-related activity in asymptomatic adults with low-to-moderate vascular risk, providing a potential mechanistic link to early cerebrovascular aging. These findings underscore the importance of microvascular health in maintaining glymphatic function and may guide early biomarkers and preventive strategies in preclinical CSVD. Introduction The glymphatic system, a brain-waste clearance pathway that relies on perivascular fluid exchange, plays a crucial role in removing metabolic waste and maintaining cerebral homeostasis. Dysfunction of this system, termed glymphopathy, has been implicated in neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease, aging-related cognitive decline, and cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD) 1 – 3 . Among the radiological biomarkers of glymphopathy, enlarged perivascular spaces (ePVS), fluid-filled spaces surrounding small penetrating vessels, have gained prominence as reliable and non-invasive indicators of impaired interstitial fluid (ISF) drainage 4 . At the same time, vascular architecture, particularly the morphology of the Circle of Willis (CoW), may play a crucial role in influencing cerebral perfusion dynamics and, consequently, glymphatic transport 5 – 7 . CoW is a key arterial collateral network that buffers blood flow and modulates perfusion in response to cerebrovascular stress 5 . Variations in CoW completeness and geometry have been associated with ischemic stroke risk, cognitive decline, and CSVD severity 8 , 9 . However, its potential role in supporting glymphatic circulation remains underexplored. Recent advances in mathematical modeling and neuroimaging have enabled the quantification of vascular geometric complexity using tools such as fractal dimension analysis. The fractal dimension (D f ) measures how completely a vascular structure fills space across scales, reflecting the intricacy and tortuosity of the network 10 . Applying this to CoW, the D f of the CoW, abbreviated as D f (W), may serve as a novel quantitative surrogate biomarker capturing inter-individual variability in vascular complexity. Yet, its functional implications for brain clearance mechanisms, such as glymphatic activity, have not been systematically examined. Furthermore, fractal geometry offers a powerful framework for understanding biological flow systems. Many cerebral vascular structures exhibit self-similarity and space-filling properties that optimize fluid distribution 11 . Higher D f in vascular networks has been linked to more efficient blood flow, autoregulation, and resilience to ischemic stress 10 – 12 . When applied to the CoW, a higher D f (W) may reflect greater redundancy and adaptability in collateral circulation, which in turn could influence the effectiveness of pulsatile cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) movement, a fundamental driver of glymphatic inflow, whereby the glymphatic system relies on CSF propulsion along periarterial spaces, driven by arterial pulsations 8 , 13 . The coherence and amplitude of these pulsatile waves are modulated by the upstream vascular structure. A more complex CoW geometry may facilitate more consistent arterial pulsatility and uniform perfusion pressure, thereby enhancing CSF influx into the glymphatic pathway. Conversely, lower CoW complexity may impair pulsation dynamics, reduce glymphatic efficiency, and contribute to waste stagnation, manifesting as ePVS and cognitive decline 14 . Despite these theoretical connections, few studies have explored the intersection between macrovascular complexity, microvascular disease, and glymphopathy 8 , 9 , 15 . Most existing work classifies CoW in binary terms (complete vs. incomplete), overlooking the continuous variability captured by fractal modelling. Integrating fractal-based vascular analysis with current surrogate markers of glymphatic-related function, such as the diffusion tensor image analysis along the perivascular space (DTI-ALPS) index 16 , and CSVD biomarkers like ePVS, may provide deeper insights into how structural vascular features influence brain clearance mechanisms. DTI-ALPS has emerged as a promising MRI-based proxy of perivascular fluid diffusion, which may indirectly reflect aspects of glymphatic-related activity. Although it does not directly measure glymphatic flow, it is increasingly applied to characterize microstructural fluid transport along perivascular spaces in vivo 16 . Despite its increasing application in human research, it is important to note that DTI-ALPS remains an experimental tool without formal validation against gold-standard dynamic or tracer-based methods of glymphatic flow measurement. It primarily captures anisotropic diffusivity along perivascular trajectories, which is interpreted as glymphatic-related activity but may also be influenced by other microstructural or vascular factors such as axonal orientation or interstitial water content. Therefore, while DTI-ALPS offers a valuable, noninvasive approximation of perivascular fluid transport, its specificity to glymphatic function should be considered preliminary. Importantly, this study targets a low-to-moderate QRISK3 population, representing working-aged adults with relatively low predicted 10-year cardio-cerebrovascular disease risk 17 . These asymptomatic or subclinical individuals are often overlooked in cerebrovascular research, yet may harbor early signs of glymphopathy and microvascular remodeling. Investigating vascular-glymphatic interactions in this group could offer critical insights into the preclinical phase of CSVD progression and inform preventive strategies before irreversible damage occurs. Finally, given that ePVS reflects downstream manifestations of glymphopathy, and CoW anatomy may regulate upstream vascular input, this study aims to integrate both perspectives by investigating the relationship between D f (W) and an indirect proxy of glymphatic-related activity (DTI-ALPS), while accounting for ePVS burden, vascular risk factors, and cognitive performance. It is hypothesized that asymptomatic individuals with more complex CoW architecture, i.e., higher D f (W), will exhibit more efficient glymphatic function, as reflected in higher DTI-ALPS indices, and that this relationship may be modulated by ePVS burden. Ultimately, this study seeks to establish a novel vascular-glymphatic link that bridges structural vascular complexity and brain waste clearance efficiency. The findings may contribute to the development of surrogate imaging biomarkers for early detection of glymphopathy and CSVD progression, especially in asymptomatic individuals. Furthermore, they may guide new preventive strategies aimed at preserving cerebrovascular and cognitive health by targeting vascular geometry and flow regulation. No significant group differences were observed for smoking status, diabetes, family history of cardiovascular disease, or other comorbidities. Notably, none of the participants had chronic kidney disease, atrial fibrillation, systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), severe mental illness, nor were they on antipsychotic drugs or long-term steroid therapy. However, individuals with ePVS were significantly more likely to be on blood pressure (BP) treatment (26.9%) than those without ePVS (5.9%), with a statistically significant association ( Χ² [1, 60] = 5.116, p = 0.024). Regarding metabolic risk factors, the total cholesterol to high-density lipoprotein (CHO/HDL) ratio and body mass index (BMI) did not differ significantly between the groups. Finally, the cardio-cerebrovascular disease risk prediction scores (QRISK3) were significantly higher in participants with ePVS compared to those without (t [58] = -2.494, p = 0.016). In terms of cognitive functioning, measured by the WAIS-IV, no significant differences were found between groups in all cognitive domains measured, i.e., PRI, WMI, and PSI. Strikingly, two neuroimaging markers showed robust group differences. The D f (W) was significantly lower in the ePVS group compared to the non-ePVS group (t [58] = 6.106, p &lt; 0.001) (Fig. 1 F-G). Similarly, the DTI-ALPS index, representing a proxy to glymphatic-related activity, was significantly reduced in the ePVS group compared to the non-ePVS group (t [58] = 7.604, p &lt; 0.001). Correlation between CoW complexity and DTI-ALPS To further investigate the interrelationships among vascular morphology, glymphatic-related activity, and CSVD markers, we conducted a correlation analysis incorporating the D f (W), DTI-ALPS index, ePVS burden, and other vascular, cognitive, and demographic parameters. The findings are illustrated in Fig. 2 , comprising a hierarchical cluster heatmap and a bivariate scatter plot with a linear fit. The heatmap revealed a distinct clustering pattern, wherein D f (W) and DTI-ALPS were positioned within the same branch, both demonstrating a moderate positive correlation ( r = 0.37, p = 0.004). Conversely, D f (W) exhibited a moderate inverse correlation with ePVS burden ( r =-0.63, p &lt; 0.001). Similarly, the DTI-ALPS index demonstrated a strong negative correlation with ePVS ( r =-0.71, p &lt; 0.001). Notably, D f (W) was negatively associated with QRISK3 score ( r =-0.46, p &lt; 0.001), age ( r =-0.41, p &lt; 0.001), and BPT ( r =-0.41, p = 0.02). The DTI-ALPS index did not correlate with age and QRISK3 score, negatively correlated with BPT ( r =-0.30, p = 0.02). D f (W) and DTI-ALPS index did not correlate with any cognitive function domain in this cohort. Mediation analysis A mediation analysis was performed to investigate whether the D f (W) mediates the association between ePVS and DTI-ALPS index, while adjusting for QRISK3 score, age, and sex. Results in Fig. 3 (A) demonstrated that ePVS burden was significantly associated with decreased D f (W) (β=–0.1905, SE = 0.0376, p &lt; 0.001). However, D f (W) did not significantly predict changes in DTI-ALPS index after adjusting for ePVS (β = − 0.1838, SE = 0.1891, p = 0.336). The direct effect of ePVS on DTI-ALPS remained statistically significant (β = − 0.4058, SE = 0.0639, p &lt; 0.001), suggesting a robust inverse relationship. The indirect effect of ePVS on DTI-ALPS through D f (W) was estimated at 0.0350 (Bootstrapped SE = 0.0437), with a 95% bias-corrected and accelerated (BCa) bootstrap confidence interval ranging from − 0.0479 to 0.1247. Since the confidence interval included zero, the indirect effect was not statistically significant, indicating no mediation. Collectively, these findings suggest that although D f (W) is influenced by ePVS burden, it does not mediate the relationship between ePVS and glymphatic-related or perivascular diffusivity proxied by DTI-ALPS index (Fig. 3 ). Sensitivity analysis A series of sensitivity tests was conducted to evaluate the robustness of the observed mediation effect of D f (W) in the relationship between ePVS and DTI-ALPS. Additionally, standardizing the mediator, i.e., D f (W), and modifying the covariate set (removing QRISK3 or age) yielded consistent mediation effects, further supporting the robustness of the findings. Discussion This study provides new insights into the vascular–glymphatic interface by evaluating CoW fractal complexity alongside perivascular burden and glymphatic-related function. Using DTI-ALPS, an established but indirect diffusion-based marker of perivascular fluid dynamics, rather than a direct measure of glymphatic flow (CSF–ISF exchange) 16 , we examined whether CoW geometry influences clearance dynamics in asymptomatic adults with low-to-moderate vascular risk. The findings from this study highlight an independent and robust association between ePVS burden and DTI-ALPS index, while suggesting that reduced CoW complexity, although related to ePVS, does not directly influence the DTI-ALPS index proxy for glymphatic-related activity. This has important implications for understanding the vascular–glymphatic interface in working-aged adults and may inform early preventive strategies targeting brain waste clearance. The cohort comprised 60 working-aged adults (mean age 46.15 ± 11.41 years) with a modest but non-negligible burden of vascular risk factors. Despite the relatively preserved clinical profile, a striking dissociation emerges between subjective health and objective neurovascular alterations, an increasingly recognized phenomenon in covert CSVD. Notably, our stratified analysis revealed that while the presence of ePVS was not significantly associated with chronological age or sex, it was disproportionately more prevalent in individuals on BPT and those with elevated QRISK3 scores. Both variables are established markers of long-term vascular burden and microangiopathic risk 18 – 20 , reinforcing the vascular etiology of ePVS even in the absence of overt cerebrovascular events. The absence of significant group-level differences across WAIS-IV cognitive domains deserves critical interpretation. On the surface, this may suggest a preserved cognitive profile among individuals with ePVS, consistent with previous longitudinal evidence that radiological markers of CSVD can accumulate silently for years before manifesting as cognitive impairment 21 , 22 . However, it is also plausible that the WAIS-IV, while broadly sensitive to global cognition, may not detect subtle executive or processing speed deficits that typify early CSVD-associated cognitive changes 23 . Moreover, our data showed trend-level associations between glymphopathy and working memory/processing indices, suggesting that more nuanced cognitive batteries or domain-specific assessments, e.g., Brief Memory and Executive Test (BMET), Stroop, and Trail Making Test B, might reveal latent deficits not captured in global scores 24 . The fact that the ePVS group exhibited both significantly lower D f (W) and reduced DTI-ALPS index raises an important question regarding the biological timing and sequence of CSVD pathology. These findings highlight that reduced DTI-ALPS values may co-occur with lower cognitive indices at a trend level. However, the analysis examined whether cognitive scores were associated with DTI-ALPS, not vice versa; thus, the directionality of this association cannot be determined. The observed trend remains biologically plausible within the framework linking glymphatic-related activity and cognitive health, but causal inference is beyond the scope of this cross-sectional analysis. Therefore, while our data aligns with hypotheses proposing that glymphatic efficiency supports cognitive resilience 25 , 26 , our results should be interpreted strictly as correlational, reflecting concurrent variability between cognitive and perivascular diffusion metrics rather than directional influence. Besides, our findings also align with a growing body of research proposing a “vascular–glymphatic continuum” in which capillary rarefaction, arterial stiffening, and altered pulsatility disrupt perivascular clearance and glymphatic flow well before neuronal dysfunction becomes evident 25 , 26 . However, it is essential to interpret these findings within the limitations of the study design. The cross-sectional nature precludes temporal inference, and the directionality of the observed associations remains speculative. For instance, while we posit that ePVS reflects underlying vascular pathology contributing to glymphopathy, it is also plausible that impaired glymphatic clearance exacerbates ePVS accumulation through failure to clear interstitial solutes, creating a bidirectional or synergistic cycle of damage. Without longitudinal data or advanced flow-sensitive MRI techniques, these mechanistic pathways remain unresolved. Furthermore, the modest sample size and limited representation of individuals with high-risk profiles may attenuate the generalizability of the findings. Stratification by QRISK3 or BPT, while informative, may also mask interactions with other unmeasured variables such as systemic inflammation or genetic susceptibility (e.g., APOE4 status), all of which have been implicated in both glymphatic and vascular integrity 27 , 28 . In sum, the dissociation between clinical normalcy and radiological pathology in this cohort echoes the emerging consensus that CSVD is not a threshold phenomenon but a progressive, silent cascade that unfolds across decades. Our findings contribute to a growing recognition that young-to-midlife or working-aged adults may represent a critical intervention window, where subtle neurovascular and glymphatic alterations offer early warning signals before irreversible cognitive decline ensues. Besides, the findings from this study revealed a moderate, statistically significant positive correlation between fractal dimension, i.e., D f (W), and DTI-ALPS index. This observation aligns with theoretical frameworks suggesting that the geometric intricacy of the cerebral vasculature plays a regulatory role in ISF dynamics 29 , 30 . Specifically, prior modeling and animal studies have posited that vascular branching patterns influence arterial pulsatility and regional perfusion heterogeneity, which are essential for effective glymphatic inflow and clearance 25 , 31 . However, upon adjusting for confounding variables, including age, sex, QRISK3 score, BPT, and ePVS burden, the predictive value of D f (W) on DTI-ALPS index (proxy to glymphatic function) was no longer statistically significant. This attenuation may suggest that vascular geometry, while potentially contributory, may not exert a dominant or independent influence on glymphatic-related efficiency in the context of this cohort’s CSVD risk. Instead, its role could be secondary to or moderated by other structural or functional changes, particularly those associated with microangiopathy. For instance, vascular stiffness and loss of compliance in small perforating arteries may reduce the amplitude of arterial pulsations, rendering geometric factors less relevant to the driving forces behind CSF-ISF exchange in affected individuals 32 , 33 . Moreover, the dominant role of ePVS in explaining the glymphopathy proxy is particularly striking. In our multivariable model, ePVS emerged as the strongest negative predictor of DTI-ALPS, with a large effect size (β = − 0.748, p &lt; 0.001) that remained robust despite adjustment for vascular and cognitive variables. This finding supports the hypothesis that ePVS may represent both a morphological marker and a mechanistic contributor to impaired glymphatic clearance. Moreover, ePVS are thought to arise from impaired drainage or ISF stasis, and their accumulation may further distort perivascular architecture, exacerbating glymphatic resistance and slowing clearance of metabolic waste such as amyloid-β and tau 22 , 34 . Besides, while our primary focus was on vascular and glymphatic relationships, exploratory correlations between DTI-ALPS and selected WAIS-IV indices such as WMI and PSI showed trend-level associations. Although not statistically significant in this sample, these patterns indicate that individuals with higher cognitive performance exhibited higher DTI-ALPS values. Importantly, this reflects an association rather than a directional or causal relationship, since the model tested whether cognition was related to DTI-ALPS rather than the reverse. However, it is plausible that altered interstitial clearance may selectively impact neural circuits with high metabolic demand or vulnerability to solute accumulation, domains where working memory and processing speed are particularly sensitive 35 , 36 . From a mechanistic standpoint, the lack of a strong independent association between D f (W) and glymphatic proxy may also be attributable to individual variability in CoW anatomy, which was not explicitly controlled in this analysis. For example, in the case of the presence of hypoplastic or absent communicating arteries may compromise collateral flow and perfusion symmetry, reducing the effective fractal utility of the vascular network 37 , 38 . Future work should incorporate anatomical completeness (e.g., from TOF-MRA segmentation) and hemodynamic indices (e.g., pulsatility index, flow velocity) to more comprehensively characterize the vascular-glymphatic interface. Collectively, these findings support a model in which microvascular pathology, rather than global vascular architecture, predominates in influencing glymphatic-related function in working-aged adults. They also underscore the importance of considering both structural, e.g., ePVS, D f (W), and functional, e.g., DTI-ALPS index and cognitive performance markers in tandem, particularly in identifying individuals at heightened risk for subclinical disease progression. Furthermore, our mediation analysis provided further insight into the mechanistic interplay between CoW morphology, perivascular pathology, and glymphatic-related function. As expected, individuals with higher ePVS burden exhibited significantly reduced CoW complexity, as captured by a lower D f (W). This association supports the notion that CSVD-related microangiopathy exerts structural effects on large vessel architecture, potentially via processes such as vascular rarefaction, remodeling, or increased tortuosity 39 . Despite this relationship, D f (W) did not mediate the effect of ePVS burden on DTI-ALPS. The absence of significant indirect effects, confirmed across multiple sensitivity analyses, including bootstrapped CI and model re-parameterization, argues against a causal chain whereby impaired vascular geometry links perivascular pathology to ISF clearance deficits. This is a notable finding, as it challenges assumptions that large-vessel complexity directly facilitates or impedes glymphatic transport. Instead, our results reinforce emerging evidence that microstructural and functional properties of the vascular wall, such as arterial compliance and endothelial-perivascular coupling, may be more critical determinants of glymphatic integrity than macroscopic vascular architecture. For instance, decreased pulsatility due to arterial stiffening diminishes the perivascular pumping mechanism that drives CSF-ISF exchange 40 , 41 . Similarly, loss of astrocytic aquaporin-4 (AQP4) polarization, a hallmark of aging and CSVD, can disrupt fluid channeling even in anatomically preserved perivascular routes 25 , 34 . From a methodological standpoint, the rigor of our mediation model was enhanced by the inclusion of key covariates (e.g., age, sex, blood pressure treatment, QRISK3 score) and robustness checks. The persistence of non-significant indirect effects across all model variants suggests that the observed associations between ePVS and glymphopathy are unlikely to be explained by vascular morphological complexity. While D f (W) remains a sensitive marker of vascular compromise in CSVD, its lack of mediation underscores the need to distinguish correlative structural indices from true pathophysiological intermediaries. This insight has important translational implications. While fractal analyses of vascular networks (via MRA, CT angiography, or even retinal imaging) are gaining popularity as accessible biomarkers of cerebrovascular health 42 , 43 , our findings caution against overinterpreting such metrics as functional surrogates for glymphatic competence. Future research should aim to integrate fractal analyses with dynamic hemodynamic assessments (e.g., transcranial Doppler, arterial spin labeling) and molecular markers of perivascular integrity (e.g., AQP4, laminin, pericyte coverage) to establish more comprehensive and mechanistically grounded models. In summary, while CoW complexity declines in the context of CSVD and is associated with glymphopathy at a cross-sectional level, it does not mediate the relationship between ePVS and glymphopathy. This distinction highlights the nuanced and likely multifactorial nature of glymphatic compromise, where microvascular and glial factors may exert more direct mechanistic influence than large-scale vascular geometry alone. This study leverages a multimodal, neurovascular framework to interrogate the relationships between CoW morphology, ePVS, and glymphatic-related function. The use of fractal analysis provides a quantitative and scale-invariant measure of vascular complexity, offering higher sensitivity than traditional morphometric indices. Additionally, employing both regression and mediation approaches enables a mechanistic interpretation of findings beyond mere association. The exclusion of participants with systemic inflammatory diseases or psychiatric conditions reduces potential confounding and enhances internal validity. Several limitations warrant consideration. First, cross-sectional design limits causal inference, and longitudinal studies are needed to assess temporal dynamics between vascular remodeling and glymphatic decline. Second, while the sample size was adequate for primary analyses, it may have limited statistical power for detecting subtler mediation effects or stratified subgroup analyses. Thirdly, a critical methodological consideration is that the DTI-ALPS index, although widely adopted in clinical and preclinical studies as a noninvasive proxy of glymphatic-related activity, has not yet undergone formal biological validation against direct measures of regional heterogeneity or AQP-4 dysfunction, glymphatic flux, such as dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI, intrathecal tracer kinetics, or two-photon imaging. Besides, recent work by Li et al. 44 demonstrates that DTI-ALPS is influenced by underlying white matter microstructural asymmetry. Using postmortem human brains, the authors showed that fiber-specific microstructural differences vary with age and amyloid-β pathology and systematically bias DTI-ALPS measurements. Importantly, when these microstructural effects were minimized, the corrected DTI-ALPS index exhibited weaker associations with amyloid PET burden and cognitive performance in vivo, indicating that DTI-ALPS values may partially reflect microstructural integrity rather than perivascular fluid transport alone. Therefore, interpretations of DTI-ALPS should remain conservative, as it reflects diffusion characteristics along perivascular spaces rather than verified fluid flow 16 , 36 . This limitation also explains why some associations may appear attenuated after covariate adjustment, as DTI-ALPS may capture a mixture of perivascular, axonal, and extracellular matrix changes. Finally, future studies should integrate markers of vascular pulsatility, sleep physiology, and cognitive decline to better contextualize the clinical implications of glymphatic disruption. Nonetheless, emphasizing its reproducibility and accessibility, DTI-ALPS continues to provide an informative, albeit indirect, window into glymphatic-related microstructural dynamics 16 , 36 , particularly when used in conjunction with complementary CSVD markers such as ePVS. An additional methodological consideration is that the DTI-ALPS index may reflect, in part, underlying microstructural white matter damage rather than perivascular diffusivity alone. Approximately one-third of our cohort exhibited visible white matter hyperintensities (WMHs) (not reported), suggesting that a degree of microangiopathic change was present. Although WMHs and related metrics, such as mean diffusivity (MD) or peak width of the skeletonized mean diffusivity (PSMD), are recognized indicators of diffuse microstructural injury 45 , the present analysis focused specifically on ePVS as the microvascular hallmark of interest. This decision was made to isolate the perivascular–glymphatic component of CSVD, while maintaining statistical parsimony within a modest sample size. Future work from this cohort will incorporate WMHs volumetrics and diffusion-based integrity indices (MD, PSMD) to further clarify the relative contributions of microstructural damage versus perivascular dysfunction to DTI-ALPS variability. Such integration will provide a more comprehensive understanding of the complex interplay between glymphatic-related diffusion, white matter integrity, and small vessel pathology. In summary, this study suggests that ePVS burden is the most robust and clinically relevant imaging biomarker of impaired perivascular clearance, whereas DTI-ALPS provides an indirect diffusion-based estimate of glymphatic-related activity in asymptomatic adults with low-to-moderate vascular risk. Together, these measures highlight complementary aspects of perivascular pathology relevant to early cerebrovascular aging. Although CoW complexity declines with greater perivascular pathology, it does not independently predict glymphatic-related activity nor mediate the ePVS–glymphatic relationship. These findings underscore the primacy of microvascular health over macrovascular geometry in maintaining brain clearance, highlight the utility of ePVS and DTI-ALPS as early imaging markers of glymphopathy, and suggest that preventive interventions should target microvascular and neuroglial integrity in the preclinical stages of CSVD. Future longitudinal and multimodal investigations are warranted to clarify the temporal sequence and causal architecture linking vascular complexity, perivascular damage, and glymphopathy. Such knowledge could open new avenues for early detection, risk stratification, and intervention in cerebrovascular and neurodegenerative disorders. Methods Ethics approval, sample size estimation, and subject recruitment This study was conducted at Hospital Universiti Sains Malaysia (HUSM), with ethical approval from the Human Research Ethics Committee USM (USM/JEPeM/15030096). All methods have been performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Participants were recruited via convenience sampling from routine attendees at the Family Medicine Clinic, HUSM. Informed consent has been obtained from all participants. Demographic and clinical data were collected to individual cardiocerebrovascular risk scores using the QRISK3 algorithm. A final sample size of 58 was calculated using G*Power (v3.1.9.7) to detect a moderate correlation ( r = 0.35) between variables (α = 0.05, power = 80%) 46 , 47 . Sixty participants were enrolled to account for attrition. Of 203 screened individuals, 124 met eligibility; 60 completed the MRI (Fig. 4 ). This sample was adequate for correlational and multivariable analyses involving up to five predictors, adhering to the recommended 10:1 subject-to-variable ratio 35 , 47 . Cardio-cerebrovascular risk assessment Cardio-cerebrovascular risk was assessed using the QRISK3 (2018) online calculator ( http://www.qrisk.org/index.php ), developed by the University of Nottingham and EMIS. This tool estimates the 10-year risk of developing cardio-cerebrovascular disease based on demographic and clinical variables. Risk categories were defined as follows: low (≤ 10%), moderate (10.1–20%), and high (≥ 20.1%) 48 . Eligible participants were asymptomatic individuals aged 25–62 years, with no history of neurological disease and classified as having low to moderate cardiovascular risk based on their QRISK3 scores. Individuals with high cardio-cerebrovascular risk (≥ 20.1%) or any prior neurological diagnosis were excluded to reduce confounding from established or subclinical cerebrovascular or neurodegenerative pathology that could affect white matter integrity and cognition 49 . The study, therefore, focused on an asymptomatic, low- to moderate-risk population to improve sensitivity and maintain a homogeneous cohort for detecting subtle imaging biomarkers of early CSVD. Moreover, comorbidities including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, smoking status, and family history of cardiovascular disease were documented and summarized in Table 1 . Of these variables, only BPT demonstrated a significant association with imaging outcomes and was therefore included as a covariate in the multivariable model. The composite QRISK3 score was also retained to represent overall cardiovascular risk, while non-significant comorbidities were excluded to maintain statistical parsimony. Neurocognitive assessment Cognitive performance was assessed using the Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale, Fourth Edition (WAIS-IV) 50 . Three composite indices were analyzed to capture distinct cognitive domains relevant to white matter integrity: (i) the perceptual reasoning index (PRI), which assesses nonverbal reasoning and visuospatial problem-solving, (ii) working memory index (WMI), reflecting attention, mental manipulation, and sequential processing, and (iii) processing speed index (PSI), which evaluates the speed of visual information processing and graphomotor coordination. DTI-ALPS index analysis Raw DTI data were pre-processed using FMRIB Software Library (FSL version 5.0.9, https://www.FMRIB.ox.ac.uk/FSL/ , accessed on 10th July 2025). Pre-processing included brain extraction, correction for eddy current distortions and head motion, and voxel-wise calculation of diffusion metrics using dtifit , yielding fractional anisotropy (FA) and diffusivity values along the x -, y -, and z -axes (D x , D y , D z ). The DTI-ALPS index calculation was based on the previous literature 54 , 55 . The axial slice at the level of the lateral ventricle body was selected (Fig. 5 B-E). At this level, the perivascular spaces are oriented perpendicular to the ventricular wall, corresponding predominantly to the right–left ( x -axis) direction in the axial plane. This orientation is orthogonal to both the projection fibers (mainly along the z -axis) and the association fibers (mainly along the y -axis). Consequently, diffusivity along the x -axis in these regions partly reflects water movement along perivascular spaces. Spherical regions of interest (ROIs) with a 5-mm diameter (12 voxels each) were manually placed in the left hemisphere within the projection fibers, association fibers, and subcortical fibers (Fig. 5 E). For each ROI, diffusivity values along the x -, y -, and z -axes were extracted, i.e., (i) D x -proj and D y -proj from the projection fiber ROI, (ii) D x -assoc and D z -assoc from the association fiber ROI. Since the perivascular spaces align along the x -axis, D x -proj and D x -assoc represent diffusivity parallel to the PVS, relatively independent of the dominant fiber orientation. All ROIs were delineated by two experienced radiologists blinded to participant information to minimize bias. Fractal analysis of the CoW Three-dimensional time-of-flight magnetic resonance angiography (3D-TOF MRA) images were first spatially normalized to the International Consortium for Brain Mapping (ICBM) 152 standard template using the FMRIB’s Linear Image Registration Tool (FLIRT), version 6.0.1 ( https://www.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/fsl ) 56 . A 12-parameter affine transformation was applied to co-register each subject’s MRA to the T1-weighted average structural image (ICBM 152, 0.5 × 0.5 × 0.5 mm resolution) to minimize individual variations in head size and field of view, thereby reducing their influence on subsequent D f (W) measurements 26 . Co-registered 3D-TOF MRA volumes were converted into 2D maximum intensity projection (MIP) images using Fiji software (ImageJ, version 1.54p) 57 . Each 2D-MIP image underwent semi-automated segmentation using the fast random forest algorithm within the Trainable Weka Segmentation Tool (version 3.2.35) 58 . This step separated cerebral blood vessels from the surrounding brain tissue. Manual refinements were performed in Fiji (version 1.54p) to ensure accurate extraction of the vasculature and removal of artefacts. Segmentation was independently conducted by two trained researchers, blinded to subject groupings, under the supervision of a certified radiologist. This procedure resulted in two separate cerebral vessel segmentations per subject. Final vessel segmentations were converted to binary images. Skeletonization of the binary vascular images was then performed using Fiji’s built-in functions to reduce the vessel structures to their essential topological form, as required for fractal analysis (Fig. 6 ). Statistical analysis All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 26.0) (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), with additional mediation analysis performed via the PROCESS macro (v4.2). A two-tailed p -value of &lt; 0.05 was considered statistically significant unless otherwise specified. Descriptive statistics were computed to summarize participant demographics, vascular risk factors, cognitive scores, D f (W), DTI-ALPS index, and CSVD burden (ePVS). Continuous variables were reported as means and standard deviations (SD), and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Bivariate associations between key variables, including D f (W), DTI-ALPS, ePVS, age, QRISK3 score, cognitive indices (PRI, WMI, PSI), and blood pressure treatments (BPT), were assessed using Pearson’s correlation coefficient. A correlation matrix was generated and visualized using a hierarchical clustering heatmap and dendrogram to identify inter-variable relationships and clustering patterns. To assess the independent predictors of glymphatic function, a multiple linear regression model was constructed with DTI-ALPS as the dependent variable and D f (W), ePVS, age, QRISK3, PRI, WMI, PSI, and BPT as independent variables. Although BPT is included within the QRISK3 algorithm, it was also entered as an independent covariate because BPT showed a significant association with both ePVS ( p = 0.024) and DTI-ALPS in preliminary analyses, whereas other comorbidities did not. This approach allowed assessment of the direct vascular influence of antihypertensive therapy on perivascular diffusivity. Multicollinearity was evaluated using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) and tolerance statistics. Model fit was assessed via R², adjusted R², F-statistics, and standard error of the estimate. To explore whether D f (W) mediates the relationship between ePVS (predictor, X) and DTI-ALPS (outcome, Y), a simple mediation model (Model 4) from the PROCESS macro was applied, controlling for QRISK3, age, and sex. The indirect effect was tested using bootstrap resampling (10,000 samples) with bias-corrected 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Mediation was considered significant if the 95% CI for the indirect effect did not include zero. Finally, sensitivity analysis was conducted by excluding potential outliers and rerunning the regression models. Data distributions and effect sizes were visualized using scatterplots, standardized regression plots, and correlation heatmaps. A clustered heatmap with dendrogram was generated to display intercorrelations among vascular, glymphatic, and cognitive variables.
