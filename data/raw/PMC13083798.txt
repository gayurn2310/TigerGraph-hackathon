Dissecting microglial contributions to neurodegenerative disease pathophysiology using human pluripotent stem cells

Summary Neurodegenerative diseases are characterized by progressive neuronal dysfunction and loss. Microglia, the brain’s resident macrophages, are key contributors to disease pathogenesis, with many genetic risk variants enriched in microglia-specific genes. While rodent models have provided valuable insights, human induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC) and embryonic stem cell (ESC) technologies now enable the generation of human microglia-like cells, offering a physiologically relevant platform to study human microglial biology. This review discusses the developmental origins and functions of microglia, current differentiation approaches, and how these models help elucidate disease-relevant phenotypes and molecular mechanisms in neurodegeneration.

Introduction Microglia, first described as a distinct cell type by Pío del Río-Hortega in 1919, have since been recognized as the resident macrophages of the central nervous system (CNS) that play essential roles in immune surveillance. Over the past century, extensive research based on the use of primary murine and human microglia, immortalized microglial cell lines, and postmortem human brain tissue has revealed that microglia are not only immune sentinels, but also key regulators of CNS homeostasis through their dynamic interactions with neurons and other glial cells ( Sierra et al., 2016 ; Paolicelli et al., 2022 ). A major breakthrough occurred in 2016 when protocols were first developed to generate microglia-like cells (MGLs) from human pluripotent stem cells (hPSCs) ( Muffat et al., 2016 ). MGLs closely recapitulate human primary microglia in terms of morphology, transcriptomic profiles, and functional properties ( Abud et al., 2017 ; Douvaras et al., 2017 ; Haenseler et al., 2017a ; Pandya et al., 2017 ). Importantly, they provide a renewable, genetically diverse, and gene-editable platform, as hPSCs can be indefinitely expanded, derived from diverse genetic backgrounds, or engineered to harbor disease-associated variants. This technology thus enables mechanistic interrogation of human microglial biology in disease-relevant contexts, while bridging the translational gap between rodent and human systems ( Smith and Dragunow, 2014 ; Galatro et al., 2017 ; Xiang et al., 2018 ; Speicher et al., 2019 ) and overcoming the limited availability of living human microglia ( Mizee et al., 2017 ). Neurodegeneration is marked by neuronal loss, although it generally also includes the involvement of multiple cell types ( Temple, 2023 ) In neurodegenerative diseases including Alzheimer’s disease (AD), Parkinson’s disease (PD), and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS)/frontotemporal dementia (FTD), microglial activation is a hallmark pathological feature that has been increasingly revealed as being associated with disease progression ( Gao et al., 2023 ). Advances in genomic technologies have enabled genome-wide association studies (GWASs) to identify numerous risk loci enriched in microglia-expressed genes, underscoring their potential involvement in disease pathogenesis ( Nalls et al., 2019 ; Wightman et al., 2021 ; Langston et al., 2022 ). MGLs offer a powerful platform for investigating how these genetic variants shape microglial phenotypes and functions in a human-specific context. In this review, we presented the current understanding of the origin of microglia and their physiological roles in the CNS. We then summarized key methodologies for acquiring human MGLs and evaluated how the modeling of microglia in neurodegenerative diseases has advanced our understanding of microglial contributions to neurodegeneration. Microglia Origin While microglial ontogeny has long been debated, early transcriptomic studies using serial analysis of gene expression (SAGE) revealed the expression of hematopoietic and mesodermal lineage transcripts such as E3 protein, A1, EN-7, and B94 in microglial cell lines, suggesting a developmental trajectory from mesoderm-derived hematopoietic progenitors rather than from neuroectoderm ( Inoue et al., 1999 ). A later landmark study employing in vivo fate-mapping in mice clarified this trajectory by demonstrating that microglia originate from yolk sac (YS)-derived erythromyeloid progenitors (EMPs), which infiltrate the developing brain during early embryogenesis. These primitive macrophage precursors (PMPs) give rise to a self-renewing microglial population that persists throughout adulthood and is maintained independently of bone marrow-derived hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) ( Ginhoux et al., 2010 ; Barry-Carroll and Gomez-Nicola, 2024 ). Subsequent studies have shown that this lineage depends on the transcription factors (TFs) PU.1/Spi1 and Irf8, which orchestrate the early commitment of YS-derived primitive myeloid progenitors, whereas Myb, a key regulator of definitive hematopoiesis, is dispensable, thereby indicating that microglia arise from a developmentally distinct myeloid lineage that is separate from definitive HSC-derived macrophages ( Schulz et al., 2012 ; Kierdorf et al., 2013 ) ( Figure 1 ). This gap was substantially narrowed by a single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) study of human embryonic CNS tissues ( Bian et al., 2020 ). That study provided a high-resolution molecular atlas of human embryonic microglial development, strongly supporting the idea that human microglia also originate from the earliest wave of YS-derived primitive macrophages ( Figure 1 ). Furthermore, that study delineated the transcriptional and developmental trajectories that these progenitors undergo as they differentiate into microglia within the developing brain parenchyma. That study not only demonstrated a conserved ontogeny between species but also offered key insights into human-specific gene expression programs involved in early microglial specification. Physiological functions Under physiological conditions, microglia act as immune sentinels in the CNS. Constantly extending and retracting their highly motile processes, microglia perform immune surveillance to monitor the CNS microenvironment and detect perturbations. A critical feature enabling this surveillance is the microglial ‘sensome’, a set of highly expressed genes encoding receptors and sensors that detect changes in the extracellular milieu, including damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), apoptotic cells, and pathogens ( Hickman et al., 2013 ). As part of their immune function, microglia engage in phagocytosis to remove apoptotic cells and protein aggregates and secrete cytokines and chemokines that shape the local immune environment ( Salter and Stevens, 2017 ). Beyond their immunological roles, microglia have been characterized as active regulators of CNS homeostasis, primarily based on evidence from rodent in vivo studies. In the rodent brain, microglia play a vital role in adult neurogenesis by secreting trophic factors/anti-inflammatory cytokines and phagocytosing apoptotic newborn neuroblasts in the subgranular zone ( Battista et al., 2006 ; Sierra et al., 2010 ; Borsini et al., 2015 ; Cope and Gould, 2019 ). During development, they are central to synaptic pruning, a process mediated in part by the classical complement cascade (e.g., C1q and C3), which enables selective removal of excess or weak synapses, thereby refining neural circuits ( Stevens et al., 2007 ). In the embryonic mouse cortex, transient exclusion of microglia from the mid-embryonic cortical plate has been reported to support proper differentiation of post-migratory neurons and the establishment of functional circuits ( Hattori et al., 2020 ). Furthermore, they contribute to neuronal function by releasing neurotrophic factors, such as BDNF, which promote synaptic plasticity ( Parkhurst et al., 2013 ; Duffy and Eyo, 2025 ), and by sensing neuronal activity through dynamic calcium transients that enable activity-dependent modulation of synaptic function and circuit stability ( Umpierre and Wu, 2021 ). Previously, studies using human induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived MGLs have substantiated aspects of these neuro-supportive roles in vitro , demonstrating their ability to promote neuronal maturation, synaptogenesis and network establishment ( Mesci et al., 2024 ; Subramani et al., 2024 ). In rodent in vivo models, microglia have also been shown to support myelination by interacting with oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPCs) and modulating their maturation into myelinating oligodendrocytes ( Hagemeyer et al., 2017 ), and contribute to the maintenance of blood-brain barrier (BBB) integrity during the early phase of inflammation by upregulating CLDN5 and forming tight junctions upon direct contact with endothelial cells ( Haruwaka et al., 2019 ). In addition to maintaining CNS homeostasis under steady-state conditions, microglia also contribute to adaptive responses in rodent models of injury. For instance, CD11c-positive microglia promote pain remission via IGF1 signaling following peripheral nerve injury ( Kohno et al., 2022 ), and in the context of ischemic stroke, IGF1-expressing microglia adopt a reparative phenotype, producing neurotrophic factors such as SPP1, NENF, GDFs, and FGFs to support neuronal repair ( Crotti and Ransohoff, 2016 ; Tsuyama et al., 2018 , 2024 ). Cellular states Microglia have long been classified using dichotomous frameworks such as “resting” versus “activated”, primarily based on morphological characteristics observed via silver staining ( Paolicelli et al., 2022 ). However, this simplistic view was fundamentally challenged by the introduction of two-photon in vivo imaging in Cx3cr1 -GFP mice, which revealed that microglia are highly dynamic and continuously survey the brain parenchyma even under physiological conditions ( Nimmerjahn et al., 2005 ; Paolicelli et al., 2022 ). These findings catalyzed a shift in the field, as accumulating evidence has demonstrated that microglia are not static, but that they exist in a constitutively active and dynamic state, even in the absence of overt stimuli. Moreover, their functional states are not dichotomous, but rather exist along a spectrum and are dynamically influenced by a wide array of factors, including developmental stage, brain region, sex, species, and pathological context ( Paolicelli et al., 2022 ). This paradigm shift has been accelerated by the advent of scRNA-seq. These technologies have uncovered a heterogeneous microglial landscape, characterized by distinct cellular states such as disease-associated microglia (DAM), also known as microglial neurodegenerative phenotype (MGnD), originally identified in close proximity to amyloid-beta (Aβ) plaques in AD mouse models ( Keren-Shaul et al., 2017 ; Krasemann et al., 2017 ). Although the nomenclature for microglial clusters varies among studies, Fumagalli et al. (2025) proposed five major transcriptional states that are reproducibly observed across human RNA-seq datasets: homeostatic microglia, DAM-like states, antigen-presenting microglia (HLA/MHC class II-expressing), inflammatory/cytokine-responsive microglia (CRM), and interferon-responsive microglia (IRM/IFN). These states, along with their representative gene signatures and cross-species comparisons, are systematically summarized in their annotation framework that integrates data from postmortem AD brain tissue, xenografted human microglia, and AD mouse models ( Keren-Shaul et al., 2017 ; Krasemann et al., 2017 ; Grubman et al., 2019 ; Hasselmann et al., 2019 ; Sala Frigerio et al., 2019 ; Olah et al., 2020 ; Zhou et al., 2020 ; Claes et al., 2021 ; Sayed et al., 2021 ; Gerrits et al., 2021 ; Sun et al., 2023 ; Gazestani et al., 2023 ; Mancuso et al., 2024 ; Fumagalli et al., 2025 ). However, microglial subtypes are not easily defined by a small set of unique or stable surface markers. Marker genes for different subpopulations often show overlapping expression patterns, complicating efforts to delineate discrete subtypes ( Fumagalli et al., 2025 ). Defining these functional states, understanding their specific roles in health and disease, and elucidating the molecular mechanisms that govern state transitions remain the major goals of microglial biology. Future studies should integrate multi-omic and spatial transcriptomic approaches to contextualize the microglial function within specific microenvironments and disease trajectories, as highlighted in a previous review ( Fumagalli et al., 2025 ). Generation of hPSC-derived microglia-like cells Microglia are tissue-resident macrophages (TRMs) whose identity is shaped by both ontogeny and the microenvironment, which together define their chromatin landscape. Lavin et al. (2014) demonstrated this by comparing the hierarchical clustering of H3K4me1- and H3K4me2-marked enhancers in macrophages of distinct developmental origins, revealing that ontogenetic relationships influence chromatin dynamics in TRMs. At the same time, they highlighted the critical role of the microenvironment by transplanting terminally differentiated TRMs into ectopic tissues, where approximately 70% of the original tissue-specific gene expression profiles were reprogrammed to match the new environment. In support of these findings, Bennett et al. (2018) showed that transplantation of HSC- or YS-derived myeloid cells into the brains of Csf1r −/− microglia-depleted mice induced the expression of microglial signature genes in both cases, but only YS-derived cells fully acquired a bona fide microglial identity. These results suggest that both the developmental origin and local environmental cues are essential for the establishment of microglia. Accordingly, in vitro differentiation strategies must incorporate not only ontogeny-mimicking lineage specification, but also brain-specific microenvironmental signals, often provided via cytokines, to faithfully recapitulate microglial identity ( Figure 2 ). Although no current in vitro protocol fully captures the transcriptomic signature of adult homeostatic microglia, primarily due to the loss of brain-specific environmental cues, distinct differences exist in the fidelity of the generated cells depending on the derivation strategy. Chemically defined, ontogeny-guided protocols are currently considered the most effective at establishing a baseline microglial identity that mirrors the in vivo developmental trajectory. By recapitulating the stepwise progression from primitive hematopoiesis, these methods provide a window to establish the precise epigenetic landscape required for microglial signature gene expression, such as SALL1 regulation mediated by SMAD4 and super-enhancers. In contrast, while TF-mediated approaches offer significant advantages in speed and scalability, direct reprogramming may bypass these critical epigenetic priming events, potentially resulting in a less stable or incomplete microglial identity. Therefore, the optimal protocol is context-dependent: ontogeny-guided approaches may be preferable for mechanistic studies requiring high biological fidelity, whereas TF-mediated methods enable substantially shorter differentiation timelines and faster experimental turnaround, thereby facilitating high-throughput screening applications. Ontogeny-mimicking strategies Chemically defined approaches Chemically defined differentiation protocols aim to recapitulate in vivo developmental stages through stepwise supplementation of small molecules and recombinant cytokines or growth factors, thereby directing cell fate specification under controlled in vitro conditions ( Figure 1 ). A prerequisite for the rational design of such protocols is a thorough understanding of the ontogeny and developmental trajectory of the target cell type. Unlike neurons and astrocytes, which originate from neuroectodermal lineages, microglia are derived from YS-derived EMPs that emerge via Myb -independent primitive hematopoiesis ( Kierdorf et al., 2013 ). This hematopoietic origin of microglia, distinct from that of other brain-resident cells, was only elucidated in the early 2010s, providing a conceptual framework for recapitulating microglial development in vitro ( Alliot et al., 1999 ; Ginhoux et al., 2010 ; Schulz et al., 2012 ). Consistent with this developmental insight, the first chemically defined protocol for inducing MGLs was reported by Muffat et al. (2016) . Since then, multiple differentiation protocols have been developed, and several studies have demonstrated that the intermediate progenitors generated during differentiation exhibit features of primitive hematopoiesis, either by expressing markers consistent with YS-derived macrophage ontogeny or by arising through a MYB -independent developmental pathway ( Abud et al., 2017 ; Buchrieser et al., 2017 ; Haenseler et al., 2017a ; McQuade et al., 2018 ; Speicher et al., 2019 ). A comprehensive comparison of these protocols, including procedural details and phenotypic differences, can be found in the review by Speicher et al. (2019) . TF-mediated approaches The spatiotemporal activation of lineage-specific gene expression networks is essential for cell fate transition during development. This process requires chromatin remodeling from an inactive, silent state to an active, open conformation at key regulatory regions such as promoters and enhancers. Pioneer factors play a critical role by binding directly to nucleosomal DNA in closed chromatin and opening the chromatin structure, thereby enabling other TFs and protein machineries, including histone-modifying complexes, to access cis -regulatory elements ( Ungerbäck et al., 2018 ; Lee et al., 2019 ; Barral and Zaret, 2024 ). TF-based approaches rely on forced expression of pioneer factors, either alone or in combination with other lineage-determining TFs (LDTFs), to activate lineage-specific transcriptional programs. This strategy often enables a shorter culture period by bypassing the intermediate developmental stages ( Csatári et al., 2024 ). PU.1, an ETS domain-containing TF, functions as a pioneer factor essential for the development of both myeloid and lymphoid lineages. Although PU.1 is critical for initiating nucleosome remodeling in specific genomic regions, collaborative interactions with LDTFs are required to establish accessible chromatin regions and define cell-type-specific enhancer landscapes ( Heinz et al., 2010 , 2015 ). Consistent with this model, previous reports found that forced expression of PU.1 alone was insufficient to induce microglial identity, underscoring the need for additional LDTFs ( Chen et al., 2021 ; Speicher et al., 2022 ; Csatári et al., 2024 ). In line with this, Sonn et al. (2022) combined PU.1 overexpression with a chemically defined approach, demonstrating that PU.1 acts as a necessary pioneer factor but requires concurrent cues from other LDTFs and cytokine signaling to faithfully establish a microglial transcriptional identity. Indeed, several protocols have successfully generated MGL induction by co-expressing PU.1 with additional LDTFs such as CEBPA , CEBPB , IRF8 , and MAFB , which were previously identified as critical regulators of microglial development and maintenance ( Kierdorf et al., 2013 ; Lavin et al., 2014 ; Matcovitch-Natan et al., 2016 ; Deczkowska et al., 2017 ; Pundhir et al., 2018 ; Miller-Rhodes and Gelbard, 2021 ; Chen et al., 2021 , Chen et al., 2022 ; Speicher et al., 2022 ; Dräger et al., 2022 ; Liu et al., 2025 ; Csatári et al., 2024 ). To date, five studies have reported TF-based protocols for the derivation of MGLs. A previous review provided a comprehensive summary of these strategies ( Csatári et al., 2024 ). Early attempts to generate MGLs from peripheral blood monocytes failed to reproduce key microglial signature genes characteristic of YS-derived microglia, including CX3CR1 , P2RY12 , MERTK , and GPR34 , across multiple reported protocols ( Etemad et al., 2012 ; Leone et al., 2006 ; Melief et al., 2016 ; Ohgidani et al., 2014 ). As the developmental origin of microglia became established, differentiation strategies shifted toward ontogeny-inspired approaches that recapitulate primitive hematopoiesis, resulting in improved microglial identity and greater transcriptional concordance with primary human microglia. Despite these advances, however, directed differentiation protocols remain technically demanding and show pronounced inter-study variability, reflected in wide differences in culture duration, cellular yield, and lineage purity. Protocol-specific requirements, including mechanical manipulation, co-culture systems, and the use of non-chemically defined serum-supplemented media further impede standardization and limit broader reproducibility ( Speicher et al., 2019 ). To reduce the prolonged culture timelines and multistep workflows associated with directed differentiation, TF-mediated approaches were subsequently developed, typically employing combinations of microglial LDTFs such as PU.1, IRF8, MAFB, and C/EBP family members. These strategies can generate MGLs within days and are readily scalable, making them well suited for high-throughput applications. However, they require additional engineering steps to introduce inducible TF cassettes or rely on viral gene delivery, and implementing controlled gene-switch systems adds further practical and technical constraints ( Csatári et al., 2024 ). In the context of neurodegenerative disease modeling, ontogeny-guided, chemically defined protocols have formed the basis of many widely used MGL models and are broadly implemented in monoculture, co-culture, organoid, and xenotransplantation paradigms to examine microglial phenotypic changes and their roles in disease. By contrast, TF-mediated strategies are particularly advantageous for high-throughput genetic or pharmacological screens aimed at identifying modulators of microglial phenotypes, including inflammatory or phagocytic responses. Each strategy therefore offers complementary strengths, and previous reviews provide detailed side-by-side comparisons of available protocols ( Csatári et al., 2024 ; Speicher et al., 2019 ). Environment-mimicking signals CSF1R signaling A colony-stimulating factor 1 receptor (CSF1R) signaling pathway is indispensable for the development, survival, and maintenance of microglia. CSF1R is uniquely expressed by microglia in the CNS under physiological conditions and mediates signals from two ligands, interleukin-34 (IL-34), and colony-stimulating factor 1 (CSF-1, also known as M-CSF) ( Ginhoux et al., 2010 ; Wang et al., 2012 ). Although structurally similar, these cytokines differ in cellular origin; IL-34 is primarily produced by neurons, whereas CSF-1 is secreted by astrocytes and oligodendrocytes, and exerts regionally distinct roles in maintaining microglial homeostasis within gray and white matter, respectively ( Easley-Neal et al., 2019 ; Paolicelli et al., 2022 ). Genetic ablation of Csf1r results in complete loss of microglia, underscoring its indispensable role in YS-derived macrophage lineages ( Ginhoux et al., 2010 ). Notably, Csf1 op/op mice retained a small population of microglia ( Blevins and Fedoroff, 1995 ), suggesting a compensatory role for IL-34 in the absence of CSF-1. Pharmacological inhibition of CSF1R in adult mice leads to the depletion of ∼99% of microglia within the CNS; however, withdrawal of the inhibitor enables complete repopulation within 1 week ( Elmore et al., 2014 ). Importantly, in xenotransplantation models using human MGLs (xMGs), successful engraftment requires human-specific CSF-1 because of species-specific ligand-receptor incompatibilities ( Hasselmann et al., 2019 ). Therefore, activation of the CSF1R signaling pathway through the provision of CSF-1 and/or IL-34 is essential for in vitro MGL differentiation, supporting their survival and maintenance. Several independent studies have demonstrated that TGF-β1 is indispensable for microglial development and homeostasis both in vitro and in vivo . Butovsky et al. (2014) reported a loss of microglia in the CNS of adult CNS-specific Tgfb1 −/− mice and found that only cultured adult microglia exposed to TGF-β1 reacquired the transcriptional profile of freshly isolated microglia. Consistently, Utz et al. (2020) showed a reduction in microglial numbers in Tgfbr2 -deficient mice, supporting the notion that microglia, but not border-associated macrophages (BAMs), require TGF-β signaling for their development. In line with these in vivo findings, Abud et al. (2017) demonstrated that withdrawal of TGF-β1 from MGL cultures triggered widespread transcriptomic changes, with over 1,500 genes differentially expressed within 24 h. Notably, several microglial signature genes, including P2RY12 , TGFBR1 , and CD33 , were downregulated, emphasizing the essential role of TGF-β1 in maintaining microglial transcriptional identity in vitro . At the epigenetic level, Fixsen et al. (2023) showed that SMAD4, a central mediator of the TGF-β signaling pathway, activates a conserved microglia-specific super-enhancer that regulates Sall1 expression, a key transcriptional regulator of microglial identity. Collectively, these findings underscore that TGF-β1 is not merely supportive, but is essential for establishing and preserving bona fide microglial identity. Therefore, its inclusion in in vitro differentiation protocols should be considered fundamental for faithfully recapitulating the native microglial phenotype. Disease models using microglia-like cells MGLs have emerged as a valuable tool for modeling neurodegenerative diseases, allowing researchers to dissect human microglial responses to disease-associated stimuli and genetic variants. As illustrated in Figure 3 , current MGL workflows generally involve several key stages: the generation of patient-derived or isogenic gene-edited hPSCs, their differentiation into MGLs by chemically defined or TF-mediated approaches, and the subsequent application of these cells across in vitro and in vivo disease-modeling platforms. These systems support a broad array of functional readouts, including analyses of cellular morphology, phagocytosis, chemotactic migration, and cytokine production, and collectively provide a foundation for mechanistic investigations of human-specific microglial phenotypes and responses, offering a powerful platform for evaluating the functional impact of risk variants associated with neurodegenerative diseases in a human-relevant context. Given that a large proportion of MGL-based studies to date have focused on AD, this section provides a detailed overview of AD research followed by a concise summary of studies addressing other neurodegenerative diseases such as PD, ALS/FTD, and CSF1R-related adult-onset leukoencephalopathy with axonal spheroids and pigmented glia (CSF1R-ALSP). AD AD is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder clinically characterized by cognitive decline and memory loss, and it is neuropathologically defined by the accumulation of Aβ plaques and neurofibrillary tangles composed of hyperphosphorylated tau protein. Numerous studies have been conducted on the development of disease models and drug discovery research utilizing neural cells derived from iPSCs from patients with AD ( Kondo et al., 2013 , 2017 , 2021 , 2022 ). However, GWAS have revealed that many of the genetic risk loci for late onset AD are highly enriched in genes preferentially expressed by microglia ( Schwabe et al., 2020 ; Wightman et al., 2021 ; Bellenguez et al., 2022 ; Sudwarts and Thinakaran, 2023 ), shifting the research focus toward microglia as central modulators of AD pathogenesis. Despite the identification of many microglia-relevant AD risk variants, most studies have focused on variants of APOE and TREM2 ( Chen and Colonna, 2021 ), which are discussed in detail below. APOE APOE is the strongest susceptibility gene for late onset AD, with the ε4 allele conferring a significantly elevated disease risk relative to ε3 and ε2. APOE encodes a lipid transport protein predominantly expressed in astrocytes under physiological conditions. However, its expression becomes upregulated in microglia under pathological or stressed conditions ( Xu et al., 2006 ; Mathys et al., 2019 ; Jackson et al., 2024 ). MGLs offer a valuable in vitro model for studying APOE -associated microglial function in the human genetic context. Compared with human APOE -targeted replacement (TR) or knock-in mice, which rely on murine promoter-5′ untranslated regions and thus may not faithfully recapitulate human transcriptional regulation ( Maloney et al., 2007 ; Tcw et al., 2022 ), MGLs preserve human-specific cis -regulatory elements and transcriptional architecture. Several studies have demonstrated genotype-dependent effects of APOE on MGLs. Lin et al. (2018) first showed that ε4 MGLs exhibit increased inflammatory gene expression, reduced process complexity, and impaired phagocytosis of Aβ 42 in monoculture, with diminished Aβ clearance in cerebral organoid models. Similarly, Konttinen et al. (2019) found that ε4 MGLs display impaired migration, reduced phagocytosis, and a heightened inflammatory response. Previous studies have established lipid dysregulation as a central mechanism of ε4-induced microglial dysfunction, highlighting the accumulation of lipid droplets (LDs) as a critical pathological feature. ε4 MGLs display elevated LD levels, suppressed lysosomal and lipid efflux gene expression, and a pronounced shift toward glycolytic metabolism, impairing their ability to support neuronal activity and promote a proinflammatory state ( Tcw et al., 2022 ; Victor et al., 2022 ). LD-burdened ε4 MGLs show diminished responsiveness to neuronal cues and upregulation of Acyl-Coa synthetase (ACSL1), a key enzyme involved in triglyceride synthesis. Pharmacological inhibition of ACSL1 effectively reduces LD accumulation and restores microglial surveillance of neuronal signals, supporting the hypothesis that LD build-up contributes directly to microglial dysfunction and impaired neuron-microglia crosstalk ( Victor et al., 2022 ). Such lipid dysregulation may perturb microglial energetic and signaling equilibrium, thereby potentially biasing APOE ε4 microglia toward altered or maladaptive cellular states rather than fully engaging DAM- or HLA-related transcriptional signatures defined in “ cellular states ” section. Expanding on these findings, Haney et al. (2024) revealed that fibrillar Aβ exposure triggers APOE genotype-dependent LD formation in MGLs, with ε4 cells exhibiting markedly higher LD content. Crucially, LD-high ε4 MGLs secreted neurotoxic factors that induced tau phosphorylation and neuronal apoptosis, whereas the conditioned media from LD-low MGLs did not. This suggests a toxic gain-of-function mechanism unique to ε4, in which the interaction between ε4 and lipid metabolism exacerbates downstream tau pathology and neurodegeneration. Moreover, phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K) inhibition attenuated LD accumulation and associated neurotoxicity, suggesting a potential therapeutic strategy for counteracting ε4-driven pathology. Consistent with previous findings implicating LD accumulation in ε4-associated pathology, a research project of the rare protective APOE variant, the Christchurch mutation (R136S), provided further support for the link between reduced LD burden and protection against AD pathology. Sun et al. (2024) found that MGLs carrying this allele were resistant to Aβ-induced LD formation, lipid peroxidation, and ferroptosis, while simultaneously exhibiting enhanced Aβ clearance and reduced tau pathology. Building upon growing evidence that APOE genotype shapes microglial lipid metabolism, Meier et al. (2025) developed a non-viral arrayed CRISPR screening platform in APOE ε3 and knockout MGLs to uncover mechanistic regulators of intracellular lipid handling. Motivated by genotype-dependent differences in LD accumulation, they performed a targeted CRISPR knockout screen of 334 genes involved in lipid homeostasis and neurodegeneration. The screen revealed both positive and negative regulators of lipid accumulation, some of which function independently of the APOE genotype. Notably, genes within the mTORC1 (mammalian target of rapamycin complex 1) pathway, including TSC2 and RHEB , bidirectionally controlled the LD levels, highlighting mTORC1 as a central axis in microglial lipid homeostasis and a potential therapeutic target. While these findings posit that LDs are useful targets in ε4-associated pathology, caution is warranted. LDs are not merely pathological byproducts; they serve important physiological functions, including protection against lipid peroxidation and the regulation of immune responses ( Bailey et al., 2015 ; Bosch et al., 2021 ). Therefore, the indiscriminate depletion of LDs in microglia could interfere with their essential functions. Future therapeutic strategies must balance the need to alleviate pathological lipid accumulation while preserving the physiological role of LDs. In summary, the convergence of evidence from genetic, pharmacological, and mechanistic studies of MGLs highlights LD accumulation as a key mediator of ε4 microglial dysfunction. However, the complex and context-dependent functions of LDs underscore the need for nuanced approaches for targeting lipid metabolism in AD. Future research should focus on elucidating the molecular mechanisms by which ApoE modulates lipid metabolism in microglia and identifying context-specific targets that preserve beneficial lipid signaling while attenuating disease-associated lipid accumulation. In addition to the Christchurch variant, other rare APOE protective variants such as V236E and R251G should be investigated to elucidate how they modulate ApoE function, prevent key pathological features of AD, and guide the design of therapeutics that mimic their protective effects ( Medway et al., 2014 ; Le Guen et al., 2022 ). TREM2 Triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2 (TREM2). TREM2 is a myeloid cell-specific receptor belonging to the immunoglobulin superfamily. It functions as a key component of microglial sensomes, enabling microglia to recognize and respond to phospholipids, Aβ, ApoE, and other damage-associated ligands ( Daws et al., 2003 ; Wang et al., 2015 ; Zhao et al., 2018 ; McQuade et al., 2020 ). Intracellular signaling by TREM2 is mediated through its adaptor protein TYROBP (also known as DNAX-activating protein of 12 kDa [DAP12]), which activates downstream effectors, such as spleen-associated tyrosine kinase (SYK), extracellular signal regulated kinase (ERK), and phospholipase C-gamma-2 (PLCG2) ( McQuade et al., 2020 ). MGLs are pivotal in characterizing the functional consequences of AD-associated TREM2 variants. Previous studies have demonstrated that missense mutations, such as T66M and W50C, hinder protein maturation, resulting in partial signaling deficits with mild phagocytic impairment ( Brownjohn et al., 2018 ; Garcia-Reitboeck et al., 2018 ). These findings suggest that the AD risk in TREM2 variant carriers may arise from slow cumulative dysfunction rather than complete receptor loss. Subsequent studies have shown that R47H microglia exhibit defective immunometabolic switching and fail to upregulate glycolysis or spare mitochondrial respiration under inflammatory stress ( Piers et al., 2020 ). Notably, supplementation with tricarboxylic acid (TCA) intermediates (citrate or succinate) reverses these metabolic shortfalls and improves the neuroprotective capacity in neuron-microglia co-cultures, suggesting a potential intervention strategy ( Vasilopoulou et al., 2025 ). Crucially, this metabolic inflexibility may constrain the ability of R47H microglia to transition from homeostatic or intermediate states toward DAM or CRM phenotypes described in “ cellular states ” section, both of which have been associated with disease contexts. The inflammatory phenotype of R47H microglia is complex and appears to be influenced by cellular environment and disease stage. Although Cosker et al. (2021) reported reduced activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome, another study observed increased proinflammatory gene expression and increased phagocytic activity in R47H cells ( Penney et al., 2024 ). This inconsistency reflects the dynamic nature of microglial responses and highlights the importance of contextual factors in shaping the TREM2 function. Disruption of calcium signaling has also been observed in TREM2-deficient microglia. TREM2 knockout MGLs show heightened Ca 2+ transients downstream of purinergic receptors P2Y 12 and P2Y 13 , resulting in impaired directional chemotaxis. Pharmacological inhibition of purinergic signaling restores proper migration toward injury cues, suggesting that modulation of the purinergic or Ca 2+ signaling pathways may have therapeutic relevance ( Jairaman et al., 2022 ). Overall, these findings highlight TREM2 as a key regulator of microglial metabolism and immune functions. Variants such as R47H do not simply reduce TREM2 activity but instead reprogram microglial signaling in complex, context-dependent ways. This duality underscores the need for stage-specific personalized interventions. Several in vitro therapeutic approaches have shown promising results. Leveraging MGLs, which recapitulate patient-specific genetic backgrounds, offers a powerful platform for evaluating these strategies. PD PD is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by the loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra of the midbrain and the pathological accumulation of α-synuclein (αS). Accumulating evidence has indicated that several key PD-associated genes, such as SNCA and LRRK2 , are involved in pathways that regulate inflammation, implicating immune cells, including microglia, in PD pathogenesis. Moreover, these genes are known to be expressed in microglia and astrocytes, further supporting a role for glial cells in the disease process ( Dzamko et al., 2015 ; Haenseler et al., 2017b ). One of the central themes emerging from MGL-based studies is the defective handling of αS burden by microglia. Haenseler et al. (2017b) demonstrated that MGLs derived from PD patients with SNCA triplication accumulate excessive αS and exhibit impaired phagocytosis and modest cytokine dysregulation. Interestingly, SNCA A53T mutation did not produce similar phenotypes, suggesting that the αS protein burden itself, rather than the mutation type, is the major determinant of microglial dysfunction. In support of this, treatment of control MGLs with exogenous αS monomers recapitulated the phagocytic and inflammatory defects. Mechanistically, αS was cleared by lysosomal and proteasomal pathways; however, when overwhelmed, these systems failed to maintain protein homeostasis, implicating the saturation of degradative capacity in disease progression. In addition to protein clearance, PD microglia are also characterized by dysregulated immune signaling. Panagiotakopoulou et al. (2020) highlighted the role of inflammatory priming in exacerbating microglial dysfunction in the context of LRRK2 mutations. IFN-γ, a proinflammatory cytokine elevated in PD brains, upregulated LRRK2 expression in both wild-type and G2019S mutant MGLs. In the mutant context, IFN-γ impaired the nuclear translocation of key TFs such as NFAT1 and NF-κB p65, disrupting the immune response program. These cells secreted an altered cytokine profile and exhibited heightened phagocytic activity alongside a metabolic shift toward glycolysis, consistent with a reactive immune phenotype. These findings suggest that inflammatory cues synergize with genetic risk factors to drive aberrant microglial activation. Transcriptomic and functional reprogramming of microglia bearing PD-associated mutations was further detailed by Ohtonen et al. (2023) , who profiled LRRK2 G2019S MGLs and compared them to patient-derived postmortem brain tissue. Strikingly, these mutant microglia adopted a transcriptomic profile greatly similar to a minor (∼5.4%) but distinct “consensus PD microglia” subpopulation observed in midbrain samples from PD patients. These cells showed downregulation of homeostatic markers (e.g., TMEM119 and MEF2A ) and upregulation of stress- and clearance-related genes, including WASF2 (phagocytosis), CAT and CTSF (oxidative stress), and PSMD5 (proteasome function). Functionally, the G2019S MGLs exhibited impaired phagocytosis, elevated secretion of inflammatory cytokines, and mitochondrial dysfunction, with sex-specific differences. Collectively, studies using MGLs have demonstrated that mutations in SNCA and LRRK2 disrupt microglial homeostasis by impaired protein clearance, aberrant cytokine responses, and altered metabolism. While SNCA triplication increases the αS burden and causes phagocytic deficits, LRRK2 G2019S induces transcriptional and functional alterations. Notably, discrepancies in reported phagocytic phenotypes likely stem from differences in MGL derivation protocols (e.g., serum-containing vs. serum-free media), microglial priming conditions, and donor sex ( Ohtonen et al., 2023 ). These variables highlight the complexity of modeling human microglia and underscore the need for standardized protocols in iPSC-derived disease models. Future studies incorporating 3D co-culture systems and in vivo transplantation approaches will help clarify how microglial dysfunction contributes to neurodegeneration in the human PD brain. ALS/FTD ALS and FTD are fatal, progressive neurodegenerative disorders that were once studied as distinct clinical entities. However, converging evidence across genetic, pathological, and clinical domains now supports the view that ALS and FTD represent two ends of a disease spectrum, commonly referred to as ALS/FTD ( Chen et al., 2018 ). ALS is characterized by the selective degeneration of upper and lower motor neurons, resulting in progressive muscle weakness, atrophy, and ultimately respiratory failure. In contrast, FTD is defined by the degeneration of the frontal and temporal lobes of the brain, leading to profound changes in personality, executive dysfunction, and language impairment ( Nguyen et al., 2018 ). Genetically, several mutations including hexanucleotide repeat expansions (HRE) in C9orf72 are shared between ALS and FTD patients, underscoring a molecular continuum between the two conditions. Pathologically, cytoplasmic aggregation of TDP-43 protein is a hallmark of the majority of ALS and FTD cases, further substantiating their shared etiology. However, other genes are more specifically associated with only one of the two diseases, such as SOD1 and PFN1 for ALS, or GRN for FTD ( Ling et al., 2013 ; Nguyen et al., 2018 ). Although traditionally conceptualized from a neuron-centric perspective, increasing evidence underscores the critical role of non-cell-autonomous mechanisms, including those mediated by microglia, in driving ALS/FTD progression ( Chen et al., 2018 ; Giacomelli et al., 2022 ; Serio and Patani, 2018 ). Previous studies using MGLs have provided key mechanistic insights into how mutations in the ALS/FTD-associated genes disrupt microglial homeostasis, highlighting defects in autophagy, lysosomal processing, and inflammatory regulation, and ultimately leading to neurodegeneration. One of the most consistent findings among studies is the disruption of the autophagy-lysosome pathway. In C9orf72 mutant and knockout microglia, Banerjee et al. (2023) identified impaired autophagy initiation due to the loss of interaction between C9orf72 and key autophagy regulators, such as SMCR8 and RAB5, leading to reduced autophagosome formation. This impairment in autophagy flux, as evidenced by defective p62/LC3 turnover, resulted in sustained activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome and NF-κB signaling, particularly upon LPS stimulation. Pharmacological enhancement of autophagy using rapamycin significantly improved phagocytosis, reduced cytokine production, and improved motor neuron survival in co-culture, establishing a clear causal link between disrupted autophagy and neurotoxicity in C9orf72 mutant microglia. Similarly, Funes et al. (2024) demonstrated that PFN1 mutations (C71G and M114T) disrupt vesicular degradation without impairing initial phagocytic uptake. Despite normal lysosomal pH and hydrolytic enzyme activity, PFN1 mutant MGLs showed perinuclear clustering of LAMP1-positive lysosomes and accumulation of autophagic markers (p62, LC3), suggesting impaired cargo clearance. Mechanistically, the M114T mutation increased the binding affinity to PI3P, a critical signaling lipid for autophagic and endosomal trafficking. Treatment with rapamycin restored vesicular degradation and normalized p62 levels, mirroring findings in C9orf72 models and reinforcing the notion that impaired vesicle dynamics is a convergent mechanism in ALS/FTD-microglia. Other investigations have highlighted distinct inflammatory and toxic properties of C9orf72 mutant microglia. Vahsen et al. (2023) reported that LPS-primed C9orf72 mutant MGLs not only exhibited transcriptomic dysregulation in immune and ER-related pathways but also secreted abnormally high levels of matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP9), an endopeptidase previously shown to exert neurotoxic effects in ALS animal models based on SOD1 or TDP-43 pathology ( Kaplan et al., 2014 ; Spiller et al., 2019 ). Importantly, this MMP9 upregulation occurred via a mechanism independent of C9orf72 haploinsufficiency, indicating a gain-of-function mechanism. In co-culture with healthy motor neurons, LPS-primed C9orf72 mutant microglia induced neurodegeneration, an effect reversed by MMP9 inhibition. The authors further identified dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP4) as a downstream target of MMP9 and a potential soluble biomarker for dysfunctional microglia. In line with these findings, Sonustun et al. (2025) showed that IL-6 and IL-8 secreted from C9orf72 mutant MGLs are key drivers of motor neuron toxicity in a 3D spinal microtissue model. Treatment with telmisartan effectively reduced these proinflammatory cytokines and rescued motor neuron survival, further reinforcing the critical role of microglia-mediated inflammation in disease progression. In contrast to these pronounced phenotypes, Lorenzini et al. (2023) observed that monocultures of C9orf72 -MGLs displayed relatively modest intrinsic alterations despite harboring classic pathological hallmarks such as HRE-associated RNA foci and poly-(GP) DPR protein expression. While C9orf72 protein levels were reduced, transcript levels were largely unaltered. Under basal conditions and following LPS stimulation, these microglia showed mainly normal cytokine release and transcriptome profiles. Only select patient lines exhibited altered phagocytic activity toward synthetic Aβ 1–40 , and synaptosome uptake remained intact. The absence of overt TDP-43 pathology and the subtle nature of observed changes suggest that additional environmental stressors or diseased neural context may be required to fully unmask pathogenic phenotypes in C9orf72 microglia. Notably, Vahsen et al. (2023) suggested that the discrepancies between their findings and those of Lorenzini et al. (2023) may be attributed to differences in their experimental designs, and particularly the duration of inflammatory priming. Studies using GRN -deficient MGLs have further expanded the landscape of microglial dysfunction in FTD. Loss of progranulin (PGRN) in GRN −/− MGLs leads to prominent lysosomal defects, including enlarged LAMP1-positive endolysosomal compartments filled with DQ-BSA and increased Galectin-3 recruitment to endolysosomal membranes, reflecting membrane rupture ( Logan et al., 2021 ). Importantly, expression of lysosomal dysfunction-related genes such as CTSD , NPC2 , and CD68 is also elevated in GRN -deficient microglia ( Reifschneider et al., 2022 ). In parallel, GRN -deficient MGLs exhibit a pronounced inflammatory phenotype characterized by the upregulation of DAM genes including TREM2 , APOE , and GPNMB , as well as increased phagocytic activity. Functional studies demonstrate that this activation is largely dependent on TREM2-DAP12 signaling, as antagonistic anti-TREM2 antibodies significantly reduce both DAM marker expression and phagocytic activity. However, this intervention does not reverse underlying lysosomal abnormalities, suggesting parallel but mechanistically distinct pathways driving lysosomal dysfunction and immune activation ( Reifschneider et al., 2022 ). Taken together, these studies underscore the complex and multifactorial nature of microglial dysfunction in ALS/FTD. Autophagy-lysosome impairment emerges as a convergent mechanism across C9orf72 , PFN1 , and GRN MGL models, albeit through distinct molecular routes, ranging from impaired autophagy initiation ( C9orf72 ) to disrupted vesicle clearance ( PFN1 ) and lysosomal membrane damage ( GRN ). Meanwhile, gain-of-function mechanisms such as MMP9 overproduction confer additional neurotoxic properties independent of protein haploinsufficiency. These findings reinforce the need for combinatorial therapeutic strategies targeting both autophagy lysosomal pathways and inflammatory mediators to mitigate microglia-driven neurodegeneration in ALS/FTD. CSF1R-related adult-onset leukoencephalopathy with axonal spheroids and pigmented glia (CSF1R-ALSP) CSF1R-ALSP is a rare, autosomal dominant neurodegenerative disease caused by heterozygous loss-of-function mutations in the CSF1R gene. These mutations impair microglial survival and function, leading to progressive demyelination, axonal damage, and gliosis ( Konno et al., 2017 , 2018 ). While initially considered ultra-rare, previous genetic studies suggest that pathogenic CSF1R variants may be more prevalent than expected, highlighting a critical need for effective therapies ( Wade et al., 2024 ; Larson et al., 2025a ). One of the principal challenges in CSF1R-ALSP research has been the lack of animal models that accurately replicate the phenotypes observed in human disease. In contrast to the heterozygous CSF1R loss-of-function seen in ALSP patients, rodent models often require homozygous deletion or use of CSF1R enhancer knockouts (e.g., ΔFIRE mice) to exhibit any overt CNS phenotype. However, these models typically show complete microglial loss and slow progression that poorly mirrors the human pathological cascade ( Chitu et al., 2015 ; Arreola et al., 2021 ; Patkar et al., 2021 ; Chadarevian et al., 2024 ; Larson et al., 2025b ). This disparity has necessitated a pivot toward human in vitro systems, particularly MGLs, that can recapitulate functional impairments ( Larson et al., 2025a ). Larson et al. (2025a) tested the therapeutic hypothesis that activating TREM2, which shares downstream signaling pathways with CSF1R, including SYK and PI3K, could compensate for CSF1R loss-of-function. The authors evaluated iluzanebart, a human monoclonal antibody that selectively activates human TREM2, in both wild-type and CSF1R mutant MGLs. Iluzanebart potently activated TREM2 signaling, as measured by SYK phosphorylation, and its specificity was confirmed using TREM2 knockout MGLs. Notably, iluzanebart increased the surface expression of CSF1R and elevated soluble CSF1R levels in the culture medium, effects fully dependent on TREM2 activation. When MGLs were challenged with CSF1R inhibition via PLX5622 or CSF1 ligand withdrawal, iluzanebart restored viability and morphology, and reduced caspase 3/7 activity. Most importantly, in MGLs carrying the CSF1R I794T +/− mutation, iluzanebart rescued phosphorylated CSF1R levels and normalized cell viability without altering the total CSF1R protein, indicating enhanced receptor activation. These results suggest that TREM2 agonism can restore microglial function in the context of CSF1R deficiency by reinforcing overlapping pathways, thereby providing strong preclinical rationale for the use of iluzanebart in CSF1R-ALSP. Altogether, this work highlights the indispensable role of MGLs in modeling microglial diseases and accelerating therapy development. For disorders like CSF1R-ALSP, where human pathology is poorly recapitulated in animals, MGLs provide a uniquely suitable platform for evaluating targeted, mechanism-based therapies. Taken together, these MGL-based disease models demonstrate that human microglia display variant-specific functional alterations implicated in the pathogenesis of diverse neurodegenerative diseases, with disease-associated variants disrupting core functions such as inflammatory signaling, phagocytosis, migration, as well as impairing lysosomal function and inducing metabolic switching, changes that are often observable even under basal conditions. Importantly, although these disorders frequently converge on impairment of core microglial homeostatic functions, the underlying pathogenic mechanisms diverge: AD-associated variants prominently perturb lipid homeostasis and immunometabolic adaptability; PD-linked mutations often compromise mitochondrial homeostasis and alter inflammatory-metabolic reprogramming; and ALS/FTD mutations commonly disrupt autophagy-lysosome function and vesicular homeostasis. While some phenotypes parallel those reported in mouse studies, MGLs are particularly valuable for revealing human-specific microglial dysfunctions that are not faithfully reproduced in rodents ( Koppers et al., 2015 ; Xiang et al., 2018 ). The case of CSF1R-ALSP further illustrates this species gap, highlighting not only the ability of MGLs to model disease-relevant phenotypes but also their utility for mechanistic and therapeutic evaluation in contexts where rodent models fail to reproduce the corresponding microglial phenotypes. These observations emphasize the unique value of MGLs as a human-relevant system for defining microglial roles in neurodegenerative diseases and assessing potential therapeutic approaches. From in vitro limitations to in vivo human microglia models As noted in “ generation of hPSC-derived microglia-like cells ” section , microglia are highly plastic and dynamically responsive to environmental cues. This inherent plasticity inevitably introduces limitations in in vitro modeling, as evidenced by the finding that ex vivo microglia undergo rapid transcriptomic alterations within a few hours of isolation from the brain, resulting in a significant transcriptional discrepancy between cultured and in vivo microglia ( Gosselin et al., 2017 ). Xenotransplantation models have been developed to bridge this gap, wherein human MGLs or their precursors are engrafted into the brains of immunodeficient mice, thereby enabling the study of human microglial behavior in a physiologically relevant in vivo environment ( Hasselmann et al., 2019 ; McQuade et al., 2020 ; Claes et al., 2021 ; Parajuli et al., 2021 ; Baligács et al., 2024 ; Mancuso et al., 2024 ; Pereira et al., 2024 ). To date, such xenotransplantation approaches have primarily been applied in the context of AD. Accordingly, we will focus on key findings from these AD-related studies, which provide insight into how human microglia respond to disease-relevant cues in vivo . In these AD-related xenotransplantation studies, human microglia have been shown to exhibit complex, multi-pronged transcriptional responses to Aβ pathology that only partially overlap ∼20%–25% with murine DAM signatures. Unique human-specific states have been identified: the antigen-presenting response (HLA), marked by genes such as HLA-DRA and HLA-DPA1 , and the cytokine-responsive state (CRM), characterized by the upregulation of cytokine and chemokine genes such as CCL4 , CCL3 , and IL1B . These states are absent in mouse microglia in the context of Aβ pathology, highlighting species-specific responses ( Mancuso et al., 2024 ). Functionally, another independent study showed that human microglia play stage-dependent roles in the progression of Aβ pathology; homeostatic microglia contribute to early plaque seeding, while activated microglia later mediate plaque compaction, thereby limiting Aβ toxicity ( Baligács et al., 2024 ). These findings underscore the importance of considering microglial states and disease stages in order to understand AD pathology and develop effective therapeutics. Importantly, these xenotransplantation studies have enabled the functional interrogation of AD-associated genetic variants in human microglia. MGLs carrying the TREM2 R47H mutation exhibited impaired clustering around Aβ plaques, reduced compaction, upregulation of multiple complement cascade components, and decreased expression of markers such as CD9 and APOE , phenotypes consistent with murine models and human AD pathology ( Hasselmann et al., 2019 ; McQuade et al., 2020 ; Claes et al., 2021 ; Penney et al., 2024 ). Interestingly, while the R47H mutation increases lipid accumulation in vitro ( Andreone et al., 2020 ), in vivo data have shown reduced LDs in R47H due to impaired plaque proximity ( Claes et al., 2021 ). This discrepancy highlights the importance of the spatial context and may reflect the influence of additional in vivo- specific factors, such as interactions with other cell types or localized plaque-associated signals, which are absent in vitro . Another key insight revealed in vivo was the species-specific role of APOE . Although APOE is essential for the DAM transition in mice ( Keren-Shaul et al., 2017 ; Krasemann et al., 2017 ), its deletion in human microglia had little effect on DAM or CRM profiles, but significantly reduced the HLA-state population. This indicates that, while APOE is dispensable for the initial microglial response to Aβ, it is required for the subsequent transition from DAM to HLA. In contrast, TREM2 robustly drives both DAM and HLA programs, highlighting a hierarchical relationship wherein TREM2 initiates activation, and APOE modulates later diversification. Notably, the presence of APOE ε4 or TREM2 R47H blunted the HLA response, suggesting that this state may play a protective role ( Mancuso et al., 2024 ). These findings demonstrated that genetic risk factors modulate human microglial transcriptional plasticity and stage-specific functions in vivo . Collectively, these studies demonstrate that xenotransplantation models serve as a critical bridge across the species divide, revealing human-specific microglial behaviors that are not readily captured in murine systems. While current applications have focused largely on AD, future extension of this model to other neurodegenerative diseases, such as PD or ALS/FTD, holds promise for uncovering disease-specific microglial responses and advancing translational insights. Future perspectives Despite significant advances in the generation and application of human MGLs, several limitations remain that constrain their fidelity in modeling the human microglial biology and their utility in recapitulating the complex pathophysiology of neurodegenerative diseases ( Figure 1 ). One of the most prominent challenges is the developmental immaturity of MGLs, which appears to arise from two interrelated factors: intrinsic limitations in developmental progression and insufficient exposure to brain-specific environmental cues. Transcriptomic analyses have revealed that MGLs in vitro more closely resemble fetal-stage microglia than their postnatal counterparts, limiting their translational relevance for modeling age-associated neurodegenerative diseases such as AD and PD ( Han et al., 2023 ). This immature phenotype likely reflects incomplete epigenetic maturation, which in vivo is driven by temporally coordinated, stage-specific chromatin remodeling. A key regulator of this process is IRF8, which is indispensable in establishing an epigenetic landscape that supports postnatal microglial gene expression. Intriguingly, research has demonstrated that the stepwise increase in IRF8 genomic occupancy during development occurs independently of changes in IRF8 expression levels, suggesting that postnatal chromatin accessibility is governed by additional yet-to-be-defined epigenetic mechanisms ( Saeki et al., 2024 ). These insights underscore the need for epigenetic engineering strategies such as CRISPR-based enhancer modulation or chromatin remodeling factor induction to better replicate in vivo developmental trajectories and promote the acquisition of mature microglial identity in vitro . Beyond epigenetic engineering, targeted activation of maturation-associated transcriptional programs could help overcome the fetal-like identity of current MGLs ( Csatári et al., 2024 ), although the viability and effectiveness of this approach remain to be established. Beyond direct molecular editing, the physical properties of the microenvironment may fundamentally constrain the functional maturation of MGLs. Previous studies highlight mechanotransduction as a critical regulator of microglial phenotypes, demonstrating that substrate stiffness directly influences activation states, reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, and metabolic function ( Liu et al., 2023 ). This introduces the intriguing possibility that the “maturation barrier” restricting MGLs to a fetal-like state stems partly from the absence of physiological mechanical cues in standard culture systems. Therefore, future engineering strategies should consider integrating biophysical cues, such as tuning substrate stiffness or utilizing 3D scaffolds, to physically instruct cellular states. Such approaches could facilitate the acquisition of more physiologically relevant phenotypes, potentially bridging the gap between intrinsic genetic programming and environmental instruction. Compounding this developmental limitation is the absence of critical brain-derived environmental cues in the in vitro differentiation protocols. Although cytokine supplementation and co-culture strategies have been employed, these interventions fail to recreate the complex multicellular microenvironment of the CNS. Notably, TGF-β signaling has been shown to be insufficient for initiating or sustaining microglial identity, as the expression of microglia-specific genes rapidly declines once microglia are removed from the brain. These observations strongly imply that microglia rely on additional tissue-specific environmental cues to activate the enhancer landscape that stabilizes core identity genes ( Fixsen et al., 2023 ; Gosselin et al., 2017 ). Extension of these findings to in vitro differentiation systems leads to the suggestion that current protocols lack key brain-derived signals and that additional, yet-unidentified factors are required to achieve complete microglial maturation. In parallel with these limitations, the field has moved beyond the oversimplified M1/M2 paradigm, driven largely by advances in single-cell transcriptomics that have revealed a far richer landscape of microglial states. As additional states have been identified, microglia have been subdivided into more distinct subpopulations with diverse functional properties, including plaque-associated CD28 + PU.1 low microglia ( Ayata et al., 2025 ), which exhibit regulatory T cell-like features, and clonally expanding microglia located near plaques ( Ardura-Fabregat et al., 2025 ), each contributing differently to disease progression. Although several of these states have been characterized in vivo across multiple mouse studies, reconstituting them in vitro remains challenging. At present, aside from the induction of DAM-like signatures by exposing MGLs to brain substrates ( Dolan et al., 2023 ), efforts to reproduce heterogeneous microglial states in vitro remain limited. This gap indicates that future microglial modeling should not be limited to introducing genetic perturbations and assessing their effects at the bulk population level. Instead, it will be necessary to determine how specific microglial subpopulations emerge, how they are shaped by genetic or environmental factors, and how each state contributes to disease modulation. Systematic identification of microglial heterogeneity, together with functional evaluation of defined subpopulations, will be essential for establishing models that more accurately reflect the mechanisms underlying the pathogenesis of neurodegenerative diseases. Given the limitations of current in vitro systems, complementary approaches have turned to in vivo contexts to model human microglia under physiological conditions. To implement this approach, xenotransplantation models (xMGs) have been developed in which human MGLs or their precursors are engrafted into the brains of immunodeficient mice. These in vivo models provide a more physiological context by exposing MGLs to complex brain-derived cues from diverse neural and non-neural cell types. xMGs exhibit improved transcriptional fidelity and more functionally relevant responses compared to their in vitro counterparts, and importantly, have enabled the emergence and characterization of an expanded spectrum of microglial states. However, despite these advances, some important limitations remain. xMGs do not fully recapitulate regional CNS heterogeneity, and species-specific mismatches in ligand-receptor interactions may limit the fidelity of human microglial responses. Moreover, the absence of an adaptive immune system constrains the study of immune-microglia crosstalk, which is increasingly being recognized as a critical component of neurodegenerative disease pathophysiology ( Hasselmann et al., 2019 ; Claes et al., 2021 ; Mancuso et al., 2024 ). Together, these considerations highlight the need for microglial models that more accurately capture their developmental, environmental, and state-specific properties. Although current MGL systems provide a useful platform for disease modeling, their incomplete maturation and limited capacity to recapitulate diverse microglial states restrict their utility for mechanistic studies of neurodegeneration. Xenotransplantation approaches mitigate some of these constraints by exposing MGLs to in vivo cues and enabling the emergence of a wider range of microglial states, but species-specific signaling differences and the absence of adaptive immunity underscore their incomplete fidelity. Progress in the field will require coordinated efforts to modulate epigenetic programs, identify and reconstruct key features of the brain microenvironment, and develop human-compatible in vivo platforms capable of overcoming species-specific and immunological limitations, such as models incorporating immune reconstitution, thereby generating models that capture both the cellular fidelity and the context-dependent state dynamics and functions of human microglia. Establishing such platforms will be critical for defining microglial contributions across disease stages and for developing therapeutic strategies that target specific, disease-relevant microglial states across diverse neurodegenerative diseases.
