Disulfiram attenuates immobilization-induced knee joint contracture by suppressing Caspase-1/GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis

Joint contracture resulting from prolonged immobilization is a common complication characterized by pain, stiffness, and limited mobility. Although inflammation and tissue remodeling are known contributors, the underlying cellular mechanisms remain unclear. Here, we investigated the involvement of Caspase-1/GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis in immobilization-induced knee joint contracture and evaluated the therapeutic potential of disulfiram (DSF), an FDA-approved drug repurposed here based on its reported anti-pyroptotic activity. In a rat model of knee immobilization, we assessed joint mobility, histopathological changes, and molecular markers associated with pyroptosis. Immobilization significantly induced contracture, inflammatory infiltration, and activation of the Caspase-1/GSDMD pathway in the joint capsule. Treatment with DSF improved joint range of motion, reduced tissue damage, and reduced caspase-1/GSDMD-related pyroptosis markers and GSDMD cleavage. Notably, DSF decreased IL-1β and IL-18 protein levels without affecting their mRNA expression. Together, these findings support a role for Caspase-1/GSDMD-associated pyroptosis in immobilization-induced joint contracture and suggest that DSF alleviates contracture, at least in part, by modulating this pathway, providing new mechanistic insight and a potential therapeutic approach for preventing post-immobilization joint contracture. Introduction Joint immobilization is a common clinical strategy for managing musculoskeletal injuries by restricting movement to facilitate tissue repair. However, prolonged immobilization often leads to joint contracture, particularly arthrogenic contracture, which is primarily characterized by joint capsule fibrosis and poses a significant therapeutic challenge in clinical practice 1 – 3 . The knee joint, a major weight-bearing joint of the lower limb, is frequently immobilized in an extended position following injury, making it highly susceptible to extension-type contracture. This condition severely restricts essential functions such as walking and squatting 4 , 5 . Although existing interventions, including pharmacological, rehabilitative, and surgical approaches, have been employed, they often demonstrate limited efficacy in reversing established joint capsule fibrosis. The underlying pathology is largely attributed to the aberrant activation of fibroblasts and excessive deposition of extracellular matrix (ECM) 6 – 8 . Activated fibroblasts differentiate into contractile myofibroblasts, secreting substantial amounts of ECM components such as collagen, thereby disrupting tissue homeostasis and leading to the formation of rigid scar tissue 9 – 11 . Given that fibrosis progression is closely linked to cell death mechanisms, research on programmed cell death pathways may offer novel therapeutic insights for overcoming this challenge 12 , 13 . Recently, pyroptosis has emerged as a prominent inflammatory form of programmed cell death. This process is mediated by inflammasome activation, leading to the cleavage of caspase proteins, which subsequently trigger the cleavage of gasdermin D (GSDMD). As the final common effector downstream of inflammasome activation, GSDMD is cleaved by inflammatory Caspases at the junction between the N-terminal domain (GSDMD-NT) and the autoinhibitory C-terminal domain (GSDMD-CT) 14 , 15 . The cleaved GSDMD-NT forms membrane pores, resulting in cell rupture and the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-18 16 , 17 . These cytokines not only exacerbate local inflammation but also activate transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) signaling, promoting fibroblast proliferation and ECM synthesis, thereby creating a self-perpetuating cycle of inflammation and fibrosis 18 . While pyroptosis has been implicated in the pathogenesis of osteoarthritis and intervertebral disc degeneration, its role in immobilization-induced joint contracture remains unclear 19 – 21 . Based on these findings, we hypothesize that abnormal mechanical stress resulting from joint immobilization may activate pyroptotic pathways, leading to joint capsule fibrosis and contracture progression. Disulfiram, a well-established anti-alcoholism drug, has recently been identified as a selective pyroptosis inhibitor. It covalently modifies cysteine 191 of GSDMD, preventing its oligomerization and pore formation, thereby inhibiting cell membrane rupture and cytokine release. This unique mechanism suggests its potential as an anti-fibrotic agent 22 . In addition, DSF has been tested in several chronic inflammatory conditions, including obesity, atherosclerosis, and Alzheimer’s disease 23 – 25 . In this study, we aimed to establish a knee joint immobilization model to investigate the activation of pyroptosis in joint contracture and to evaluate the therapeutic effects of disulfiram in mitigating fibrosis through the inhibition of GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis. This study provides a theoretical foundation for developing novel therapeutic strategies that offer both joint protection and anti-fibrotic benefits, ultimately improving patient functional outcomes. Materials and methods Accordance statement All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines (ARRIVE 2.0) and regulations for animal research 26 . Animals This study utilized 18 male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats (3 months old, 250–300 g) obtained from the Experimental Animal Center of Anhui Medical University (Hefei, China) and this research was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Anhui Medical University (LLSC20221126). All rats were in good health and free of musculoskeletal disorders 27 . The rats were housed in a temperature-controlled room (24–25 °C) under a 12-hour light/dark cycle, with free access to standard chow and water, and three rats per cage. Experimental grouping and immobilization model After a one-week acclimatization period, the rats were randomly divided into three groups ( n = 6 per group): Control (Ctrl), Immobilization (IM), and Immobilization + Disulfiram (IM + DSF). The control group remained unrestrained and was allowed free movement in cages for four weeks. In the Immobilization (IM) and Immobilization + Disulfiram (IM + DSF) groups, the left knee joint was immobilized in full extension using molded aluminum splint (Patent No. ZL202120470158.0), developed by our research team. The splint was precisely cut and molded to fit the rat’s lower limb, with an inner lining of absorbent cotton fixed using instant adhesive to enhance stability and comfort. Before immobilization, anesthesia was induced via intraperitoneal injection of 2% pentobarbital sodium (40 mg/kg). The left knee was fully extended, and the splint was secured with the ankle in dorsiflexion while maintaining free movement of the hip joint. X-ray imaging was performed to confirm proper immobilization. Disulfiram (DSF; tetraethylthiuram disulfide) was purchased from MedChemExpress (MCE, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA; Cat. No. HY-B0240; stated purity 99.77% by HPLC). Both the Immobilization (IM) and Immobilization + Disulfiram (IM + DSF) groups underwent four weeks of immobilization, while the IM group received daily oral gavage of corn oil, the IM + DSF group received daily oral gavage of disulfiram at a dose of 25 mg/kg (6.25 mg/day), administered seven times per week 23 . The rats were observed daily for loosening of the immobilization device and health status to make timely adjustments. Measurement of joint range of motion (ROM) At the end of the fourth week, rats were euthanized via overdose anesthesia (150 mg/kg pentobarbital sodium). The left hind limb was completely disarticulated at the hip joint, and knee ROM was measured using a custom-designed joint mobility assessment device (Patent No. ZL202120996643.1) (Fig. 1 A), developed by our research team. During measurement, the lower leg was stabilized using a dial fixture, while an electromagnetic base and adjustable ring secured the femur to a platform (with positional adjustments based on anatomical requirements). The quadriceps femoris was severed prior to ROM measurement to remove muscular influence, following the method described by Yuan et al. 28 . The dial’s rotation angle was recorded via a pointer, and the applied torque was maintained at a standard 0.053 N·m, as determined in our previous studies 29 . ROM measurements were conducted independently by two blinded evaluators, with each limb measured three times for a total of six readings per rat, and the average value was recorded. Tissue preparation In the knee extension contracture model, the anterior joint capsule is a critical factor restricting knee flexion due to its dense connective tissue composition. After ROM measurements, anterior joint capsule samples were collected from each rat. The capsule was carefully dissected along the margin of the patella and knee joint to obtain a complete tissue sample, with careful removal of surrounding adipose tissue. Half of the medial joint capsule was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C overnight for histological analysis, while the remaining half (lateral side) were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C for Western blotting. Histological assessments included hematoxylin-eosin (H&amp;E) and Masson’s trichrome staining to evaluate inflammatory cell infiltration and fibrosis levels. Samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h to preserve tissue integrity and prevent degradation. Following graded ethanol dehydration, tissues were embedded in paraffin, sectioned at 5 μm thickness using a microtome, and mounted on slides for further analysis. H&amp;E staining Paraffin-embedded tissue sections were deparaffinized, stained with hematoxylin (Baso, Zhuhai, Guangdong, China), followed by eosin staining (Baso, Zhuhai, Guangdong, China), then cleared with xylene and mounted using neutral resin. Stained sections were examined under a light microscope (BX43F; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) at 400× magnification. Six random fields of view per section were selected for imaging and analysis. In the joint capsule, the primary cell types included myofibroblasts and fibroblasts, which were distinguished based on morphological characteristics. All cells were counted using ImageJ (NIH, USA) to assess the degree of inflammation. Masson staining Masson staining was performed using a commercial staining kit (Servicebio, Wuhan, Hubei, China). Tissue sections were deparaffinized and dehydrated before staining to evaluate collagen deposition. After staining, sections were dehydrated in anhydrous ethanol, cleared with xylene for five minutes, and mounted using neutral resin. For analysis, six random fields per section were imaged at 400× magnification using a light microscope (BX43F; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). The proportion of blue-stained areas was quantified to assess collagen deposition levels using ImageJ (NIH, USA). The images were first processed using the “Color Deconvolution” plugin with the Masson’s trichrome vector to isolate the blue collagen channel. A consistent threshold value was then applied across all images using the “Threshold” tool to distinguish collagen-positive areas. The threshold was calibrated using control samples to optimize signal-to-noise discrimination. The collagen-positive area was expressed as a percentage of the total tissue area. Western blotting Protein expression levels of GSDMD, Caspase-1, and β-actin were analyzed by Western blotting. Samples were ground into powder using a liquid nitrogen grinder, homogenized in RIPA lysis buffer (Cat: P0013B, Beyotime, China), centrifuged, and the supernatant collected. Protein quantification was performed using a BCA protein assay kit (Cat: P0398S, Beyotime, China). Next, an equivalent amount of protein from each sample pair was separated by SDS-PAGE on 10% polyacrylamide gels and electro-transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride membranes. Membranes were then incubated in TBST after blocking with 5% skim milk at room temperature for 2 h. The membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with rabbit anti-GSDMD antibody (1:1000; Cat: PA5-116815, Invitrogen, USA), rabbit anti-Caspase-1 antibody (1:1000; AG-20B-0042-C100, Adipogen, USA), and rabbit anti-β-actin antibody (1:1000; Cat: 4967 S, Cell Signaling Technology, USA). On the second day, membranes were washed three times in TBST for 10 min each and incubated with peroxidase-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG-HRP (1:5000; Cat: 7074 S, Cell Signaling Technology, USA) or anti-mouse IgG-HRP (1:5000; Cat: 7076 S, Cell Signaling Technology, USA) as the secondary antibody for 45 min at room temperature. After three additional washes in TBST, an ECL chemiluminescent substrate was applied, and signals were detected using digital imaging equipment. Band densities were quantified using ImageJ (NIH, USA), and protein expression levels of GSDMD and Caspase-1 were normalized to β-actin. Western blots were performed using pooled group lysates ( n = 6 rats per group pooled); densitometry reflects three independent immunoblot runs on separate gels using the same pooled lysate. The representative bands in the figure were selected from among these replicates. RT-qPCR measurement of IL-1β and IL-18 mRNA levels The mRNA expression levels of IL-1β and IL-18 were determined using reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR). Total RNA was extracted from samples using a RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Cat: 74104) and reverse transcribed into cDNA using a reverse transcription kit (Qiagen, Cat: 205311). qPCR was performed using TB Green ® Premix Ex Taq™ II (Takara, Cat: RR820A) with specific primers for IL-1β (Sino Biological, Cat: RP300022), IL-18 (Sino Biological, Cat: RP300068), and 18 S rRNA (Sino Biological, Cat: MP202628 ). Gene expression levels were normalized to 18 S rRNA, and relative expression was calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method (also known as the Livak method). All reactions were performed in triplicate. ELISA measurement of IL-1β and IL-18 The concentrations of IL-1β and IL-18 in the samples were determined using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). IL-1β ELISA kit (R&amp;D Systems, Cat: DY501) and IL-18 ELISA kit (R&amp;D Systems, Cat: DY521-05) were used according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, standards and samples were added to a 384-well plate and incubated, followed by the sequential addition of detection antibodies, HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies, and a substrate solution. The reaction was terminated, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm. The concentrations of IL-1β and IL-18 were calculated based on the standard curve, and all experiments were performed in triplicate. Data analysis All experimental data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10. Total and arthrogenic contracture, the degree of collagen deposition, total cell count, and the mRNA and protein levels of IL-1β and IL-18 were compared across groups using one-way ANOVA, followed by Sidak’s post-hoc multiple comparison test to evaluate pairwise differences between groups (Ctrl vs. IM; IM vs. IM + DSF). A p -value of less than 0.05 ( p &lt; 0.05) was considered statistically significant. Results Total contracture and arthrogenic contracture To evaluate the effect of disulfiram on immobilization-induced joint contracture, we measured the range of motion (ROM) of the knee joint. Compared to the control group (Ctrl), the immobilization group (IM) exhibited a significant increase in both total joint contracture and arthrogenic contracture ( p &lt; 0.0001, Fig. 1 D-F), indicating the successful establishment of the knee joint contracture model. Compared to the IM group, the immobilization + disulfiram group (IM + DSF) showed a significant reduction in joint contracture ( p &lt; 0.0001, Fig. 1 D-F), suggesting that disulfiram effectively alleviates immobilization-induced joint motion restriction. Cell count Since inflammation and fibroblast activation play crucial roles in joint contracture, we assessed inflammatory and myofibroblast infiltration using H&amp;E staining. The results demonstrated that, compared to the Ctrl group, the IM group exhibited a significant increase in cellular infiltration ( p &lt; 0.0001, Fig. 2 A, C), though precise cell identity was not confirmed histochemically in the joint capsule, indicating that immobilization induced a strong inflammatory response and fibroblast activation. However, compared to the IM group, the IM + DSF group showed a significant reduction in cellular infiltration ( p &lt; 0.0001, Fig. 2 A, C), suggesting that disulfiram alleviates immobilization-induced inflammation and myofibroblast differentiation in the joint capsule. Collagen deposition Since joint capsule fibrosis is a key factor contributing to restricted joint mobility, we further evaluated the role of disulfiram in fibrosis regulation. Masson staining results showed that, compared to the Ctrl group, collagen fiber deposition was significantly increased in the IM group ( p &lt; 0.0001, Fig. 2 B, D), indicating that immobilization successfully induced fibrosis. Excessive collagen accumulation represents ECM remodeling and is a hallmark of fibrotic progression, which is typically driven by fibroblast activation and increased matrix synthesis. However, compared to the IM group, the IM + DSF group exhibited a significant reduction in collagen fiber accumulation ( p &lt; 0.0001, Fig. 2 B, D), suggesting that disulfiram effectively mitigates immobilization-induced joint capsule fibrosis. Expression levels of the Caspase-1/GSDMD signaling pathway Given the potential relationship between inflammation, fibrosis, and pyroptosis, we further investigated the effects of immobilization on pyroptosis in the joint capsule. Western blot results showed that in the Ctrl group, GSDMD expression was relatively low with no detectable cleavage (Fig. 3 A, B). In contrast, the IM group exhibited significantly increased GSDMD expression along with its cleavage, suggesting that immobilization induced GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis (Fig. 3 A, B). Although GSDMD cleavage was not completely inhibited in the IM + DSF group, its extent was reduced compared to the IM group (Fig. 3 A, B). Additionally, Caspase-1 expression was significantly elevated in the IM group, while Caspase-1 levels in the IM + DSF group showed a non-significant decreasing trend compared to the IM group (Fig. 3 A, B), indicating that immobilization-induced pyroptosis might be mediated via the Caspase-1/GSDMD pathway, and that disulfiram partially suppresses this process. Discussion Joint immobilization is a commonly employed clinical strategy to protect joints; however, prolonged immobilization can lead to irreversible joint capsule fibrosis, ultimately resulting in joint contracture. Our previous studies demonstrated that in a rat knee extension immobilization model, fibrosis progresses slowly within the first week, accelerates during weeks 2–3, and reaches a pathological stabilization phase by week 4 30 . Joint capsule fibrosis severely compromises joint function and may even cause permanent disability, highlighting the necessity of early anti-fibrotic interventions during immobilization. Current management of joint contracture relies mainly on rehabilitation and surgical interventions; however, pharmacological options that directly target capsular fibrosis remain limited and recurrence is common in severe cases 31 . Recent studies have established that pyroptosis plays a crucial role in organ fibrosis 32 , 33 . This study aimed to determine whether pyroptosis occurs during joint contracture development and to assess the therapeutic potential of the pyroptosis inhibitor disulfiram in alleviating contracture. Our findings reveal that joint immobilization induces significant pyroptosis, evidenced by the activation of Caspase-1 and GSDMD, as well as the increased release of inflammatory cytokines IL-1β and IL-18. Moreover, immobilized rats exhibited reduced joint mobility, increased inflammatory cell infiltration, and excessive collagen deposition. Disulfiram treatment effectively suppressed pyroptosis, as indicated by the slightly reduced cleavage of GSDMD, complete inhibition of IL-1β release, and significant reduction in IL-18 levels. Although DSF is a known inhibitor of pyroptosis, our data showed detectable cleavage of GSDMD in the DSF-treated group. This observation aligns with prior mechanistic studies demonstrating that DSF does not inhibit the proteolytic cleavage of GSDMD by Caspase-1, but rather blocks the pore-forming activity of the cleaved N-terminal fragment 34 . Concurrently, joint mobility improved, and inflammation and fibrosis were alleviated. This study is the first to implicate pyroptosis in joint contracture pathogenesis and provides a novel therapeutic strategy for clinical intervention. Mechanical forces influence fibroblast survival and activation through mechanosensors such as PIEZO1 ion channels and integrin receptors 35 – 37 . On one hand, mechanical stress upregulates survival factors like BCL-2 while inhibiting PGE2 synthesis, leading to the abnormal persistence of myofibroblasts 38 , 39 . On the other hand, the PIEZO1-KCNN4 signaling axis mediates calcium influx and intracellular potassium depletion, activating the NLRP3 inflammasome and triggering GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis. Previous studies have demonstrated that mechanical stress directly activates NLRP3 inflammasomes via PIEZO1, promoting pyroptotic responses 40 . Our immunoblotting results confirm that GSDMD is expressed in the joint capsule across all groups but is significantly upregulated and cleaved following joint immobilization, indicating the presence of pyroptosis (Fig. 3 A, B). This suggests that immobilization-induced mechanical unloading also promotes pyroptosis. These seemingly opposing findings may in fact reflect different modes of mechanobiological disruption. Both excessive mechanical stress and mechanical unloading or abnormal joint immobilization can serve as danger signals, disrupting cytoskeletal integrity, inducing oxidative stress, and activating inflammasomes. In immobilized joints, prolonged disuse may alter cell–matrix interactions, oxygenation, and local cytokine profiles, thereby initiating a pyroptotic response through a sterile inflammation mechanism. IL-1β and IL-18 released during pyroptosis initiate a cascade of inflammatory responses, recruiting immune cells and activating fibroblasts, thereby perpetuating inflammation-driven fibrosis 41 . IL-1β exacerbates fibrosis through multiple pathways, including direct cellular damage, activation of the TGF-β1/Smad signaling pathway, and enhancement of collagen deposition 42 – 44 . Meanwhile, IL-18 promotes fibroblast proliferation via the STAT3 and NF-κB pathways 45 – 48 . Inhibitors targeting IL-1β and IL-18 have been shown to effectively suppress fibrosis progression 46 , 49 . Pyroptosis and inflammasome-related cytokines (e.g., IL-1β and IL-18) have been increasingly implicated in fibroblast activation and extracellular matrix remodeling. These findings support a mechanistic link between inflammation and capsular fibrosis and provide a rationale for targeting pyroptosis-related pathways in immobilization-induced joint contracture. Pyroptosis primarily relies on Caspase-1 signaling and GSDMD activation 50 . In the canonical pyroptotic pathway, pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) activate NLRP3 and other inflammasomes, leading to Caspase-1 activation, GSDMD cleavage, and the formation of membrane pores that facilitate IL-1β and IL-18 release. In the non-canonical pathway, lipopolysaccharides (LPS) directly activate Caspase-4/5 (human) or Caspase-11 (mouse), similarly leading to GSDMD cleavage and pyroptosis 16 , 17 . As a central executor of pyroptosis, GSDMD knockout effectively blocks pyroptosis, and inhibitors targeting GSDMD cleavage or pore formation have shown broad-spectrum anti-inflammatory potential, offering advantages over traditional anti-inflammatory therapies 50 . Disulfiram, an FDA-approved anti-alcoholism drug, has been recently identified as a pyroptosis inhibitor that covalently modifies GSDMD at Cys191, thereby preventing pore formation and inhibiting pyroptosis 34 . Unlike strategies that solely target NLRP3 or downstream inflammatory cytokines, disulfiram directly inhibits GSDMD-mediated pore formation, reducing IL-1β and IL-18 release. This mechanism effectively disrupts the vicious cycle of inflammation, fibroblast activation, and collagen deposition, suggesting a novel anti-inflammatory and anti-fibrotic therapeutic approach 51 . Our study demonstrated that disulfiram treatment significantly improved joint mobility, reduced inflammation, and decreased collagen deposition, as indicated by ROM assessments, H&amp;E staining, and Masson’s staining. Immunoblotting and ELISA results further confirmed that disulfiram effectively downregulated GSDMD cleavage and suppressed IL-1β and IL-18 release, ameliorating joint contracture symptoms (Fig. 5 ). Using FDA body-surface-area normalization, our rat dose (25 mg/kg/day) corresponds to a human equivalent dose of ~ 4.05 mg/kg/day, which lies within the clinically used dosing range for disulfiram. Although we did not identify the specific cell types undergoing pyroptosis, fibroblasts and synoviocytes are considered potential contributors based on previous studies. Further investigations using cell-type-specific staining are warranted. Additionally, the increased total cell count observed in the immobilized joint capsule may result from inflammatory cell infiltration and fibroblast activation, which could outweigh pyroptotic cell loss at the tissue level. Prior studies have also shown that disulfiram inhibits GSDMD activation during tendon-to-bone healing, reducing IL-1β and HMGB1 release and mitigating peri-tendinous fibrosis, findings that are consistent with our results 52 . Interestingly, a recent study indicates that disulfiram also inhibits NF-κB nuclear translocation, thereby reducing transcription of pro-inflammatory cytokines 53 . This mechanism may act synergistically with its effect on pyroptosis to further suppress inflammation in immobilized joints. Accordingly, we describe Caspase-1/GSDMD inhibition as a key mechanism supported by our data, while acknowledging that DSF’s effects may not be exclusively restricted to the pyroptosis pathway. For example, the specific inflammasome subtype activated by mechanical stress remains unclear. Further studies are required to determine whether PIEZO1 activation primarily triggers NLRP3 or other mechanosensitive pathways. A limitation of this study is that we did not employ IL-1β/IL-18 blockade, rescue challenges, or additional GSDMD-targeting tool compounds to establish pathway specificity in vivo; future studies will incorporate cytokine antagonism/neutralization and comparative GSDMD inhibition to delineate the contribution of this axis to DSF-mediated protection. Also, while our study focused primarily on joint capsule inflammation and fibrosis, we recognize that immobilization can also lead to muscle atrophy, fibrosis, and contractile dysfunction-key contributors to myogenic contracture. It is possible that DSF’s anti-inflammatory and anti-fibrotic effects may also extend to skeletal muscle, potentially reducing immobilization-induced muscle fibrosis or stiffness. Further studies are needed to assess DSF’s effects on muscle morphology and function under immobilization conditions. Additionally, the penetration of disulfiram into human joint tissues, its long-term safety, and its efficacy in reversing chronic contracture require further evaluation. In this animal study, systemic toxicity assessments (e.g., serum biochemistry, organ weights, or major-organ histology) were not performed, although body weight did not differ among groups (Supplementary Fig. S2 ). Future research should focus on elucidating the precise molecular mechanisms of disulfiram and exploring combination therapies with other anti-fibrotic agents, such as TGF-β inhibitors. Systematic evaluation of its long-term safety and effectiveness will facilitate the clinical translation of pyroptosis-targeting therapies, laying the groundwork for the development of innovative joint-protective and anti-fibrotic treatment strategies. Conclusion In summary, this study provides evidence that pyroptosis is significantly involved in immobilization-induced knee joint contracture and plays a key role in joint capsule fibrosis. Disulfiram effectively mitigates fibrosis and improves contracture symptoms, likely by blocking the pore-forming activity of cleaved GSDMD, rather than by inhibiting Caspase-1 activation (Fig. 5 ). These findings suggest that targeting pyroptosis may represent a promising therapeutic strategy for joint fibrosis treatment.
