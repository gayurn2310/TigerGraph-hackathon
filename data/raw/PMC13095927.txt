Multiple Adverse Outcomes Associated with Risperidone in People with Dementia: An Individual Participant Data Meta-Analysis

Introduction and Objectives Risperidone has modest efficacy for behaviours and psychological symptoms of dementia and is associated with many adverse events. Current guidelines limit its use to no longer than 12–16 weeks. This study aims to evaluate adverse outcomes over time, identify key predictors, and examine high-risk subgroups to inform safer prescribing. Method A one-stage individual participant data meta-analysis of six randomised controlled trials (risperidone: n = 1009; placebo: n = 712) was conducted. Mixed-effect generalised linear models and proportional hazards mixed-effects models estimated treatment effects, predictors, and subgroup differences for adverse outcomes over varying time periods. Results Risperidone was associated with increased risks of cerebrovascular (hazard ratio [HR] 4.11; 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.77–9.51; p = 0.001) and major cardiovascular events (HR 2.00; 95% CI 1.23–3.26; p = 0.006), with median (interquartile range) onset at 4.3 (5.9) and 4.8 (6.8) weeks of treatment, respectively. Somnolence occurred consistently during treatment, whereas upper respiratory tract infections (odds ratio [OR] 2.31; 95% CI 1.24–4.32; p = 0.009) and extrapyramidal symptoms emerged (OR 2.93; 95% CI 1.68–5.08; p &lt; 0.001) after week 4. Older age, male sex, and baseline cardiac pharmacotherapy use predicted serious adverse outcomes. Conclusion Most adverse effects occur after 4 weeks of treatment. Attention to baseline risk factors is essential to minimise harm. Risk–benefit calculators may guide individualised prescribing. Key Points Risperidone-related adverse effects often occur before the 12-week review recommended by current guidelines. Baseline cardiac therapy use, older age, and male sex predict serious adverse outcomes. Risks associated with risperidone varied by baseline use of antidepressants, musculoskeletal agents, and furosemide. Introduction Dementia is a growing global health challenge, with the number of people affected projected to reach 158 million by 2050 [ 1 ]. Behaviours and psychological symptoms of dementia (BPSD), also known as neuropsychiatric symptoms [ 2 ], affect up to 90% of individuals with dementia [ 3 ]. Although non-pharmacological interventions are recommended as the first-line treatment for BPSD [ 4 ], people with dementia remain high users of antipsychotics [ 5 – 7 ]. The efficacy of antipsychotics in managing these symptoms is limited [ 8 ], and their safety profile poses a major concern. Alarmingly, a recent study reported that up to 80% of antipsychotic prescriptions for people with dementia are considered high risk [ 9 ]. Antipsychotics have been linked to several severe adverse outcomes, including increased risks of mortality, cardiovascular adverse events, falls, and pneumonia [ 10 , 11 ]. Additionally, other adverse effects such as extrapyramidal symptoms could lead to poor quality of life and potentially worsen BPSD among patients [ 12 ]. However, evidence remains inconsistent—particularly when comparing typical and atypical antipsychotics, and even among agents within the atypical class [ 12 , 13 ]. Therefore, there is an urgent need for a more personalised approach to antipsychotic prescribing in dementia, ensuring that benefits are weighed carefully against risks for each person. Tailoring treatment by identifying the time periods when adverse events are most likely to occur, recognising subgroups at heightened risk, and determining predictors of harm could enable more informed prescribing. Individual participant data meta-analysis (IPD-MA), which pools raw data from multiple trials, has greater statistical power to detect low-incident adverse events, explore predictors, and conduct robust subgroup analyses [ 14 , 15 ]. We previously employed IPD-MA to identify predictors and modifiers of therapeutic response in risperidone users [ 16 ]. To date, only two IPD-MA studies have examined mortality [ 17 , 18 ] and cerebrovascular adverse events (CVAE) [ 18 ], but these analyses did not address a broad range of outcomes (e.g. major adverse cardiovascular events [MACE], falls, pneumonia, somnolence, extrapyramidal symptoms, cognitive decline, weight gain), different time periods, or subgroups at risk. Similarly, a review summarising predictors of adverse events in people with dementia focused solely on mortality and hospitalisation [ 19 ]. Building on our earlier work [ 16 ]—and given that risperidone is the only antipsychotic approved for BPSD in certain countries [ 4 ]—this study uses IPD-MA to examine risks of multiple adverse outcomes across different time periods, identify predictors, and explore high-risk subgroups. Methods Data Sources and Trial Characteristics Individual participant data (IPD) were obtained through the Yale Open Data Access (YODA) Project, an independent initiative supporting secure and transparent clinical trial data sharing [ 20 ]. This IPD-MA included data from six placebo-controlled clinical trials evaluating risperidone for the treatment of BPSD, as detailed in our previous publication [ 16 ]. Dementia was diagnosed using Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 4th edition (DSM-IV) criteria, and BPSD was assessed with the Behavioural Pathology in Alzheimer’s Disease (BEHAVE-AD) rating scale [ 21 ]. Trials required participants to have a Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) score ≤ 23 and evidence of BPSD, defined as a BEHAVE-AD total score ≥ 8, a score ≥ 2 on any BEHAVE-AD psychosis item, or a qualifying Cohen–Mansfield Agitation Inventory (CMAI) frequency score [ 16 ]. Individuals with other psychiatric disorders were excluded. All participants were institutionalised, and psychotropic medications were discontinued during the washout period. Duration of the included trials varied from 4 weeks to 12 weeks. A complete description of each trial’s inclusion and exclusion criteria is provided in Supplementary Table S1 (see the electronic supplementary material). Access to these participant-level datasets was requested via the standard YODA platform procedure on May 1st, 2024. Trial characteristics and risk of bias were evaluated using the Revised Cochrane Risk of Bias tool (RoB 2), consistent with methods described in our prior study [ 16 ]. Multiple Adverse Outcomes Data on adverse outcomes were initially extracted from each individual trial and subsequently harmonised based on the clinical expertise of the research team. Detailed information regarding the original adverse events in each trial and the harmonised grouped outcomes is presented in Supplementary Table S2 (see the electronic supplementary material). Adverse outcomes were classified into three types: time-to-event outcomes, dichotomous outcomes, and continuous outcomes. Time-to-event outcomes were the first occurrence of severe events including all-cause mortality, CVAE, MACE, falls, and pneumonia during the trial. Incident severe events were captured during the trial and for up to 30 days after treatment cessation, consistent with protocol requirements for post-treatment reporting of serious adverse events. MACE was defined with five components encompassing cardiovascular death, stroke, unstable angina, myocardial infarction, and heart failure, as recommended by previous literature [ 22 ]. Dichotomous outcomes included any events of somnolence, extrapyramidal side effects (EPS), and upper respiratory tract infections (URTIs) in two distinct periods: from baseline to week 4 of treatment and from week 4 onward. The person-level change from baseline to the last follow-up of the MMSE score, weight, fasting blood glucose, and serum sodium level were used as continuous outcomes. Additionally, parkinsonism was assessed using the parkinsonism subscale score in the Extrapyramidal Symptom Rating Scale (ESRS) in four trials, and the Simpson-Angus Scale (SAS) in two trials. Covariate Extraction Baseline characteristics and medication use were extracted and harmonised from all trials. Demographic characteristics included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), and dementia subtype. Clinical conditions included the MMSE score, the total BEHAVE-AD score, and the presence of BPSD, previously classified using the BEHAVE-AD domains: psychosis, activity disturbances, aggression, sleep disturbances, affective disturbances, and anxieties/phobias [ 16 ]. Medication use at baseline was categorised using Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) codes [ 23 ], including cardiovascular drugs (B01, C02, C03, C07, C08, C09, C10), cardiac therapy (C01), antidiabetic drugs (A10), and musculoskeletal drugs (M01, M02, M03, M04, M05). Psychotropic medications included opioids (N02A, R05DA04), antiepileptics (N03A), antipsychotics (N05A), hypnotics and sedatives (N05B), anxiolytics (N05C), and antidepressants (N06A). Prochlorperazine (ATC code N05AB04) was excluded from the antipsychotic group due to its primary use as an antiemetic. A detailed summary of all extracted variables is provided in Supplementary Table S3 (see the electronic supplementary material). Statistical Analysis Descriptive Analyses Baseline characteristics of participants were reported descriptively, using frequencies and proportions for categorical variables, and means (standard deviations) or medians (interquartile ranges) for continuous variables, depending on their distributions. We performed a one-stage IPD-MA using multivariable mixed-effects models with random intercepts. This method addresses variability between studies (heterogeneity) by allowing baseline risks (intercepts) to differ while assuming consistent effects of predictors across studies [ 15 ]. Depending on the outcome type, we applied different models: Cox proportional hazards models for time-to-event outcomes, logistic regression for dichotomous outcomes, and linear regression for continuous outcomes. Results were reported as hazard ratios (HRs) for time-to-event outcomes, odds ratios (ORs) for dichotomous outcomes, and mean differences (MDs) for continuous outcomes between baseline and last follow-up, each accompanied by their 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Changes in parkinsonism scores (baseline to final follow-up) were expressed as standardised mean differences (SMDs) to accommodate the use of two rating scales. The effect sizes of SMDs were interpreted as small (0.2–0.5), medium (0.5–0.8), and large (&gt; 0.8) [ 24 ]. Estimating Risperidone Effects Across Multiple Adverse Outcomes The associations between risperidone and each adverse outcome were assessed in the entire study population. For each outcome, risperidone effect was adjusted for potential confounding factors including age, sex, BMI, MMSE score, dementia type (Alzheimer’s disease, vascular dementia and mixed type dementia), total BEHAVE-AD score, and baseline medication use. In addition, the presence of each BPSD at baseline (psychosis, aggressiveness, activity disturbances, sleep disturbances, affective disturbance, and anxieties/phobias) was included in the models to minimise confounding by prescribing indication [ 25 ] and better isolate risperidone effect on risk. To reduce false-positive findings due to multiple comparisons, the Benjamini–Hochberg method was applied for p value correction [ 26 ]. Predictors of Adverse Outcomes Stratified by Treatment Group To explore predictors within each treatment group (risperidone or placebo), multivariable models were built separately for each adverse outcome. Covariates included in the final models were selected based on clinical relevance and supporting literature [ 19 ], including age, sex, BMI, MMSE score, dementia type, baseline presence of BPSD, total BEHAVE-AD score, and medication use at baseline. Subgroup Analysis Subgroup analyses were conducted using the covariates available in the dataset: age, sex, BMI, MMSE score, dementia type, baseline BPSD status, total BEHAVE-AD score, and baseline medication use. Interaction analyses between treatment groups and specific subgroup factors were initially conducted, adjusting for age, sex, BMI, MMSE score, and baseline medication use. Then, stratification of subgroups was carried out to evaluate the effect of risperidone within each subset of patients, with adjustments for the same covariates. Model Diagnostics and Missing Data The variance inflation factor (VIF) was calculated for each covariate to assess multicollinearity, ensuring all variables had a VIF below 2. Logistic and linear mixed-effects models were estimated using the glmer and lmer functions, respectively, from the R package lme4 [ 27 ]. Cox proportional hazard mixed-effects models were fitted using the coxme function from the R package coxme [ 28 ]. Missing covariate data were handled through full-information maximum likelihood estimation under the assumption of missing at random (MAR), incorporated within the mixed-effects models [ 29 ]. All analyses were conducted using R statistical software, version 4.3.0. Results Cohort Characteristics A total of 1721 participants were included in the analysis, with 712 in the placebo group and 1009 in the risperidone group. Baseline demographics were comparable between the two groups. The mean age was 83 years in both groups, and approximately 70% of participants were female. Most participants were diagnosed with Alzheimer’s disease. The most frequent BPSD were aggression, activity disturbances, and psychosis. Use of cardiovascular drugs at baseline was similar between both groups. The other baseline characteristics were similar across both groups, except that antipsychotic use was higher in the placebo group ( p &lt; 0.001). BEHAVE-AD Behavioural Pathology in Alzheimer’s Disease, CI confidence interval, HR hazard ratio, MD mean difference, MMSE Mini-Mental State Examination Discussion Summary of Main Findings To our knowledge, this is the first IPD-MA of clinical trial data to assess multiple adverse outcomes of risperidone in people with dementia. Risperidone use was associated with an increased risk of CVAE and MACE. The risk of somnolence was elevated both before and after week 4, whereas EPS and URTIs were associated with risperidone use predominantly in the later treatment period. Weight gain and cognitive decline were likewise associated with risperidone use. Baseline use of antidepressants, musculoskeletal medications, and furosemide modified risperidone’s effects, increasing the risks of hyponatraemia, falls, and mortality, respectively. Risperidone and Multiple Adverse Outcomes Severe Adverse Outcomes In this IPD-MA, risperidone use was not associated with a statistically significant increase in all-cause mortality, consistent with findings from several reviews of randomised controlled trials [ 13 , 17 , 30 ], whereas certain cohort studies have reported statistically significant associations [ 31 ]. Similarly, no significant associations were observed between risperidone and falls or pneumonia, although prior cohort studies have reported mixed findings [ 10 , 12 ]. These discrepancies may reflect differences in study populations, follow-up duration, and baseline risk between randomised controlled trials and observational cohorts. Our analysis also identified elevated risks of CVAE and MACE including cardiovascular death, stroke, myocardial infarction, unstable angina, and heart failure in risperidone users compared to placebo. These results are in line with findings from real-world studies [ 10 – 13 ] and with the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) “black box” warning regarding antipsychotic-related cardiovascular events [ 32 ]. Although the median onset of these adverse events in our study occurred more than 4 weeks after initiation, one cohort study reported that the risk of stroke and other serious cardiovascular events was highest shortly after antipsychotic initiation and remained elevated for a 2-year period [ 10 ]. Differences in patient characteristics such as the severity of BPSD at baseline between randomised controlled trials and observational cohorts, as well as potential unobserved bias, may partly explain these patterns; nevertheless, risperidone use should be limited to the shortest duration necessary. Effects on Central and Peripheral Nervous System Somnolence was consistently associated with risperidone use, both early and late in treatment, while EPS and URTIs emerged after week 4. The mechanism of antipsychotic-induced EPS is well-understood, which is mainly due to the blockage of dopamine D 2 receptors [ 4 ], whereas the link with URTI is still unknown. Although these events are generally not life-threatening, they can impair comfort, function, social engagement, and quality of life, which may exacerbate BPSD—potentially undermining treatment goals [ 12 , 33 ]. Metabolic Changes and Cognitive Function Weight gain was associated with risperidone use, consistent with previous reports [ 8 ]. In contrast, no significant changes were observed in serum sodium or fasting glucose, which aligns with evidence that hyponatraemia [ 34 ] and glucose abnormalities [ 35 ] are relatively uncommon with risperidone compared with other antipsychotics. Cognitive decline was also linked to risperidone, which is clinically relevant given its potential to exacerbate BPSD manifestations such as wandering and psychosis [ 36 , 37 ]. Risk Stratification Baseline use of cardiac therapy was identified as a predictor of all-cause mortality and MACE, including cardiovascular death. Real-world evidence similarly indicates that individuals with dementia and pre-existing cardiovascular disease are at a substantially increased risk of stroke, a risk further amplified with risperidone use [ 11 ]. These findings reinforce current recommendations to exercise caution when initiating risperidone in individuals with a history of cardiac disease, given their elevated risk of stroke or transient ischaemic attack [ 13 , 38 ]. Conversely, we found that individuals with psychosis had lower risks of stroke and MACE, independent of risperidone use, which is consistent with prior reports [ 18 ]. Considering our earlier study showing risperidone’s efficacy in psychosis [ 16 ], these symptoms may represent an important treatment target. In our subgroup analysis, no BPSD domains were identified as risk modifiers. However, one real-world study suggested that psychosis and aggression may modify stroke risk, although it examined multiple antipsychotics rather than risperidone alone and did not adjust for concomitant medications or comorbidities [ 25 ]. As our cohort primarily comprised individuals with agitation, further studies are needed to clarify whether risks differ across specific BPSD symptoms. There was a trend towards higher all-cause mortality with risperidone compared to placebo among participants using furosemide at baseline in our study. Although statistical significance was not reached, this pattern was previously noted [ 17 ]. One possible explanation is that furosemide users may be more susceptible to hypovolaemia [ 39 ], a condition in which risperidone use warrants caution [ 13 , 38 ], as it may predispose to severe outcomes such as hypovolaemic shock or sudden death—events previously reported with antipsychotic use [ 12 , 13 ]. Additionally, a reduction in serum sodium was observed among participants taking antidepressants at baseline when treated with risperidone compared to placebo. While risperidone itself is not strongly associated with hyponatraemia [ 34 ], the combined effect of multiple psychotropic medications may increase this risk [ 40 ]. Greater weight gain was also observed among participants with baseline affective disturbances, who tended to have more severe behavioural symptoms and cognitive impairment. This may reflect increased appetite in those with pre-existing mood disorders or greater nutritional support provided to those with more advanced disease. It should be noted that these subgroup analyses lack statistical power and should be interpreted with caution, serving primarily as hypothesis-generating findings [ 41 ]. Strengths and Limitations This IPD-MA combined trial data from multiple regions, allowing examination of a broad spectrum of adverse outcomes and improving the applicability of results across diverse clinical settings. By using a one-stage, centred modelling approach, we minimised ecological bias between studies, and the random-intercept S-learner method followed current best practice for estimating individualised treatment effects in IPD-MA [ 14 , 15 ]. Several limitations should be considered. First, because the dataset was limited to YODA risperidone trials, relevant external studies were not captured, potentially constraining generalisability across populations and settings. Second, race/ethnicity was not included in the analyses because the low incidence of outcomes within non-Caucasian groups made model estimation unreliable. As a result, the findings may not be generalisable to Asian, African, or other underrepresented populations. Third, detailed comorbidity data were not consistently reported across trials, which may have introduced residual confounding; however, baseline medication categories were used as proxies for underlying conditions to mitigate this issue. In addition, the analysis assumed data were MAR, which could bias pooled estimates if the assumption does not hold. Inclusion of unpublished trials (BEL-14 and INT-83) may reduce publication bias; however, the absence of peer review for these datasets introduces potential for other biases. Statistical power for subgroup and predictor analyses was limited as this is not predefined, and these findings should be regarded as exploratory. Finally, because most included trials used flexible titration schedules, we did not evaluate dose–response relationships for other adverse events. Implications for Research and Clinical Practice First, current clinical guidelines recommend limiting risperidone use for psychosis and agitation in dementia to no longer than 12–16 weeks [ 38 , 42 , 43 ]. However, the current population-based study shows that antipsychotic prescribing often deviates from guideline recommendations, with a median treatment duration of 28 weeks [ 44 ]. Given the observed associations between risperidone and multiple adverse outcomes in our study, its use may ideally be limited to no longer than 4 weeks. This recommendation is supported by our finding that the time to first severe adverse event often occurs after week 4. Furthermore, some of the events have the potential to exacerbate BPSD, such as URTIs, EPS, or cognitive decline, thereby contradicting the intended therapeutic purpose. Our previous analysis also showed that treatment response at week 2 can predict response at week 4 [ 16 ]. Therefore, an early evaluation at 2 weeks is also recommended. If no benefit is observed, discontinuation may be considered [ 42 ]. Second, considering the FDA “black box” warning on antipsychotic use in dementia [ 32 ] and our observed association between risperidone and cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events, there is a clear need for a validated risk calculator to estimate individual likelihood of these outcomes. Prediction models developed in psychiatric populations have shown promise and could be adapted for dementia care to support shared decision-making and more precise risk–benefit assessment [ 45 – 47 ]. Additionally, although a previous study reported no association between risperidone dose and mortality [ 17 ], the dose–response relationship for other severe adverse events remains unexplored. More cohort studies are needed to evaluate the impact of dosing patterns on these risks. Finally, future studies should further investigate the hypotheses generated from our subgroup analyses, particularly the potential interaction between baseline furosemide use and risperidone, to clarify whether certain patient groups are at heightened risk. Conclusion Risperidone is associated with a broad spectrum of adverse outcomes, notably cerebrovascular and major cardiovascular events, including cardiovascular death. Most events occurred after week 4, underscoring the need to limit treatment duration to as short as possible to reduce harm and avoid undermining therapeutic benefit. Baseline risk factors should be carefully evaluated before initiation. Future research should further investigate subgroup-specific risks to guide safer, more individualised prescribing.
