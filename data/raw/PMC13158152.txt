Novel insights into early Alzheimer's disease: quantitative gradient recalled echo MRI reveals preatrophic neuronal and myelin injury concurrent with emerging amyloid pathology

Abstract BACKGROUND Brain amyloid accumulation is considered an early sign of Alzheimer's disease (AD) pathology, appearing decades before manifestation of clinical symptoms. However, very little is known about neurodegeneration during this early AD stage. Herein we use quantitative gradient recalled echo (qGRE) magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) to reveal preatrophic microstructural neuronal and myelin damage concurrent with amyloid pathology. METHODS qGRE MRI and positron emission tomography (PET) amyloid [ 11 C] Pittsburgh Compound‐B ( 11 C‐PiB) images were acquired concurrently from 141 study participants. Two qGRE‐derived biomarker indices – neuronal density index (NDI) (a proxy for neuronal density) and myeline density index (MDI) (a proxy for myelin density) – are used here to quantify neuronal and myelin damage. RESULTS qGRE detected significantly lower gray matter neuronal density and white matter myelination in the group of participants with amyloid level below the conventional amyloid‐positive threshold (mean cortical standardized uptake value ratio of 1.42), despite the absence of atrophy. DISCUSSION qGRE‐based NDI and MDI in‐vivo biomarkers identify microstructural brain preatrophic neurodegeneration and demyelination in early amyloid pathology.

1 BACKGROUND Alzheimer's disease (AD) is a continuous neurodegenerative disease, with pathology onset decades before the appearance of clinical symptoms, usually accompanied by a trajectory of rapid brain atrophy (loss of brain tissue volume). 1 To provide effective intervention and therapy, diagnostic methods should allow identification of the microstructural pathological changes already present at the silent preclinical AD stage. 2 Currently, brain accumulation of amyloid beta (Aβ) and/or cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) amyloid level reduction are considered among the earliest AD biomarkers, serving as an indirect indicator of ongoing neurodegeneration rather than a direct measure of neuronal loss. 3 The in vivo methods identifying neurodegeneration are usually based on measurements of tissue atrophy (volume reduction) with structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). At the same time, a post mortem neuropathological study 4 demonstrated that the neuronal loss in AD exceeds what is identified by atrophy alone. This underscores the importance of introducing biomarkers that can detect neuronal cell damage before it becomes apparent through atrophy measurements, indicating preatrophic neurodegeneration. These biomarkers could provide more effective early detection of neurodegeneration, especially in the preclinical stages of AD. Herein we use MRI‐based quantitative gradient recalled echo (qGRE) method 5 as a sensitive tool for identifying preatrophic neuronal cell damage. qGRE is an advanced MRI technique based on the GRE sequence with multiple gradient echoes and data analysis making it possible to generate the qGRE metrics sensitive to tissue microstructural, R 2 t ∗ , and hemodynamic, R 2 ′ , properties. A strong positive association was previously demonstrated between the R 2 t ∗ and the gene profiles associated with the neuronal cells across the cortical brain gray matter (GM), 6 which allowed for the introduction of the R 2 t ∗ ‐based neuronal density index (NDI) as a surrogate of neuronal density. 6 The recently developed transient hydrogen bond (THB) theory 7 relates magnetic resonance (MR) signal relaxation in the brain to the water molecules (major source of MRI signal) interaction with lipid bilayers forming cellular membranes in GM and myelin sheath in white matter (WM). Using this theory we introduce a qGRE‐based myeline density index (MDI) as a surrogate for WM tissue myelination. MDI can serve as an important link between AD tissue pathology in WM and amyloid burden, as myelin dysfunction was reported as a driver of Aβ deposition in AD. 8 Previous studies already demonstrated the utility of qGRE metrics in AD. 9 , 10 They reported that the cell‐specific relaxation metric, R 2 t ∗ , was sensitive to differentiating the healthy control group (cognitively unimpaired, amyloid negative [Aβ − ] 11 ) from a preclinical AD group (cognitively unimpaired, amyloid positive [Aβ + ] 11 ). 9 The authors have showcased the significantly decreased R 2 t ∗ in mild AD due to the loss of cellular integrity. 9 Another recently introduced biomarker, called dark matter (tissue with a reduced concentration of neurons), corresponding to low R 2 t ∗ values, made it possible to distinguish healthy brains from preclinical AD cases. 10 Also, qGRE R 2 t ∗ measurements have shown higher loss of neurons in mild AD and preclinical AD compared to volumetric tissue losses. 10 Our hypothesis is that qGRE‐based NDI and MDI biomarkers can serve as surrogates for the assessment of neuronal and myelin tissue damage. These measures may provide insights into microstructural brain tissue pathology associated with Aβ accumulation in preclinical AD stages, where conventional brain MRI volumetric measures may be less sensitive. 12 , 13 The aim of this study was to utilize the highly sensitive qGRE‐based imaging biomarkers NDI in cortical GM and MDI in subcortical WM to detect microstructural changes due to early preatrophic neurodegeneration (prior to volume loss) accompanying amyloid accumulation in the brain. This vital microstructural information could potentially be utilized for improved patient selection for anti‐amyloid treatment. 2 METHODS 2.1 Participants A total of 141 participants (14 African American, two Asian, 125 White), aged between 48 and 88 years (69.5 ± 7.1), 83 females and 58 males (sex observed by the investigator), were recruited through the Knight Alzheimer's Disease Research Center (ADRC; n = 100) and the Study to Evaluate Amyloid in Blood and Imaging Related to Dementia (SEABIRD; n = 41). Additional demographic information is presented in Table S1 . This study was approved by the Washington University Institutional Review Board. All participants provided written informed consent. Both qGRE MRI and PET amyloid [ 11 C] Pittsburgh Compound‐B ( 11 C‐PiB) images were acquired during the same visit. 2.2 MRI data acquisition and analysis Brain MRI was performed at Washington University in St. Louis, Missouri, using 3T MRI (Siemens, Erlanger, Germany). The MRI protocol consisted of three‐dimensional (3D) multi‐GRE (mGRE) sequence with phase stabilization block and a standard clinical T1‐weighted magnetization‐prepared rapid gradient‐echo imaging (MPRAGE). MPRAGE parameters were as follows: field of view (FOV) = 256 × 256 mm, inversion time (TI)/echo time (TE)/repetition time (TR) = 1100/3.37/2000 ms, flip angle = 10°, resolution = 1 × 1 × 1 mm 3 , and acquisition time = 6 min. Three‐dimensional mGRE parameters were as follows: FOV = 256 × 192 mm, in‐plane resolution = 1 × 1 mm 2 (read, phase), 80 2‐mm slices, 10 gradient echoes with first echo time 4 ms, echo spacing = 4 ms, TR 50 ms, flip angle = 30°. A phase stabilization echo was collected after each of the 10 th echoes to correct for phase inconsistencies due to physiological fluctuations. 14 The total acquisition time with 2D GRAPPA parallel imaging 15 was about 6 min. The acquired raw data of the mGRE sequences were reconstructed using an in‐house MATLAB‐based (MathWorks Inc., Natick, MA, USA) program. After phase correction for the physiological fluctuations 14 and GRAPPA reconstruction, k‐space data from each radio frequency (RF) channel were converted to the spatial domain and combined together using a previously developed algorithm. 16 A 3D spatial Hanning filter was applied to reduce Gibbs ringing artifacts and noise. Data were analyzed using a theoretical model of BOLD (blood oxygen level‐dependent) effect 17 to disentangle the contribution of tissue‐cellular‐specific relaxation ( R2t *) and BOLD contributions to the total R2* relaxation. 5 , 17 , 18 With the introduction of the concept of cells as an endogenous contrast agent, a quantitative relationship between the R 2 t ∗ of qGRE metric and NDI – a parameter that represents a proxy for the neuronal density – was established in Wen et al. 6 as follows: (1) R 2 t ∗ = 5.8 + 20.4 · N D I . where the parameter NDI is dimensionless and varies from 0 (for tissue devoid of neurons) to 1 (for tissue with 100% neurons). The value of NDI in healthy adult human brains typically varies between 0.3 and 0.7, equivalent to R2t * in a range between 12 and 20 s −1 . As demonstrated in Yablonskiy and Sukstanskii, 7 brain tissue longitudinal and transverse relaxation rate parameters are defined mostly by water proton quantum interactions, with lipid bilayers forming cellular membranes and a myelin sheath. Hence, R 2 t ∗ values in WM reflect the density of bilayers in the multilamellar myelin structure. Accordingly, herein we introduce MDI as a surrogate of myelin density in WM: (2) M D I = R 2 t ∗ R 2 t ∗ where ⟨ R 2 t ∗ ⟩ is the mean value of R 2 t ∗ across WM in all subjects. RESEARCH IN CONTEXT Systematic review : Prior studies established brain amyloid accumulation, detected via PET, as an early hallmark of AD, often preceding clinical symptoms by decades. However, neurodegeneration during this presymptomatic phase remains poorly characterized, with conventional MRI typically revealing atrophy only in later stages. qGRE MRI has emerged as a sensitive tool for assessing microstructural tissue integrity, offering proxies for NDI and MDI independent of atrophy. Interpretation : This study demonstrates that qGRE MRI detects significant reductions in GM neuronal density and WM myelination in individuals with amyloid pathology – even below standard amyloid positivity thresholds (mcSUVR 1.42) – in the absence of atrophy. These findings reveal concurrent preatrophic neuronal and myelin injury during early amyloid accumulation, suggesting that microstructural neurodegeneration begins earlier than previously recognized and may represent a critical therapeutic window. Future directions : Future research aims at further validation of NDI and MDI as predictive biomarkers in longitudinal cohorts to establish their role in tracking early AD progression. Integrating qGRE with other modalities (e.g., tau PET, fluid biomarkers) could refine staging of preclinical AD. Clinical trials targeting amyloid should assess whether preserving microstructural integrity delays cognitive decline, potentially redefining intervention timing. 2.2.1 FreeSurfer segmentation and co‐registration Brain segmentation was performed on T1‐weighted MPRAGE images using FreeSurfer software (Martinos Center for Biomedical Imaging). First, the T1‐weighted (T1‐W) qGRE images were registered to the FreeSurfer T1‐W template images using Functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging of the Brain (FMRIB)'s Linear Image Registration tool in FSL software 19 , 20 to create FreeSurfer labels of GM and WM. 21 The FreeSurfer segmentation was quality‐checked and manually edited if any issues were identified. To calculate ROI volumes (cm 3 ) and mean R2t * values, the inverted FreeSurfer co‐registration matrix was applied to the T1‐weighted qGRE images and the corresponding naturally co‐registered qGRE metric maps. Note that volume measurements were not normalized to total intracranial volume. 22 , 23 2.2.2 11 C‐PIB PET amyloid imaging 11 C‐PiB PET tracer was utilized for PET imaging of cerebral Aβ pathology in vivo, 24 which was prepared according to protocol by Mathis et al. 25 In this cohort, dynamic PET imaging was conducted for 1 h with a Biograph mMR PET/MR scanner (Siemens Medical Solutions, Erlangen, Germany) in 3D mode after intravenous administration of approximately 12 mCi of PiB. Images were reconstructed using a 128×128×109 matrix. PiB PET burden was quantified using the mean cortical standardized uptake value ratio (mcSUVR), calculated across cortical GM regions defined by FreeSurfer. 26 The cerebellar cortex was used as the reference region for calculating regional SUVRs. 26 The mcSUVR was determined by averaging across the SUVR of individual cortical regions defined by FreeSurfer. mcSUVR values lower than 1.42 are usually considered to be Aβ − and above 1.42 are considered to be Aβ + . 27 2.3 Cognitive status In this study, the cognitive performance of participants was assessed by the Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE) in the Knight ADRC cohort and the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) in the SEABIRD cohort. Both tests have 30‐point screening scores. 28 MMSE scores between 25 and 30 are considered to be normal, scores between 21 and 24 are considered to signify mild impairment, and scores between 10and 20 are considered to signify moderate impairment. MoCA scores between 26 and 30 are considered normal, between 18 and 25 are considered to signify mild impairment, and scores between 10 and 17 are considered to indicate moderate impairment. 2.3.1 Group classifications In our cohort of participants, individual mcSUVR values range from 0.8 to 4.2. To quantify the group differences based on the qGRE‐defined NDI, MDI, and volume measurements in cortical GM and subcortical WM, we stratified the participants into different groups by varying the mcSUVR cutoff values as detailed in Table 1 . In the first strategy, groups were defined based on mcSUVR cutoffs at 1.42 and 1.2. Control‐1 and Control‐2 groups were defined as groups of participants with mcSUVR below 1.42 and 1.2, respectively. Aβ + ‐1 and Aβ + ‐2 groups were defined by exceeding mcSUVR cutoffs of 1.42 and 1.2. In the second strategy, Aβ + groups were selected from the participants with mcSUVR between 1.2 and a sliding upper mcSUVR limit of 1.6, 2.0, 2.4, 2.8, 3.2, 3.6, and 4.0. This approach made it possible to assess the sensitivity of the qGRE metrics to neurodegeneration at very low levels of amyloid accumulation. In addition, we investigated the interplay between cognition and amyloid status. For this, we compared the qGRE‐defined NDI, MDI, and volume measurements in (a) Aβ + and Aβ − participants (mcSUVR cutoff at 1.42) with cognitively normal status and (b) cognitively normal and impaired participants with Aβ − status (mcSUVR &lt; 1.42). The number of principal components that accounted for 50% of the variance was considered sufficient to capture most of the information in the data. Group differences for NDI, MDI, and volume measurements in individual regions and PCA‐derived components were established using the Mann‐Whitney U test. A p value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Individual region‐wise group comparisons were also performed using the Mann‐Whitney U test. To account for multiple comparisons across different brain regions, the false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied using the Benjamini‐Hochberg procedure. 29 Statistical significance was defined at a FDR‐adjusted q value &lt; 0.05, where the q value represents the p value corrected for multiple comparisons. In addition to the Mann‐Whitney tests, effect sizes were computed to assess the robustness of group differences, given the limited sample size, particularly in the Aβ + group selected with mcSUVR values between 1.2 and 1.6 ( n = 14). Effect sizes were quantified using Cohen's d , where values of d ≈ 0.2 were interpreted as small effects, d ≈ 0.5 as medium effects, d ≈ 0.8 as large effects, and d ≥ 1.05 as very large effects. We also report the percentage and sign of the differences in NDI and MDI between groups, which were calculated as follows: (3) % d i f f e r e n c e = c o n t r o l g r o u p _ m e a n − A β + g r o u p _ m e a n c o n t r o l g r o u p _ m e a n × 100 An analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was performed to examine changes in NDI, MDI, and volume in individual regions with respect to amyloid status (defined at mcSUVR = 1.42 and 1.20), while including age, sex, and APOE ε4 status as covariates. Statistical significance is indicated as *** p &lt; 0.001, ** p &lt; 0.01, and * p &lt; 0.05 for comparisons between the control and Aβ + groups across age, sex, and status. To directly assess the predictive performance of qGRE‐derived measures, a classification analysis was performed (group separated at mcSUVRs of 1.2 and 1.42) using logistic regression. The model included 40 GM (34 cortical and six deep GM) R2t * and 34 subcortical WM R2t * ROIs as predictors, along with age and APOE status. Model performance was evaluated using stratified 10‐fold cross‐validation, with out‐of‐fold predicted probabilities used to compute performance metrics. 30 Classification performance was quantified using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), 31 along with sensitivity, specificity, and negative predictive value (NPV), reported at the Youden‐optimal threshold. 32 The statistical significance of model performance was assessed using permutation testing (500 permutations), with p values reflecting the probability of achieving the observed AUC under the null hypothesis of no association. 3 RESULTS 3.1 Assessing neurodegeneration across different amyloid thresholds Figure 1 presents the surface maps of the percentage group differences and the statistical significance ( p values &lt; 0.05) obtained from Mann‐Whitney U tests (uncorrected for multiple comparisons) between the control and Aβ+ groups. At the PiB mcSUVR cutoff of 1.2, the NDI and MDI in the Aβ+ group were consistently lower than those in the control group, except for small increases in NDI in the frontal pole (+1%) and right lateral occipital (+0.7%) regions. The largest lower values of NDI in the Aβ+ group, compared to the control group, were observed in the left medial orbital frontal (−7.5%), left parsorbitalis (−8.2%), and right rostral anterior cingulate (−10.7%) regions. The largest lower values of MDI in the Aβ+ group, compared to the control group, were observed in the left middle temporal (−6.3%) and right parahippocampal (−7.7%) regions. In contrast, the percentage of group differences in volume between Aβ+ and control groups showed both negative and positive values, indicating that volumetric alterations are not always uniformly directional. At the standard PiB mcSUVR cutoff of 1.42, NDI in the cortical left and right hemispheres of GM exhibited significant group differences between Aβ − and Aβ + groups in several brain regions, for example, middle temporal ( p = 0.0005) and superior temporal ( p = 0.0004) regions of the left hemisphere and rostral anterior cingulate ( p = 0.0008) and superior temporal ( p = 0.0007) regions of the right hemisphere of the brain. MDI in subcortical WM exhibited even stronger group differences. However, volumetric measurements revealed significantly fewer regions with group differences; specifically, these were limited to the left superior frontal ( p = 0.002) and superior temporal ( p = 0.002) regions, as well as the right parahippocampal ( p = 0.02) and superior frontal ( p = 0.001) regions. When the mcSUVR cutoff was reduced from 1.42 to 1.20, MDI and NDI demonstrated comparable regional patterns of group differences, including regions identified at the 1.42 cutoff. Even after FDR correction (Table S2 ), significant differences were observed in the MDI at both mcSUVR cutoffs (1.2 and 1.42), across 25 subcortical WM regions, in most of the frontal lobe regions (except in the pars triangularis and medial orbital frontal regions), temporal lobe regions (except the left entorhinal cortex, fusiform, and transverse temporal regions), in the cingulate regions (except the left rostral cingulate and caudal anterior cingulate regions), parietal lobe regions, and occipital lobe (except the right lateral occipital region). After FDR correction, significant differences in NDI were identified at both mcSUVR cutoffs (1.2 and 1.42) in the frontal lobe (precentral and superior frontal regions), in the temporal lobe (banks of the superior temporal sulcus, superior temporal, middle temporal, and inferior temporal regions), in the parietal lobe (left supramarginal, left inferior parietal, right precuneus, left post central, and right superior parietal regions), and in the occipital lobe (left pericalcarine and left lateral occipital regions). In contrast, volume‐based measurements did not exhibit significant group differences after FDR correction in the left and right hemispheres at either mcSUVR cutoff, 1.2 or 1.42 (except the right superior frontal region). Both NDI and MDI at the 1.42 and 1.2 cutoffs showed statistically significant differences between Aβ − and Aβ + groups, even after adjusting for age, sex, and APOE ε4 status (Table S3 ). In the NDI model, age, sex, and APOE ε4 status did not play a significant role. However, unlike NDI, age played a significant role in the MDI model in quite a few regions. In contrast, volumetric measurements did not show statistical differences after adjusting for age, sex, and APOE ε4 status. The data in Table 2 show the percentage of NDI and volume differences for three subcortical GM regions – hippocampus, thalamus, and amygdala – for the two PiB mcSUVR cutoff levels of 1.2 and 1.42. For both cutoff levels, the NDI and volumes were significantly lower in the Aβ + groups, suggesting the presence of atrophy and additional neurodegeneration in the remaining tissue. We did not include in our analysis any iron‐rich structures as iron increases R 2 t ∗ and can bias NDI measurements. Separation of these two effects can be achieved by the method developed in Yablonskiy et al., 33 which will be a subject of future consideration. Number of participants in each group and their cognitive status are defined in Table 1 . 4 DISCUSSION Previous reports linked amyloid burden to synaptic density alterations using SV2A PET approaches 34 and to regional cortical thinning in clinically normal people. 12 Synaptic loss is now recognized as one of the earliest structural consequences of amyloid accumulation, preceding neuronal death and overt cognitive decline. 35 Our results extend this evidence by demonstrating, through in vivo qGRE MRI, that preatrophic neuronal and myelin damage accompanies amyloid pathology at a stage when conventional volumetric MRI shows few significant changes. While hippocampal and entorhinal volume loss are sensitive markers of mild cognitive impairment, cortical thinning in temporal, parietal, and frontal regions typically emerges as the disease progresses to dementia. 36 , 37 , 38 However, cortical thickness measures exhibit less sensitivity to detect amyloid‐related changes that occur before atrophy become visible, 13 and structural volume differences often show insignificant changes at low amyloid burden. 12 Brain microstructural changes detected using diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) have included decreased fractional anisotropy and increased diffusivity at high amyloid levels, consistent with demyelination and axonal degeneration. 39 In cortical GM, DTI metrics were linked to both Aβ and tau accumulation in cognitively unimpaired and MCI individuals, with cortical diffusion appearing more sensitive to early Aβ‐related microstructural changes than cortical thickness. 13 These studies established that MRI‐detectable microstructural changes in both GM and WM preceded gross atrophy in the amyloid‐driven cascade of AD providing the broader context within which the qGRE‐based NDI and MDI biomarkers introduced here offer a mechanistically grounded, biophysically specific complement to existing structural MRI approaches. Our study identified several new key findings. Significantly lower NDI in the cortical GM and MDI in subcortical WM were observed in participants with PiB amyloid mcSUVR values exceeding 1.42, while volumetric measurements revealed substantially fewer group differences. Significant NDI and MDI group differences were observed even when the Aβ+ group was restricted to participants with mcSUVR &lt; 2.4, while volumetric differences emerged only when the upper mcSUVR limit was extended to 3.4. Significant NDI reductions were also identified in subcortical GM structures such as the hippocampus, thalamus, and amygdala. Importantly, significant microstructural neurodegeneration was present in cognitively normal Aβ + individuals but absent in Aβ − cognitively impaired participants, confirming that the observed changes were driven by amyloid pathology rather than cognitive status. The pattern of NDI reductions most prominent in the middle temporal, superior temporal, supramarginal, and entorhinal cortices aligns with the hierarchical amyloid spreading sequence described by Thal et al., 40 with these neocortical association areas corresponding to Thal phase 1 of early to intermediate stages of amyloid spread. The vulnerability in these regions is consistent with their high baseline synaptic and neuropil complexity. 41 In vivo SV2A PET studies have consistently shown that synaptic loss in AD is most widespread and severe in precisely these medial temporal and neocortical association regions. 42 , 43 According to the THB model, 7 R2t *‐derived NDI is proportional to the density of cellular and myelin membranes, meaning that regions with high baseline membrane density, as reflected in their rich neuronal process architecture, provide a greater substrate for early Aβ‐mediated membrane disruption, rendering them particularly sensitive to NDI reductions at the earliest stages of amyloid pathology. MDI reductions in precentral, middle temporal, and parahippocampal WM tracts connecting these regions are consistent with secondary Wallerian degeneration downstream of cortical neuronal loss, a mechanism well documented in AD. 44 , 45 The robustness of MDI reductions across both amyloid threshold and after FDR correction across 25 subcortical WM regions is further supported by recent experimental evidence from Depp et al., 8 who demonstrated that myelin dysfunction acted not only as a consequence but as an upstream driver of Aβ deposition in mouse models by accumulating the Aβ‐producing machinery in axonal swellings and distracting microglia from plaque clearance. This bidirectional relationship positions MDI as a biomarker that captures both a consequence and a potential driver of the amyloid cascade, consistent with broader evidence that WM abnormalities and oligodendrocyte dysfunction are early and widespread features of AD pathology. 45 , 46 The demonstration that NDI‐ and MDI‐based group differences survive adjustment for age, sex, and APOE ε4 status strengthens the interpretation that the observed changes are driven by amyloid pathology itself. Notably, age played a significant role in the MDI model in several brain regions, consistent with the established link between brain aging, oligodendrocyte dysfunction, and AD risk, 8 , 46 while NDI was largely independent of these covariates. Current eligibility criteria for US Food and Drug Administration‐approved anti‐amyloid therapy using lecanemab 47 and donanemab 48 rely on amyloid‐PET confirmation, 49 and binary classification near the mcSUVR threshold is known to be uncertain. Our qGRE‐based logistic regression model with elastic net regularization predicts amyloid negativity with a probability of about 0.80 to 0.88 at both Aβ + thresholds (mcSUVR ≥ 1.2 and mcSUVR ≥ 1.42). This consistently strong performance highlights the model's reliability for ruling out amyloid abnormality, a property directly relevant for triaging participants for confirmatory amyloid‐PET, especially near the clinical threshold. These findings have potential to improve patient selection for disease‐modifying treatment. We further hypothesize that individuals with minimal preatrophic neurodegeneration may respond better to anti‐amyloid therapy, while those with more advanced microstructural damage could be better candidates for treatments targeting neuronal or myelin integrity directly. Our work has several limitations. The present cross‐sectional design limits causal inferences, and longitudinal studies are needed to establish the trajectory of NDI and MDI decline relative to amyloid and tau accumulation. The sample size is modest, particularly at the low amyloid burden (mcSUVR 1.2 to 1.6, n = 14), which represents a limitation of the current study. Although the observed trends suggest potential biological relevance, these findings should be interpreted with caution and require validation in larger, independent cohort. The classification analysis requires replication in larger multisite datasets to establish generalizability across scanner types. Although we evaluated classification performance using R2t* in combination with age and APOE status for predicting amyloid positivity, future studies will incorporate additional biomarkers, including blood‐based measures and qGRE‐derived metrics, to further assess and improve prediction of brain amyloid burden. Further, this study focused on group‐based comparisons across mcSUVR thresholds, thus continuous relationships between mcSUVR and imaging measures (e.g., MDI, NDI, or volume) were not evaluated. Future studies using regression framework may provide additional insight into these associations. Finally, the relationship between MDI and WM hyperintensities, increasingly recognized as early co‐occurring features of AD, 50 will be addressed in future work. In conclusion, qGRE‐identified preatrophic neuronal and myelin damage is a predominant, atrophy‐independent manifestation of early brain tissue degeneration concurrent with amyloid accumulation. The THB model provides a mechanistic explanation for NDI and MDI sensitivity: Both biomarkers detect reductions in lipid bilayer membrane density accompanying the degradation of neuronal processes (dendrites and axons) and myelin sheath. The corresponding R2t * changes carry sufficient individual‐level discriminative signal to classify amyloid status with pooled AUC reaching 0.74 in both GM and WM models, with strong performance value relevant to screening. Integration of qGRE measurements with tau PET, plasma biomarkers, and amyloid‐PET staging represents a promising direction for enhancing early detection and improving treatment stratification in AD.
