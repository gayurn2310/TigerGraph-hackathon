Longitudinal multi-modal data prediction model for mild cognitive impairment by deep survival analysis

Background Timely prediction of cognitive decline in patients with Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI) is crucial for guiding optimal therapeutic interventions. In this study, we aimed to develop a deep survival analysis model that leverages longitudinal, multi-modal data to estimate the probability of dementia conversion, thereby facilitating personalized treatment planning in clinical practice. Methods We employed a deep neural network model specifically designed for survival analysis to predict the progression from MCI to Alzheimer’s Disease (AD). The model integrated longitudinal biomarkers, including neuropsychological assessments and neuroimaging measures, along with baseline demographic characteristics and genetic risk factors, using data from the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) database. Results This study enrolled 922 baseline MCI patients for analysis. The predictive performance was evaluated using a test set at time intervals \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta t$$\end{document} = 1, 2, 3, 4 years from the landmark time s = 1. The prognostic model exhibited outstanding predictive capability, attaining cdAUC values of 0.9089 ± 0.01 alongside BS of 0.1651 ± 0.01 with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta t$$\end{document} = 1 year on the test set, when all variable sets were incorporated into the time-dependent Cox survival neural network (tdCoxSNN) model. Through feature significance evaluation, the Functional Activities Questionnaire (FAQ) emerged as the most influential predictive element. Conclusions By systematically integrating diverse longitudinal biomarkers, we developed a dynamic prediction model for MCI using deep survival analysis. This approach enables accurate individual risk stratification, facilitates the early identification of high-risk individuals, and supports informed, personalized clinical decision-making. Background Alzheimer’s disease (AD), recognized as the primary contributor to dementia among elderly populations, represents a gradually worsening neurological condition characterized by memory decline and cognitive dysfunction [ 1 ]. In the United States alone, around 7.2 million adults aged 65 and older are currently living with AD dementia, a figure projected to surge to 13.8 million by 2060 without effective disease-modifying interventions [ 2 ]. Global dementia cases reached approximately 57 million in 2021 with nearly 10 million new cases annually [ 3 ]. Projections indicate this global burden will continue to rise exponentially with population aging, estimated to reach 78 million by 2030 and 152 million by 2050 [ 4 ]. Mild cognitive impairment (MCI) has been established as a pivotal intermediary stage between healthy cognition and dementia development, with a well-documented elevated risk of progressing to AD [ 5 ]. Meta-analyses and large-scale longitudinal cohort studies (including data from the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative, ADNI) have consistently reported that the annual conversion rate from MCI to AD ranges from 10 to 15%, translating to approximately 30–45% of MCI cases progressing to AD within 6 years [ 5 , 6 ]. Epidemiological evidence further supports that MCI serves as a critical prodromal phase for AD, with 40–60% of AD patients having a prior MCI diagnosis [ 7 ], highlighting the clinical significance of targeting MCI for early intervention. Early detection of MCI and accurate prediction of its progression trajectory enable healthcare providers to administer timely preventive strategies and personalized interventions, while reliable predictive models can assist in clinical decision-making for treatment planning and identification of high-risk individuals. A variety of AD-related biomarkers are under investigation to monitor disease progression from MCI to dementia, including cognitive assessments, advanced neuroimaging techniques, and cerebrospinal fluid analyses [ 8 ]. Structural Magnetic Resonance Imaging (sMRI) provides insight into cortical atrophy and other structural abnormalities, serving as a critical tool for understanding the mechanisms of neurodegeneration [ 9 ]. As the major genetic risk factor, the APOEε4 allele may contribute to age-related neuronal vulnerability, white matter (WM) damage, and cognitive decline [ 10 – 12 ]. The identification and systematic evaluation of reliable progression markers have the potential to improve diagnostic accuracy for AD and facilitate the development of personalized interventions that optimize clinical outcomes and quality of life for affected individuals [ 13 ]. Recent works have applied deep learning models to predict MCI-to-AD conversion using data from the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI), yet most approaches solely rely on baseline data such as a single-timepoint MRI scan, cognitive test scores, or genetic biomarkers, failing to leverage the dynamic, longitudinal data that captures disease progression trajectories over time [ 14 – 17 ]. As a neurodegenerative condition marked by gradual deterioration, AD necessitates longitudinal follow-up to effectively track clinical trajectories in MCI patients and mitigate diagnostic inaccuracies [ 18 ]. Many studies have demonstrated that integrating multiple longitudinal biomarkers can substantially enhance predictive performance [ 19 – 21 ]. In contrast, incorporating longitudinal data—including serial MRI scans, repeated neurocognitive assessments, and sequential clinical measurements—offers notable advantages: it enables the capture of subtle, progressive changes in brain structure and cognitive function that are often undetectable in cross-sectional baseline data, thereby improving the model’s distinguish ability [ 18 ]. Many studies have demonstrated that integrating multiple longitudinal biomarkers can substantially enhance predictive performance [ 19 – 21 ]. Most current deep learning methods have been primarily applied to the classification and diagnosis of AD [ 22 – 24 ], while their use in AD prognosis or prediction remains relatively limited—especially studies leveraging longitudinal data [ 25 – 27 ]. Relevant deep learning-based works focused on AD prediction are summarized in Table 1 . Given the critical role of incorporating multiple biomarkers and implementing longitudinal follow-up, we developed a dynamic prediction model for individual risk prediction, building on and extending prior deep survival research. The model combined time-dependent Cox model [ 28 ] with a deep neural network to represent the nonlinear relationship between longitudinal processes and time-to-event processes. Notably, the methodology eliminates dependence on restrictive prior assumptions or conventional parametric constraints while maintaining predictive accuracy, addressing limitations of some earlier models. Second, it effectively leverages existing longitudinal data for model training without requiring explicit modeling of longitudinal trajectories, offering advantages in handling complex temporal dynamics compared to discrete-time approaches. Additionally, we conduct a systematic comparison of the predictive performance of 3 distinct multi-modal biomarker combinations (encompassing demographic characteristics, neuroimaging, neuropsychological assessments, genetic), providing actionable insights into optimal biomarker selection for clinical application. Methods Participants All data in this study were obtained from the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) database, a multicenter, longitudinal observational study designed to integrate comprehensive neuroimaging, clinical, and neuropsychological assessment data for tracking the progression of Alzheimer’s disease. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Detailed information regarding the study protocols, including inclusion and exclusion criteria, is available at http://www.adni-info.org/ . For the ADNI study, written informed consent was obtained for all participants and the study protocol was approved by the institutional review board at each participating center before protocol-specific procedures were performed. We constructed a retrospective cohort by reviewing subjects from 2005 through December 2022. Participants were included if they (1) were diagnosed with MCI at study entry and (2) underwent at least two visits during the study period until AD diagnosis or until December 2022, whichever came first. Cognitive status was systematically reassessed approximately every six months or annually, and was classified as either MCI or AD. Detailed diagnostic criteria for assessing cognitive status are provided in the Supplementary Material. Variables The predictors consisted of baseline characteristics and longitudinal variables. Baseline characteristics encompassed the demographic information including educational attainment, gender, age range, and marital status, along with genetic predisposition indicators such as apolipoprotein E (ApoEε4) allele carriage status. Longitudinal variables from two modalities of biomarkers i.e. cognitive function and structural MRI morphometry were also analyzed. Cognitive functioning underwent comprehensive assessment using eleven neuropsychological assessments: Alzheimer’s Disease Assessment Scale (ADAS), Functional Activities Questionnaire (FAQ), Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Test (RAVLT), Trial Making Test-version B (TMT-B), modified Preclinical Alzheimer’s Cognitive Compositewith Digit Symbol Substitutior (mPACCdigit), modified Preclincal Alzheimer’s Cognitive Composite with Trail B (mPACCtrailsB). Detailed descriptions and calculation methods regarding cognitive assessments are available in the Supplementary Material. It should be noted that in the diagnostic criteria for MCI and AD in the ADNI database, MMSE (Mini-mental state evaluation) and CDRSB (Clinical dementia rating sum of boxes) were all taken into account, which were excluded in the subsequent construction of all prediction models in this study. We applied MICE (Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations, MICE) for missing data. MICE is a widely used and robust technique that generates multiple plausible values for each missing data point based on the observed data, thereby reducing potential biases associated with missing data and improving statistical power. MICE is well-suited to preserve the relationships between variables and avoid the potential biases that might arise from simpler methods such as mean imputation or listwise deletion. Structural MRI (sMRI) data underwent spatial normalization through projection onto a comprehensive gray matter density framework, incorporating 116 neuroanatomical divisions as per the automated anatomical labeling protocol. This parcellation scheme differentiated cerebral territories into 90 cortical subcortical zones and 26 cerebellar subdivisions [ 29 ]. Due to the long time span of the ADNI study, variations in MRI equipment manufacturers, system configurations, and multicenter data acquisition, sequence parameters of neuroimaging may differ. To enhance the availability and mitigate the impact of interscanner variability, a normalization protocol was implemented involving determination of proportional gray matter density values for individual brain structures relative to total intracranial volume. Additional details regarding the preprocessing and feature extraction procedures for sMRI are provided in the Supplementary Material. Multi-modal survival comparison Multi-modal data fusion integrates diverse data sources to provide a comprehensive characterization of disease profiles and facilitate the identification of AD-related biomarkers. To evaluate the prognostic utility of different biomarker combinations for predicting MCI-to-AD conversion, we developed predictive models incorporating four demographic variables, ApoEε4 status, neuropsychological assessments, and neuroimaging data. Three distinct feature sets were constructed, while maintaining consistent hyperparameter configurations across all models: FS1: Demographic information (4 features)+ ApoEε4 + sMRI data (116 features) FS2: Demographic information (4 features) + ApoEε4+ Neuropsychological assesments (11 features) FS3: Demographic information (4 features) + ApoEε4+ Neuropsychological assesments (11 features) + sMRI data (116 features) To assess the predictive performance of three features sets, we employed three distinct models: the time-dependent Cox model (tdCox), time-dependent Cox survival neural network (tdCoxSNN) [ 28 ] and the discrete-time deep learning framework Dynamic-DeepHit [ 35 ]. The dataset was randomly split into a training set and an independent test set (8:2 ratio, n = 184 for the test set). Ten-fold cross-validation(10-fold CV) was performed on the training set for model development: nine subsets were used for training in each iteration, and one subset for internal validation. The mean ± standard deviation values presented in the results were obtained from the independent test set. The cumulative dynamic AUC (cdAUC) and Brier Score (BS) were used to assess the performance of the predictive models. Higher scores of cdAUC and lower scores of BS indicate superior performance, respectively. To evaluate feature significance, measurements of the same features across multiple time points were grouped into unified categories. This study utilized the sparse group lasso approach [ 36 ], integrating both lasso and group lasso penalty terms, to assess the significance of features. Through structured penalties, the group lasso not only can screen out important features, but also can reveal the overall importance pattern of the features over time. Specifically, it preserves non-zero groups while simultaneously selecting key elements within each group, achieving both inter-group sparsity and intra-group sparsity. Evaluation metrics The evaluation of predictive performance in survival models primarily focuses on two key dimensions: calibration and discrimination. Calibration measures the agreement between the predicted probabilities and the observed outcomes, while discrimination evaluates a model’s ability to distinguish between subjects who experience the event and those who do not. Both types of metrics were incorporated in assessing the performance of our models. The tdCoxSNN model demonstrated effective individualized risk prediction and has the potential to facilitate early identification of high-risk individuals, thereby supporting clinical decision-making. Incorporating demographic information, ApoEε4 status, neuropsychological assessments, and neuroimaging data significantly improved predictive performance. Furthermore, we assessed the relative importance of different biomarkers, identifying the FAQ as the most important neuropsychological feature and the FFG as the most critical neuroimaging feature. Dynamic prediction [ 41 ] leverages longitudinal variables collected during follow-up to continuously update risk estimates based on patients’ most recent data. Current approaches for dynamic prediction include joint modeling, which often suffers from computational complexity and reduced performance under model misspecification, and landmarking, which is straightforward to implement but is typically constrained by reliance on proportional hazards assumptions [ 42 , 43 ]. Recent advances in artificial intelligence have enabled the development of novel survival analysis frameworks using deep neural networks [ 44 – 47 ], effectively overcoming limitations of traditional statistical models, such as the need to predefine linear associations between variables and risk functions. These deep learning-based models have demonstrated superior performance compared with conventional approaches in accurately capturing the complex relationships between covariates and time-to-event outcomes [ 45 , 47 ]. The results of our predictive modeling indicate that tdCoxSNN achieved robust and satisfactory performance on our multivariate longitudinal data. Compared with the other two models, the conventional tdCox and the deep learning-based survival model Dynamic-DeepHit, tdCoxSNN consistently outperformed both. Unlike Dynamic-DeepHit, tdCoxSNN does not require the selection of discrete time intervals, thereby simplifying the handling of longitudinal data during model fitting. Notably, tdCoxSNN maintained strong predictive performance across all experimental scenarios using identical network architectures and hyper-parameters, highlighting its inherent robustness. Further tuning of these hyper-parameters may further enhance model accuracy. The tdCoxSNN model is also computationally efficient, with the training loss typically converging within 20 epochs and requiring less than one minute per training session. Additionally, tdCoxSNN can directly integrate high-dimensional longitudinal information, such as neuroimaging data, by stacking additional neural network components. This integrated framework facilitates the development of dynamic predictive models capable of effectively leveraging complex longitudinal biomarkers, including MRI scans and omics data, within a unified neural network architecture. We conducted a structured comparison of three distinct feature set (FS1, FS2, FS3) combinations. This systematic analysis provides empirical evidence on the incremental predictive value of each data modality, offering practical guidance for feature selection in clinical prediction models. Predictive capacity substantially diminished when excluding neuropsychological assessments, highlighting these cognitive assessments as critical predictive elements for modeling MCI-to-AD progression trajectories. We also observed that as the prediction horizon increases, the predictive performance of all three models decreases. This is primarily because for shorter horizons, this assumption is reasonably robust, as longitudinal changes are minimal, and accumulated historical data can effectively constrain the risk score estimation. However, for longer horizons, the discrepancy between the assumed constant predictors and their true dynamic trajectories becomes amplified. Even with more accumulated historical data, the model cannot fully capture the long-term, non-stationary changes in predictors, leading to biased risk score calculations and reduced prediction accuracy. The study conducted a comprehensive evaluation of feature importance, revealing that neuropsychological assessments played a pivotal role in predictive modeling, as evidenced by the top ten most influential variables. Among all longitudinal biomarkers, the FAQ emerged as the most important feature. The FAQ provides a straightforward and clinically applicable measure, with potential to enhance clinical utility. Previous research has shown that the FAQ is effective in distinguishing individuals with MCI from those with early-stage AD [ 48 ]. Engagement in daily activities and social interactions may confer additional benefits, potentially by promoting blood flow within the prefrontal cortex-striatum circuit [ 49 ]. From a temporal perspective, the most important features were collected within the first four years of follow-up, suggesting that early follow-up data are more informative than later measurements for predicting the progression from MCI to AD. We also conducted a prognostic risk stratification analysis, which has substantial clinical significance for distinguishing MCI patients at high risk of progression to AD from those at low risk, who are likely to remain stable or experience slow progression. This stratification enables clinicians to implement targeted interventions and personalized management strategies more effectively. High-risk individuals may benefit from proactive, intensive, and tailored interventions, whereas low-risk patients can follow a more conservative management approach, focusing on maintenance of current status, preventive care, and long-term monitoring. Furthermore, risk stratification supports the prioritization of limited healthcare resources toward high-risk populations most likely to gain significant clinical benefit, thereby enhancing the efficiency and effectiveness of healthcare delivery. Our study had several limitations. Firstly, the restricted sample size and characteristics of subjects drawn exclusively from the ADNI database. The external validation on publicly available cohorts (e.g., NACC) is not feasible in the current study, primarily due to inconsistencies in data modalities and longitudinal follow-up protocols between these public cohorts and our research objectives for AD progression survival analysis, which would lead to incomparable and biased validation results. As a result, this study relied solely on internal validation, which may constrain the generalizability of the findings. However, The reported performance reflects internal validation within ADNI would further ensure accurate interpretation. Secondly, while utilizing longitudinal whole-brain structural neuroimaging, our study focused on region-of-interest-based volumetric measurements obtained through prior-knowledge-guided brain tissue segmentation. Future studies could employ advanced techniques like deep neural networks to capture spatial variations and subtle patterns in brain structure. Thirdly, multi-modal biomarkers can be extended to other modalities, such as biomarkers in cerebrospinal fluid, including β-amyloid protein, total tau protein, and phosphorylated tau protein, which are key elements reflecting the pathological physiology of AD. The current investigation specifically centers on the APOE ε4 genetic variant. A paramount direction for future research is the multi-modal integration of this model with CSF biomarker and multiple genetic variants to develop a more robust and clinically comprehensive prognostic tool. Conclusion In summary, this study developed a dynamic risk prediction model utilizing longitudinal biomarkers to estimate the risk of progression from MCI to AD. The model integrates a time-dependent Cox framework with a deep neural network, enabling accurate individualized risk prediction for MCI patients. We further evaluated the relative importance of longitudinal biomarkers and performed prognostic stratification within the MCI cohort by classifying individuals into high-risk and low-risk groups. This approach may facilitate the early identification of individuals at elevated risk and support more informed, personalized clinical decision-making.
