Increased type 3 deiodinase reduces hippocampal T3 signaling and is associated with cognitive impairment in a sporadic Alzheimer’s disease animal model

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by amyloid-β and tau pathology, neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, and cognitive decline. Emerging evidence indicates that thyroid hormone (TH) dysregulation may also contribute to AD pathogenesis, particularly through impaired hippocampal signaling. THs are crucial for neuronal metabolism, plasticity, and mitochondrial integrity, and their local availability is tightly controlled by deiodinases. This study investigated the role of type 3 deiodinase (D3) and its redox-dependent regulation via glutathione (GSH) in a streptozotocin (STZ)-induced model of sporadic AD. Male Wistar rats received intracerebroventricular STZ or vehicle and were evaluated 16 weeks later. STZ-treated rats exhibited significant deficits in short-term ( p = 0.0090) and long-term ( p = 0.0117) memory, accompanied by elevated hippocampal IL-6 and TNF-α ( p &lt; 0.0001), increased protein carbonylation ( p = 0.0162), and depletion of GSH ( p = 0.0326) and sulfhydryl groups ( p = 0.0165). D3 mRNA ( p &lt; 0.0001) and protein ( p = 0.0247) were upregulated, while T3-responsive genes Klf9 ( p &lt; 0.0001) and HR ( p = 0.0353) were downregulated, indicating impaired TH signaling. Mitochondrial dysfunction was evidenced by reduced expression of Nrf1 ( p = 0.0337), PGC-1α ( p = 0.0035), Bcl2 ( p = 0.0305), and COX4 ( p = 0.0077), with increased CHOP ( p = 0.0034), GRP78 ( p &lt; 0.0001), and ERK activation ( p &lt; 0.0001).Together, these findings demonstrate that inflammation- and redox-driven D3 upregulation decreases hippocampal T3 availability, disrupting TH signaling and promoting mitochondrial dysfunction and cognitive decline in sporadic AD. Introduction Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a growing global public health challenge and the leading cause of dementia, accounting for 60 to 80% of all cases 1 . While aging remains the primary risk factor, the pathogenesis of AD is multifactorial, involving complex interactions between genetic susceptibility and modifiable conditions including hypertension and type 2 diabetes mellitus 2 . The hallmark lesions of AD—extracellular amyloid-β (Aβ) plaques and intracellular neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs) composed of hyperphosphorylated tau—are accompanied by robust neuroinflammatory responses 3 , 4 . These involve the activation of glial cells and the release of proinflammatory mediators, perpetuating a toxic environment that exacerbates neuronal dysfunction 5 , 6 . This persistent inflammatory state 7 , together with oxidative stress and synaptic impairment, contributes to a self-reinforcing cycle of neurodegeneration 8 – 10 that underlies the progressive cognitive decline and neuropsychiatric disturbances characteristic of the disease trajectory 11 . Although the classic mechanisms of AD have been extensively described, growing evidence suggests that other factors may also play a critical role in the onset and progression of the disease. Among these, disruptions in endocrine signaling particularly involving thyroid hormones (THs) have emerged as critical modulators of neurodegenerative processes, with increasing data implicating altered cerebral TH signaling in AD pathogenesis 12 . THs play a fundamental role in various physiological processes and are essential in the brain, where they regulate neurogenesis, myelination, synaptic plasticity, and neuronal metabolism processes crucial for neurodevelopment and the maintenance of cognitive function 13 . The hippocampus, which undergoes early and pronounced degeneration in AD, is particularly sensitive to TH signaling 14 . Among THs, triiodothyronine (T3) plays a pivotal role in modulating synaptic plasticity, glial cell activity, and energy metabolism within this region. Experimental studies have demonstrated that alterations in TH availability can disrupt hippocampal function, potentially contributing to memory deficits and accelerating the progression of neurodegenerative processes 15 , 16 . Moreover, recent studies suggest that THs may be influenced by the brain’s redox environment, partly through their effects on glutathione (GSH) metabolism 17 – 19 . GSH is a critical antioxidant in the brain, protecting neurons against oxidative damage and maintaining redox homeostasis. In AD, impaired GSH metabolism is implicated in the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and oxidative stress, which exacerbate neurodegeneration 19 . The local bioavailability of T3 is precisely regulated by a family of selenoenzymes known as deiodinases types 1, 2, and 3 (D1, D2, and D3). While D1 and D2 catalyze the conversion of thyroxine (T4) to active T3 20 , D3 exclusively inactivates T4 and T3 into reverse T3 (rT3) and T2, respectively. These enzymes are oxidoreductases, requiring reducing equivalents primarily low-molecular-weight thiols such as GSH to sustain catalytic activity 21 . This redox dependency is particularly significant in the context of AD, where oxidative stress is a prominent feature. GSH not only supports deiodinase function but also plays a critical role in cellular defense. Therefore, disruptions in redox homeostasis potentiate alterations in D3 activity 22 , impairing local THs metabolism and potentially reducing neuronal resilience. Although D3 upregulation is documented in various pathological states, its involvement in AD remains largely unexplored. Increased D3 activity in the brain could diminish intracellular T3 availability, thereby compromising mitochondrial function and endoplasmic reticulum (ER) homeostasis two key pathways implicated in neurodegeneration. Despite growing recognition of the link between thyroid hormone signaling and AD, the specific mechanisms of TH inactivation—particularly via D3—and their impact on hippocampal neuronal viability remain poorly understood. To address this gap, the present study investigates the molecular mechanisms underlying TH inactivation and assesses its contribution to cognitive impairment in a rodent model of sporadic AD, thereby advancing our understanding of thyroid dysfunction as a potential driver of neurodegeneration. Materials and methods Animals The experiment used sixteen male Wistar rats, 90 days old, weighing 300–340 g, from the Animal Experimentation and Reproduction Center of the Animal Laboratory (CREAL). The animals were kept under alternating light and darkness conditions (12 h/12 h) and a constant temperature of 22 ± 2 °C, with free access to food and water. They were housed in polyethylene cages on a bed of autoclaved pine shavings. All experimental procedures involving animals were conducted in accordance with the standards of the Ethics Committee on Animal Use (CEUA) of the Federal University of Rio Grande do Sul, in compliance with the Brazilian Arouca Law (Law No. 11,794, October 8, 2008), and were approved under protocol No. 41,744 by CEUA/UFRGS. This study was reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines ( https://arriveguidelines.org ). The sample size was determined based on an effect size of 0.53, a statistical power of 90%, and a significance level of p &lt; 0.05, using behavioral test parameters as reference, resulting in 8 animals per group. Randomization was performed by an independent researcher using a list of random numbers generated at Random.org. Each animal received a numerical code and was randomly allocated to experimental groups, ensuring balance in body weight and age. The analyses were conducted in a blinded manner regarding group allocation. The animals were randomly divided into two experimental groups (Control and STZ) and followed for 16 weeks after sham or ICV surgery. At the end, cognitive and neurochemical parameters were evaluated, as shown in the experimental design in Fig. 1 . Fig. 1 Experimental schedule. Sixteen male Wistar rats were randomly assigned to two experimental groups: control and streptozotocin (STZ)-treated. Both groups underwent intracerebroventricular surgery to administer HBSS (control) or STZ. The animals were monitored for 16 weeks, at the end of which the novel object recognition (NOR) test was performed. Twenty-four hours after the test, the animals were euthanized, and their hippocampi were collected for molecular and biochemical analyses. Surgical procedure Intracerebroventricular infusion of STZ is used as an experimental and preclinical model of sporadic AD 23 , 24 . Briefly, animals were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of ketamine (75 mg/Kg) and xylazine (10 mg/Kg) and placed in a stereotaxic apparatus. A sagittal incision was made in the midline of the scalp. The lateral ventricles were accessed by the following coordinates: 0.9 mm posterior to the bregma 1.5 mm lateral to sagittal suture of 1.5 mm; 3.6 mm beneath the surface of the brain. The rats received a single bilateral infusion in each hemisphere (using a Hamilton syringe, model 701) of 5 µL STZ (3 mg/kg) or vehicle (Hank balanced salt solution) containing, in mM: 137 NaCl; 0.63 Na 2 HPO 4 ; 4.17 NaHCO 3 ; 5.36 KCl; 0.44 KH 2 PO 4 ; 1.26 CaCl 2 ; 0.41 MgSO 4 ; 0.49 MgCl 2 , and 10 glucose, pH 7.4. Novel object recognition test (NOR) The NOR test was used to assess short- and long-term memory in rodents, based on innate exploratory behavior. The protocol consisted of three stages: habituation (day 1), with free exploration of the open field (50 × 50 cm) for 10 min; training (day 2), with exposure to two identical objects for 5 min; and, after 1 h, the short-term memory test, in which one of the objects was replaced by a new one. On day 3, the long-term memory test was performed, replacing the new object with a third, also new, object. The Recognition Index was calculated as: (time exploring the new object × 100)/(total exploration time) 25 . Euthanasia and tissue collection Sixteen weeks after STZ administration, cognitive performance was evaluated using the novel object recognition test. At the end of the experimental protocol, animals were deeply anesthetized by intraperitoneal administration of a supradose of ketamine (150 mg/kg) combined with xylazine (30 mg/kg). This anesthetic regimen was used exclusively to induce deep anesthesia prior to euthanasia, in accordance with institutional ethical guidelines and internationally accepted animal welfare standards, ensuring rapid loss of consciousness and the absence of pain or distress. Following confirmation of deep anesthesia, animals were decapitated, and hippocampal tissues were rapidly collected, immediately frozen, and stored at − 80 °C for subsequent neurochemical and molecular analyses. Real-time PCR Total RNA was extracted from tissue samples using the trizol method. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using the SuperScript First-Strand Synthesis System for RT-PCR (Invitrogen). Quantitative real-time PCR was subsequently performed using SYBR Green PowerUp PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems) on an ABI Prism VII7 Sequence Detection System (Applied Biosystems). Standard curves exhibited coefficients of determination (R²) greater than 0.99, and amplification efficiencies ranged from 80% to 100%. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCt method, with values normalized to the reference gene(s) and expressed relative to the sham or untreated control group. Carbonyl measurement The technique to assess Carbonyl concentrations was performed as described 26 . Duplicate aliquots of 0.3 mg of protein homogenate of each tissue were incubated with 500 µL of 10 mM 2.4-dinitrophenylhydrazine or 1.0 mL of 2 M HCl (blank tube). 250 µL of 50% trichloroacetic acid was added to the aliquots as previously described. The samples were subsequently centrifuged at 8,000 g for 30 min to obtain the protein pellets, immediately washed with ethanol-ethyl acetate at a 1:1 (v/v) ratio. The difference between the 2.4-dinitrophenylhydrazine-treated and HCl-treated samples (blank) was used to calculate the carbonyl content determined at 370 nm. Carbonyl content was calculated using the millimolar absorption coefficient of hydrazine (e370 nm = 21,000000 M-1 cm-1), and the results expressed in nmol carbonyl/mg protein. Malondialdehyde levels The technique to assess MDA concentrations was performed as previously described 27 . The fluorescence of the organic phase was read at wavelengths of 515 and 553 nm of excitation and emission, respectively. A calibration curve was performed with 1,1,3,3-tetra- methoxypropane and subjected to the same treatment as the supernatant. MDA levels 137 were calculated as nanomoles of MDA/mg protein. Sulfhydryl content Sulfhydryl content was measured accordingly 28 . Where the reduction of 5,5′-dithio-bis (2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB) by thiols generates a yellow derivative (TNB) and measured at 412 nm. In brief, 30 µL of 0.1 mM DTNB was added to 120 µL of tissue supernatants. A 30-min incubation followed this at room temperature in a dark room. Results were calculated as nmol of TNB/mg of protein. Antioxidant defenses Reduced glutathione (GSH) levels were measured according to a standard method 29 . Briefly, tissues were homogenized in the presence of 300 µL of 20 mM sodium phosphate and 140mM KCl buffer, pH 7.4. Samples were centrifuged, and fifteen microliters of tissue preparation incubated with an equal volume of ophthaldialdehyde (1 mg/mL methanol) at room temperature for 15 min in the presence of 20 volumes (1:20, v/v) of 100 mM sodium phosphate buffer, pH 8.0, containing 5mM EDTA. Fluorescence was measured using wavelengths of 350 nm and 420 nm. GSH concentrations calculated as nmol/mg protein. Discussion This study investigated T3 availability and its relationship with cognitive function in a 16-week experimental model of sporadic AD. Our findings demonstrate that the long-term effects of AD induction - particularly neuroinflammation and redox imbalance - not only impair cognition but also disrupt local TH metabolism in the brain. Specifically, we observed an upregulation of D3 expression and function, accompanied by a downregulation of T3-regulated genes, indicating a reduced availability of T3. This low T3 state was further associated with impaired mitochondrial function, ER stress, and increased apoptotic signaling 16 weeks after ICV STZ administration. Collectively, our results highlight a significant disruption of TH signaling in the brain, which may exacerbate neuronal dysfunction and contribute to the progression of cognitive decline and neurodegenerative processes in this model of sporadic AD. ICV administration of STZ has been widely used by our research group and others as a well-established model for inducing morphological, molecular, and behavioral alterations that recapitulate key aspects of the pathophysiology of sporadic AD 34 . In the present study, consistent with previous findings from our group 23 , we observed that a single ICV dose of 3 mg/kg STZ led to early cognitive deficits, detectable at 4 weeks 35 and persisting through 16 weeks post-injection 36 , as confirmed in the present study (Fig. 2 ). Cognitive performance was assessed 16 weeks after STZ administration using the NOR task. Animals in the STZ group exhibited a significant reduction in recognition index at 1 h and 24 h after training, indicating deficits in short-term (Fig. 2 a) and long-term (Fig. 2 b) memory. These findings corroborate previous reports indicating that STZ administration leads to hippocampal-dependent cognitive decline, mimicking features of sporadic AD 37 . In previous studies by our group, in addition to the cognitive deficits reported here, we observed marked astroglial reactivity, evidenced by increased expression of glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), reduced levels of glutamine synthetase (GS), and elevated monoamine oxidase-B (MAO-B) in the STZ-induced AD model at the amyloidogenic phase (16 weeks) 36 . Although these glial and neurochemical markers were not assessed in the present study, prior findings support our current hypotheses, as astrocytic dysfunction—combined with STZ-induced neurotoxicity—likely contributes to neurochemical imbalance and subsequent cognitive decline 36 , 38 . Taken together, these results reinforce the validity of the intracerebroventricular STZ model as a relevant experimental approach for investigating key pathophysiological mechanisms underlying sporadic AD. Notably, neuroinflammation appears to act not merely downstream of these changes but as a key driver of the degenerative cascade interacting synergistically with astrocytic dysfunction. It is important to highlight that the observed behavioral deficits are closely accompanied by robust neuroinflammatory changes, which are now recognized as key contributors to the onset and progression of neurodegeneration in AD 39 . In the context of the ICV-STZ model, neuroinflammation is not merely a secondary phenomenon but rather a central pathogenic mechanism. STZ administration triggers chronic activation of microglia 6 , 40 , the brain’s resident immune cells, which adopt a proinflammatory phenotype characterized by the release of cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, as well as increased expression of reactive oxygen species (ROS). These mediators not only disrupt neuronal homeostasis but also impair synaptic integrity and plasticity—processes essential for learning and memory consolidation 41 , 42 and are tightly regulated by transcriptional inflammatory pathways. Among these pathways, the pro-inflammatory transcription factor NF-κB has been implicated as a central regulator of neuroinflammation in Alzheimer’s disease. NF-κB mediates the expression of cytokines, chemokines, and other inflammatory mediators that exacerbate neuronal damage in both human and experimental models. Recent evidence highlights the dual role of NF-κB in neurons and glial cells, supporting its contribution to disease progression and its potential as a therapeutic target 43 . Consistently, experimental modulation of NF-κB signaling in neurodegenerative models has been shown to reduce neuroinflammation and improve cognitive outcomes, underscoring the mechanistic relevance of this pathway in Alzheimer’s pathology 44 . Sustained activation of NF-κB and the resulting persistent pro-inflammatory state promote mitochondrial dysfunction and exacerbate oxidative damage, establishing a feed-forward loop that perpetuates cellular stress and neuronal loss 35 , 45 . This self-amplifying cycle of inflammation and oxidative stress is consistent with the well-documented redox imbalance in AD brains and supports the notion that targeting neuroinflammation could represent a viable therapeutic strategy. In the present study, the STZ-induced model recapitulated these pathological hallmarks, as evidenced by elevated markers of neuroinflammation and oxidative stress (Figs. 3 and 4 , and 5 ), thereby validating its relevance for investigating early pathophysiological events in sporadic AD. Effect size analysis further supported the robustness of these alterations, revealing biologically large effects across inflammatory, redox, mitochondrial, and thyroid hormone–related pathways, including parameters showing marginal statistical significance that nonetheless exhibited substantial biological relevance (Supplementary Table S1 ). Furthermore, the chronic inflammatory environment influences not only classical neurodegenerative pathways but also local TH signaling, particularly through the modulation of deiodinase activity. Astrocytes, via D2, convert T4 into T3 and deliver it to neurons, thereby supporting synaptic plasticity, energy metabolism, and cholinergic and glutamatergic neurotransmission 46 . In contrast, neurons predominantly express D3, which inactivates T3 and finely tunes its intracellular availability 47 . Neuroinflammation and oxidative stress can destabilize this delicate balance, underscoring the critical role of TH metabolism in astrocyte–neuron communication. Classical studies have shown that oxidative stress and proinflammatory cytokines significantly alter deiodinase expression and activity, ultimately impairing local TH availability 17 , 48 . In particular, D3—the enzyme responsible for T3 inactivation—is often upregulated under conditions of cellular stress, hypoxia, and inflammation. Increased D3 activity decreases local T3 concentrations, thereby compromising key processes such as neurogenesis, synaptic plasticity, and neuronal energy homeostasis 49 . Supporting this concept, a recent study by Sepúlveda et al. (2024) demonstrated that hippocampal T3 availability is reduced in a 4 week STZ-induced model of sporadic AD. This reduction was associated with cognitive impairment, heightened inflammatory markers, and increased apoptotic signaling. Notably, T3 supplementation in that model was sufficient to reverse these pathological changes, suggesting a protective role of T3 in neuronal homeostasis and survival 12 . In our study, we expanded on these observations by confirming a sustained reduction in T3 signaling at a later stage (16 weeks post-STZ), evidenced by decreased expression of T3-regulated genes (Fig. 6 c and d). Unlike previous studies that primarily reported changes in T3 levels or the effects of hormone replacement therapy, our investigation focused on the underlying mechanisms responsible for the T3 reduction. In this context, we observed a significant upregulation of D3 expression, which we interpret as a direct consequence of the proinflammatory and pro-oxidative environment characteristic of this AD model. In this scenario, the exacerbated D3 action induced by STZ toxicity may compromise the balance of the astrocyte-neuron axis and thyroid metabolism, reducing T3 signaling in neurons and favoring cognitive dysfunction. This situation creates a paradox: while astrocytes act as a source of active T3, neuronal D3 hyperactivity removes it, leading to a state of “local hypothyroidism” in the brain. Thus, disruption of this astroglial-neuronal communication contributes to the loss of synaptic integrity, impaired neurotransmission, and increased neuronal vulnerability to the degenerative processes that characterize the experimental AD phenotype 47 , 50 . These findings are in line with previous literature, indicating that D3 is an inflammation-sensitive enzyme whose expression is amplified in hostile microenvironments, ultimately leading to intracellular T3 depletion and impaired TH-mediated transcriptional activity. Although D3 upregulation may initially serve as an adaptive response to cellular stress, its chronic activation under sustained neuroinflammatory conditions likely leads to a pathological suppression of TH signaling 17 , 48 , perpetuating neuronal dysfunction. Sustained suppression of TH signaling perpetuates a state of chronic energy deficit and cellular vulnerability, creating a negative feedback loop that exacerbates neuronal damage. Thus, our results highlight the contribution of altered TH metabolism—driven by this now-pathological D3 expression—as a critical and underrecognized factor in the pathophysiology of AD. The consequences of reduced T3 availability in the brain appear to be profound, particularly in neurons, which rely heavily on TH signaling to maintain both metabolic efficiency and structural stability. In our study, decreased expression of T3-responsive genes was accompanied by molecular hallmarks of mitochondrial dysfunction (Fig. 7 ), ER stress (Fig. 8 ), and activation of apoptotic pathways (Fig. 7 c) within the hippocampus of STZ-treated animals. These findings reinforce the concept that impaired T3 signaling leads to widespread cellular dysregulation, compromising multiple layers of neuronal homeostasis. Given the essential role of T3 in maintaining mitochondrial oxidative capacity, the observed downregulation of Nrf1, PGC-1α and COX4 suggests that reduced T3 availability directly impairs energy metabolism in neurons (Fig. 7 a, b and d). Concurrently, the increased expression of ER stress markers, including CHOP and GRP78 (Fig. 8 a and b), suggests that disruption of T3-mediated control over protein folding, trafficking, and degradation mechanisms contributes to proteostatic collapse—an established pathological hallmark of AD 51 . In addition, we observed a downregulation of the anti-apoptotic gene Bcl-2, indicating a shift toward a pro-apoptotic molecular profile and increased neuronal vulnerability. These findings collectively suggest that sustained T3 deficiency not only disrupts metabolic and proteostatic balance but also promotes apoptotic signaling, potentially accelerating neurodegenerative processes (NDPs) 52 . Our results are consistent with a growing body of evidence positioning TH signaling as a key regulator of neuronal resilience. When this axis is compromised—particularly through inflammation-driven upregulation of D3 and consequent inactivation of T3—neurons may become markedly susceptible to secondary insults such as oxidative stress, chronic inflammation, and aberrant protein accumulation, all of which contribute to the progression of NDPs. Conclusions Taken together, our findings suggest that altered TH metabolism—particularly the inflammation-driven upregulation of D3—constitutes a pathogenic mechanism that may amplify neurodegenerative cascades in sporadic AD. This dysregulation leads to a local reduction in T3 availability, contributing to mitochondrial dysfunction, endoplasmic reticulum stress, and activation of apoptotic pathways, ultimately compromising neuronal homeostasis. The interplay between neuroinflammation, redox imbalance, and impaired TH signaling thus forms a novel and underexplored pathogenic triad in AD, with each component reinforcing the other in a self-perpetuating cycle of neurodegeneration. These insights underscore the pivotal role of TH signaling in maintaining neuronal integrity and point toward new therapeutic avenues. Approaches targeting deiodinase activity—such as the use of selective D3 inhibitors—or restoring local T3 action through brain-penetrant analogues and modulation of redox balance may represent promising strategies to mitigate metabolic dysfunction and cognitive decline. Elucidating the molecular mechanisms that connect inflammation, oxidative stress, and TH dysregulation may not only deepen our understanding of AD pathophysiology but also guide the development of targeted therapies capable of halting or reversing disease progression. Limitations Some limitations of this work are worth mentioning. First, the importance of sex in generating the Alzheimer’s model should be considered, as it may influence the severity and progression of disease-related outcomes 53 . Additionally, behavioral, neurochemical, and molecular parameters were assessed at a single time point, 16 weeks after STZ administration. Although this time frame is appropriate for evaluating chronic effects of STZ, the inclusion of multiple time points would provide a more dynamic view of disease progression and D3 dysregulation, helping to distinguish whether the observed alterations represent early, intermediate, or late events within the pathological cascade. Finally, while inflammatory status was assessed through IL-6 and TNF-α measurements, the lack of evaluation of IL-1β, a key pro-inflammatory cytokine implicated in neuroinflammation and Alzheimer’s disease pathology, represents a limitation of the present study.
