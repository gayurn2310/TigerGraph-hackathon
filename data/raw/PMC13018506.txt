Perivascular fluid clearance links choroid plexus changes to cognitive performance in cerebral small vessel disease

Choroid plexus (CP) dysfunction may impair cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) turnover and perivascular glymphatic clearance, but whether CP microstructural injury correlates to cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD)-related brain alteration and cognitive performance remains unclear. We investigated whether imaging markers of perivascular fluid transport mediate the associations between CP alterations and CSVD pathology. CP microstructure was assessed by mean apparent propagator (MAP) diffusion imaging and χ-separation susceptibility mapping in 139 CSVD patients and 52 healthy controls. Perivascular clearance measures included the diffusion tensor image-along perivascular space (DTI-ALPS) index, basal ganglia free-water fraction (FW-BG), and perivascular space volume fraction (PVSVF-BG). Compared with controls, CSVD patients showed CP microstructural abnormalities and altered perivascular clearance markers, which was identified by random-forest model as predictors of CSVD severity (OOB-AUC = 0.755, 95%CI: 0.641–0.836) . Mediation analysis revealed that imaging markers of perivascular fluid clearance significantly mediated the associations between CP alterations and structural brain injury (average causal mediation effect , ACME=-110.118 to 80.121, FDR-p &lt; 0.05). Notably, PVSVF-BG was the only glymphatic imaging metric mediating the links of both CP volume and susceptibility with cognitive performance (ACME=-142.474 to 67.351, FDR-p &lt; 0.05). These findings indicate that CP microstructural injury are present in mild CSVD with the absence of CP volumetric enlargement, and are associated with CSVD markers and cognitive performance, which are mediated by disrupted perivascular fluid transport, highlighting the CP-glymphatic axis as a candidate pathway for further longitudinal and mechanistic investigation. Introduction Cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD) is a common cerebrovascular disorder and a leading cause of vascular cognitive impairment and dementia in the elderly [ 1 , 2 ]. It involves structural and functional abnormalities of small cerebral vessels, leading to characteristic neuroimaging features such as white matter hyperintensities (WMH), lacunes, and cerebral microbleeds (CMB) [ 3 ]. These imaging markers are strongly associated with an increased risk of stroke and progressive cognitive decline [ 2 ]. Despite its clinical significance, the pathobiological mechanisms of CSVD remain incompletely understood, and no proven disease-modifying therapies are currently available, highlighting an urgent need for mechanistic studies. Growing evidence suggests that impaired glymphatic function—a perivascular clearance pathway whereby cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) circulates through perivascular spaces (PVS) to remove metabolic waste—may contribute to CSVD-related brain injury and cognitive decline [ 4 ]. The choroid plexus (CP), which produces approximately 65–80% of the CSF, influences perivascular fluid circulation by regulating CSF production and composition [ 5 , 6 ]. Composed of a fenestrated capillary network surrounded by a monolayer of epithelial cells and stromal immune cells, the CP also serves as the blood-CSF barrier [ 7 ]. Enlargement or dysfunction of the CP has been associated with inflammation and disrupted CSF homeostasis, suggesting a link to waste clearance [ 8 ]. Nevertheless, the interplay between CP abnormalities and impaired perivascular fluid transport in CSVD remains unclear, providing a rationale for this study. Given its high vascularity, the CP is susceptible to microvascular damage in CSVD [ 9 ]. Prior studies have linked increased CP volume to greater WMH burden and overall CSVD severity but have seldom explored CP microstructural changes [ 10 , 11 ]. A recent report found that impaired glymphatic clearance partially mediated the association between CP volume and processing speed in patients with extensive WMH, implying an interaction between CP morphology, fluid clearance and cognitive function [ 12 ]. However, it remains unknown whether CP microstructural alterations accompany perivascular CSF clearance dysfunction in CSVD, and how each contributes to structural brain alterations and clinical outcomes. We hypothesized that CSVD-related microvascular injury may disrupt CP microstructure, and perturb CSF dynamics, leading to impaired perivascular fluid flow and worse neurological outcomes. Notably, existing MRI studies have predominantly focused on macroscale CP morphology [ 13 , 14 ], while microstructural changes remain underexplored. Histological evidence indicates the CP undergoes age- and disease-related alterations, including epithelial atrophy, tight junction loss, fibrosis, and calcification [ 15 – 17 ]. Recent neuroimaging advances enable us to probe these features in vivo: the mean apparent propagator (MAP) diffusion MRI can estimate non-Gaussian diffusion metrics sensitive to cellularity and structural complexity [ 18 ]; and sub-voxel quantitative susceptibility mapping (QSM) with χ-separation can distinguish paramagnetic (iron/hemosiderin) from diamagnetic (calcification) components, allowing characterization of CP tissue composition [ 19 ]. Concurrently, neuroimaging biomarkers have emerged to assess distinct features of perivascular/glymphatic fluid dynamics. For example, MRI-visible PVS was reported to be spatially aligned with the trajectory of small arterioles and may indicate CSF influx along perivascular pathways [ 20 ]. The diffusion tensor imaging‑along the perivascular space (DTI‑ALPS) index quantifies water diffusivity along the deep medullary vein at the lateral ventricle body level. It has been shown to correlate with glymphatic clearance rate measured by intrathecal gadolinium MRI and is therefore considered as an indirect proxy for perivascular CSF efflux [ 21 ]. Free-water (FW) estimates the extracellular water volume fraction and is suspected to reflect CSF- interstitial fluid exchange [ 22 , 23 ]. Growing evidence has linked these biomarkers to CSVD severity and cognitive deficits [ 22 , 24 ], underscoring the potential role of impaired perivascular clearance in this disease process. In this study we integrated structural, diffusion and susceptibility‑weighted MRI to comprehensively characterize in vivo CP microstructure and glymphatic fluid clearance in CSVD. Our objectives were threefold: (1) to investigate the changes of CP microstructural metrics and perivascular clearance biomarkers in CSVD patients; (2) to examine associations between CP/perivascular metrics and established CSVD markers (e.g., WMH, lacunes, brain atrophy); and (3) to explore how alterations in CP structure and perivascular clearance relate to cognitive performance. By adopting this multimodal approach, we sought to provide a holistic view of the CP-glymphatic axis in CSVD. Methods Study participants This study was approved by the institutional review board of our hospital (No. 2023 − 149) and adhered to the Helsinki Declaration. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant. A total of 211 individuals were screened from February 2023 to March 2025 and 72 were excluded because of imaging criteria not met, poor MRI quality or missing clinical data (see Supplementary Fig. S1), leaving 139 patients with CSVD for analysis. CSVD was diagnosed based on established neuroimaging criteria (STRIVE-2) [ 3 ], including the presence of lacunes, CMBs, WMH (Fazekas score ≥ 2) and enlarged PVS (numbering ≥ 10). The exclusion criteria were: (1) age &lt; 50 years; (2) any other imaging evidence of preexisting structural brain lesion (e.g., hemorrhage, cerebral trauma or tumors); (3) cerebral aneurysm or ≥ 50% stenosis of an intracranial or extracranial artery on MR angiography ; (4) history of large territorial stroke (infarction core &gt; 2 cm) or other neurologic/psychiatric disorders; (5) contraindications to MRI examination; (6) severe MRI artifacts, incomplete multimodal MRI data or neuropsychological scales. Healthy controls (HCs) were recruited from the community during the same period. Their exclusion criteria included: (1) age &lt; 45; (2) history of stroke, traumatic brain injury, neurological or psychiatric disease, cerebrovascular malformation, or systemic diseases that could severely affect the brain; (3) presence of any lacune, microbleed, or confluent WMH on MRI. Clinical assessments A cumulative vascular risk score (VRS) was calculated as the sum of four vascular risk factors (0–4): hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, diabetes, and ever smoking [ 25 ]. All participants underwent comprehensive cognitive testing. The Digit Span Test (DST) assessed working memory and attention; the Animal Verbal Fluency Test assessed verbal fluency and executive function; the Digit Symbol Substitution Test (DSST) assessed processing speed. The Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) was used to evaluate global cognition. MRI protocols MRI was performed on a 3.0 T scanner (Signa Premier, GE Healthcare) with a 48-channel head coil. The protocol included 3D T1-weighted magnetization-prepared rapid gradient echo (MPRAGE), a 3D multi-echo gradient echo (GRE) for QSM, multi-shell diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), 3D T2-weighted fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR), and MR image compilation (MAGIC) sequences. Detailed imaging parameters are provided in Supplementary Methods S1. Assessment of CSVD burden All neuroimaging markers of CSVD (WMH, enlarged PVS in the basal ganglia, lacunes, and CMBs) were evaluated by two experienced raters (L.D. and Z.P.) blinded to clinical data, following STRIVE-2 guidelines [ 3 ]. CSVD severity was graded using a modified ordinal scale (range 0–6) [ 26 ]: one point for (a) total Fazekas WMH grade 3–4, (b) presence of any lacune, (c) CMBs numbering 1–4, and (d) enlarged PVS in basal ganglia grade 3–4; and two points for (e) CMBs numbering ≥ 5 and (f) Fazekas WMH grade 5–6. Participants with CSVD were stratified a priori by total CSVD burden into mild and severe groups. Mild CSVD burden (CSVD-m) was defined as total score of 1 point, whereas severe CSVD burden (CSVD-s) as 2–6 points [ 26 , 27 ] (Details of rating reliability are in Supplementary Methods S2.) CP segmentation and volume quantification The CP of the lateral ventricles was segmented on 3D T1-weighted images using a deep learning algorithm (3D U-Net Convolutional Neural Network) [ 28 ]. All automated segmentations were reviewed and manually corrected by a senior radiologist blinded to clinical information. The final CP mask was used for subsequent analyses after coregistration to other image modalities. WMH volume on FLAIR was automatically segmented using the TrUE-Net toolbox [ 29 ] followed by manual correction. The resulting WMH mask was dichotomized into periventricular WMH and deep WMH by applying a 10-mm distance threshold from the ventricular surface [ 30 , 31 ]. After “lesion filling” of T1 images with the WMH mask, cortical, white matter, and thalamic volumes were obtained using FreeSurfer v7.1.1 (recon-all pipeline) and normalized to total intracranial volume (TIV). Brain parenchymal fraction (BPF) was calculated as brain parenchymal volume/TIV. CP volume (CPV) and WMH volumes were expressed as ratios to TIV as well. PVS segmentation The PVS probability map was generated from 3D T1 images using a previously validated deep learning algorithm (3D nnU-Net) [ 32 ]. PVS volume in our study focused on basal ganglia because it was reported to be related to arteriolosclerosis in CSVD [ 22 ]. To validate the effectiveness of PVS volume fraction (PVSVF-BG = PVS volume/ basal ganglia volume), we performed Spearman correlation analysis with the visual rating scores ( r = 0.434, =p &lt; 0.001) . Quantitative susceptibility mapping (QSM) and χ-separation The GRE data were processed using the SEPIA toolbox to generate susceptibility maps [ 33 ], following the recommended steps outlined in the consensus paper [ 34 ]. R2* maps were first calculated from the GRE using the ARLO algorithm, then R2′ maps (representing reversible transverse relaxation) were obtained [ 35 , 36 ]. The R2′ and raw susceptibility maps were input to the APART algorithm to perform susceptibility source separation, yielding paramagnetic/positive (χ pos ), diamagnetic/negative (χ neg ) and total (χ total ) susceptibility maps [ 37 ]. Diffusion MRI analysis Diffusion data were processed using an in-house pipeline (NeuDiLab) based on DIPY ( https://dipy.org ) and AMICO [ 38 ] ( https://github.com/daducci/ MICO/ ). Eight sets of quantitative parametric maps from the MAP model were as follows: non-Gaussianity (NG), axial/radial non-Gaussianity (NG AX , NG RAD ), mean-squared displacement (MSD), q-space-inverse variance (QIV), return-to-origin/axis/plane probability (RTOP/RTAP/RTPP) [ 39 , 40 ] (Detailed diffusion significance of MAP metrics in supplementary Method S3). FW maps were estimated using the bi-tensor free-water elimination model [ 41 ], by fitting a two-compartment model (isotropic tensor, 3.0 × 10 − 3 mm 2 /s) in each voxel. To maintain consistency with MAP-MRI processing and improve fitting stability, FW estimation leveraged all available diffusion shells acquired in this study (b = 0/1000/1800/2500 s/mm²). Subsequently, the mean FW fraction across the bilateral basal ganglia (FW-BG) were calculated. This ROI was pre‑specified based on our prior CSVD work showing more pronounced FW abnormalities in the basal ganglia relative to white matter (FW-WM) [ 42 ], and to align FW-BG with the deep perivascular phenotype emphasized in the current study (e.g., basal ganglia PVSVF). To extract CP-specific diffusion and susceptibility metrics, the T1-weighted image and CP mask were non-linearly coregistered to the diffusion and GRE spaces through b0 and magnitude images by using Advanced Normalization Tools (ANTs, https://github.com/ANTsX/ANTs ). DTI-ALPS calculation The DTI-ALPS index was computed using a semi-automated pipeline with established reliability [ 43 ]. Diffusion data was preprocessed (including denoising, Gibbs artifact removal, eddy-current/motion correction, skull stripping, and tensor fitting using low-b data only) by using MRtrix 3.0 software in FMRIB Software Library (FSL, https://fsl.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/ ). Color-coded fractional anisotropy (FA) and mean diffusivity (MD) maps of each subject were generated and then co-registered to the JHU-ICBM FA template. Four 5-mm diameter spherical ROIs were automatically placed in the areas of bilateral projection fibers (specifically, the corona radiata) and association fibers (specifically, the superior longitudinal fasciculus) at the level of the lateral ventricular body. To ensure ROIs remained within the intended fiber bundles and to avoid obvious lesion boundaries, the automated ROIs’ placement was visually inspected and manually adjusted if needed by an experienced radiologist who was blinded to the participants’ clinical information. To assess the reliability of this semiautomated ROI placement, intra-rater reliability was evaluated on a randomly selected subset of 20 subjects, yielding an excellent intraclass correlation coefficient of 0.847 (p &lt; 0.001). The diffusivity values for x-, y-, and z-axes of the bilateral projection fibers (Dxx_proj, Dyy_proj) and association fibers (Dxx_assoc, Dzz_assoc) were extracted and the ALPS index was computed using the formula: ALPS index = mean (Dxx_proj, Dxx_assoc)/mean (Dyy_proj, Dzz_assoc). Finally, the DTI-ALPS index was defined as the average of the bilateral ALPS indexes. To reduce potential confounding from white matter microstructure, we extracted FA and MD values within ALPS ROIs for covariates correction [ 44 , 45 ]. Statistical analysis All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS software (version 25.0) or R (version 4.5.1) with significance at p &lt; 0.05 (two tailed). The Shapiro-Wilk test was applied to evaluate the normality of data distribution. Group differences of demographic and clinical variables were tested using χ 2 tests, 2-sample t tests, or Mann-Whitney U tests, as appropriate. A generalized linear mixed model with age, sex and VRS adjusted was performed to evaluate group differences in MRI metrics. For DTI-ALPS index, FA and MD were set as additional covariates. Bonferroni correction addressed multiple comparisons of these imaging outcomes. Within each group, Pearson or Spearman partial correlation was conducted to test associations between CP and perivascular clearance markers, as well as their relationships with brain atrophy and injury, adjusting for age, sex and VRS. Multivariable linear regression analyzed the impact of CP and perivascular clearance metrics on cognitive function, controlling for age, sex, VRS, and education. The mediation analysis was performed using R “mediation” package to explore statistical mediation patterns of perivascular clearance metrics in the relationship between CP measures and CSVD-related structural outcomes, with age, sex and VRS adjusted. Indirect effects were estimated as the average causal mediation effect (ACME), with statistical uncertainty quantified using bias-corrected 95% bootstrap confidence intervals based on 5,000 bootstrap replicates. Because the data are cross-sectional, mediation results were interpreted as association-based and hypothesis-generating rather than evidence of temporal or mechanistic causality. To control for multiple comparisons, the false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure. A random forest classifier (10,000 trees) was used to identify predictors of CSVD severity. Model evaluation followed a repeated nested splitting strategy. Specifically, the CSVD cohort was repeatedly (100 repetitions) stratified into 70% training and 30% testing sets. Within each repetition, Boruta feature selection (2,000 iterations) was performed using the training set only, and the random forest model was fitted on the selected features within the same training set. The held-out test set was used exclusively for performance evaluation. Model performance (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve [AUC] and accuracy) was reported as mean with 95% confidence intervals across repetitions. Furthermore, model calibration was assessed using the average Brier score. Features selected in ≥ 90% of repetitions were considered robust predictors of CSVD severity. Random forest analysis Random forest modeling with Boruta feature selection algorithm revealed that PVSVF-BG, FW-BG, and RTAP of CP (selected in ≥ 90% of iterations, Fig. 3 ) were the most robust predictors of CSVD severity. The model demonstrated good discriminative performance on the unseen testing sets, with an average AUC of 0.755 ( 95% CI: 0.641–0.836 ) and an average accuracy of 0.705 ( 95% CI: 0.585–0.805 ). Discussion Our study identified microstructural alterations in the CP alongside evidence of impaired perivascular fluid transport in patients with CSVD. Specifically, we observed changes in CP microstructure (MAP and susceptibility metrics), elevated FW-BG, PVSVF-BG, and reduced DTI-ALPS index in CSVD patients compared to controls. Notably, these abnormalities were detectable even when CP volume was not yet enlarged and correlated with conventional CSVD markers (WMH, lacunes, CMBs, and brain atrophy). Random-forest modeling also identified PVSVF-BG, FW-BG, and CP RTAP as the most stable predictors of severe CSVD. Importantly, perivascular clearance markers statistically mediated much of the relationships between CP changes and structural brain injury, revealing a potential link between disrupted CSF dynamics and degeneration in CSVD. Moreover, PVSVF-BG emerged as a key mediator linking CP volume and susceptibility changes to cognitive aging. CP microstructural alterations in CSVD Age‑related diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease (AD) feature CP epithelial atrophy, stromal fibrosis and vascular wall thickening [ 8 , 46 ]. Our study similarly detected reduced RTAP/RTOP and increased CP-FW in patients with mild CSVD, even when CP volume remained normal, indicating that microstructural alterations can be detectable in the absence of overt volumetric enlargement. The MAP-derived diffusion metrics, which capture higher‑order diffusion properties beyond conventional DTI [ 18 , 39 ], may therefore be more sensitive to early CP pathology. These findings parallel reports of elevated CP-FW in Aβ‑positive individuals without CP enlargement [ 31 ] and highlight CP microstructure as a potential biomarker of CSVD. The CP consists of a tight epithelial layer separated from fenestrated capillaries by a cellular stroma [ 7 ]. A decrease in RTOP coupled with increased CP-FW in our patients suggests disruption of CP’s epithelial barrier and vasogenic edema, consistent with pathological reports of CP epithelial degeneration, basement membrane thickening, and fluid leakage in aging and dementia [ 46 , 47 ]. Elevated NG RAD in CP further implies greater microstructural heterogeneity, which may reflect inflammatory cell infiltration and plasma protein extravasation. Such immune cell accumulation in the CP has been documented in diseases like multiple sclerosis (MS) [ 48 ] and is relevant in CSVD as well [ 1 ]. Additionally, reduced RTAP is consistent with compromised microvascular patency within the CP due to edema and inflammation [ 39 ]. Collectively, these observations are consistent with a hypothesis in which CP barrier failure may relate to edema, inflammation, and structural disorganization of the CP. Longitudinal and histopathological studies are needed to verify this proposed conjecture in CSVD. CP alterations and perivascular-related proxies in relation to CSVD imaging markers Consistent with prior studies linking CP enlargement to greater WMH burden in CSVD, AD, and MS [ 10 , 12 , 13 ], our findings further illuminate the relationship between CP alterations and brain injury in CSVD. We found that beyond volumetric enlargement, CP microstructural metrics (e.g., MAP-derived diffusion markers) showed even broader associations with CSVD severity, including WMH volume, lacunes, and CMBs. This suggests that CP integrity is intricately linked to the core small vessel pathology and diverse CSVD lesion types. The CP regulates CSF secretion, composition, and flow patterns to maintain brain homeostasis [ 5 ]. Therefore, CP dysfunction may reduce CSF turnover [ 8 ], which is associated with impaired perivascular clearance of waste and neurotoxic accumulation [ 49 , 50 ], and correlated with brain injury and degeneration. In line with this hypothesis, we found that CP abnormalities were significantly correlated with markers of impaired perivascular fluid transport (higher FW-BG, PVSVF-BG, lower DTI-ALPS), and that these markers were crucial mediators between CP changes and brain atrophy and lesions. These observations underscore that a dysfunctional CP, impaired CSF clearance/glymphatic pathways, and progressive brain injury are tightly interconnected in CSVD. Recent longitudinal studies have also demonstrated altered perivascular clearance markers related to CSVD progression, including increasing WMH volumes and lacune numbers [ 22 ], which supported our observation that clearance dysfunction were correlated to CSVD severity. Additionally, DTI-ALPS index was reported to mediate the relationship between CP and WMH volume in diverse contexts including CSVD, MS, and AD [ 12 , 31 , 50 ]. By integrating three complementary perivascular biomarkers with CP microstructural measures, our study strengthens the evidence that impaired CSF clearance is a critical link between CP pathology and widespread brain injury in CSVD, positioning the CP as a potential contributor. Pathological alterations in the CP—such as basement membrane thickening, fibroblast and immune cell infiltration, and accumulation of lipids and protein aggregates—may impair CSF secretion and turnover [ 46 ]. Given that CSF-driven clearance removes up to ~ 70% of interstitial solutes and proteins from the brain, its dysfunction could lead to reduced elimination of neurotoxic and pro-inflammatory factors, blood–brain barrier disruption, increased interstitial fluid stasis, and damage to perivascular astroglial endfeet (loss of AQP4 polarization) [ 51 , 52 ]. This is consistent with a model in which such a chronic, deleterious microenvironment promotes the development of focal white matter lesions and irreversible tissue loss [ 49 ]. While this hypothesis could potentially explain analogous findings in other neurodegenerative diseases, direct confirmation in longitudinal human studies and mechanistic animal models is essential. Perivascular clearance markers linking CP alterations to cognitive performance Previous studies have suggested that glymphatic/perivascular clearance failure is a final common pathway to dementia [ 13 ]. Studies have shown that the DTI-ALPS index, which correlates with glymphatic clearance, is associated with cognitive performance, particularly in executive functions and attention [ 53 ]. Hong et al. reported both DTI-ALPS and CP volume correlate with domain-specific cognition [ 13 ]. In our CSVD cohort, however, only FW-BG showed a direct association with processing speed (DSST) after multiple comparisons correction, which unveils the cumulative effects of interstitial fluid retention and tissue damage may be a more robust biomarker of information processing efficiency than CP in CSVD. We furtherly revealed PVSVF-BG, an indicator of PVS burden (CSF influx), mediated the effect of CPV and susceptibility on cognitive outcomes. Like our results, DTI-ALPS was shown to mediate the relationship between CP volume and global cognition or processing speed in patients with WMH and in AD [ 12 , 54 ], supporting the idea that perivascular clearance is a critical bridge linking CP pathology and cognition. It is worth noting that different perivascular clearance metrics (e.g., PVSVF vs. DTI-ALPS) may capture distinct aspects of fluid transport, and their relative importance could vary with disease context and stage. CP susceptibility alterations and implications for iron homeostasis Another interesting finding from our mediation analysis is that CP susceptibility alterations were more strongly tethered to cognitive status than CP diffusivity changes. In our exploratory group analyses, CSVD patients showed reduced CP paramagnetic susceptibility on χ-separation mapping, which may reflect a loss of paramagnetic content (such as iron) rather than calcific deposition—an insight not attainable with conventional QSM. Taken together, we infer dysregulation of the CP’s iron homeostasis (decreased paramagnetic substances in CP), may lead to toxic metal leakage into the CSF and secondary deposition within the brain parenchyma, exerting greater effects on cognitive function than the microstructural injury captured by diffusion metrics. Previous studies have linked brain iron imbalance to neurodegeneration and cognitive aging [ 55 , 56 ], indirectly supporting this interpretation and underscoring the biological importance of iron metabolism in CP-related cognitive effects. Limitations and future directions Our study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional, single-center design and modest sample size limit temporal and causal inferences between CP changes, impaired CSF clearance, and brain injury. Consequently, our findings are framed as statistical mediation rather than mechanistic evidence. These analyses also rely on assumptions that cannot be fully verified in observational cross-sectional data, including no residual/unmeasured confounding of the exposure-mediator and mediator-outcome paths, and the adequacy of model specification (e.g., linearity and omission of relevant interactions/nonlinearities). Future longitudinal studies are essential to validate these potential pathways and clarify the temporal dynamics of these pathological processes. Second, although we incorporated three imaging biomarkers to reflect perivascular CSF dynamics [ 22 ], these metrics remain indirect proxies and may reflect mixed pathology beyond fluid clearance impairment [ 23 , 43 ]. Specifically, FW is often interpreted as increased extracellular/interstitial water content, yet it can also be influenced by BBB disruption, neuroinflammation, and vasogenic edema. Because FW was estimated using multi-shell data including higher b-values, the Gaussian assumption of the DTI-based bi-tensor model may not be fully satisfied; thus, absolute FW quantification and biological specificity should be interpreted with caution. Future studies using restricted b-value FW fitting, models better suited for high-b regimes, and more direct tracer-based validation are warranted. PVS volume largely capture the prominence/visibility of PVS and can be affected by vascular remodeling, local atrophy, and segmentation performance, while DTI-ALPS is derived from diffusion properties within predefined ROIs and may be influenced by white matter microstructural alterations and ROI placement rather than directly measuring CSF flux or solute clearance. In addition, although FW-WM is commonly used in CSVD studies, it was not included here as a complementary regional analysis; therefore, the regional generalizability of FW findings warrants further investigation in future work. Because these MRI markers cannot fully capture the complexity of brain-wide CSF clearance, future studies incorporating intrathecal or intravenous gadolinium administration MRI techniques are warranted for glymphatic/perivascular clearance validation. Third, vascular/metabolic covariates beyond the four common risk factors were not systematically collected (e.g., atrial fibrillation, renal function, and medication data), limiting the developments of sensitivity models with expanded covariate sets and leaving the possibility of residual confounding. Finally, although our multiparametric MRI protocol revealed significant CP microstructural alterations, the underlying histopathological correlations remain speculative. Direct confirmation via histological examination in animal models or post-mortem tissue will be crucial. In conclusion, CP microstructural abnormalities and impaired perivascular fluid transport are present even in CSVD patients with mild burden. CP alterations link to CSVD imaging markers and cognitive performance, partially through disrupted CSF clearance pathways. These findings implicate the CP-perivascular fluid axis as an important player in CSVD pathogenesis and a potential target for therapeutic intervention.
