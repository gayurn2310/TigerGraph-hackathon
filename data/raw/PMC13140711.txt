Associations between sleep disturbance and cerebrospinal fluid Aβ, and shared proteomic signatures in Alzheimer's disease

Abstract INTRODUCTION Sleep disturbance is increasingly recognized as a risk factor for Alzheimer's disease (AD), but longitudinal clinical evidence linking sleep behaviors to cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) amyloid beta (Aβ) dynamics and related proteomic signatures remains limited. METHODS We analyzed 1205 participants from the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) to examine associations between sleep disturbance, CSF Aβ42 level, and cognition. To prioritize molecular candidates associated with these phenotypes, we integrated CSF proteomics with weighted gene co‐expression network analysis (WGCNA) and two‐sample Mendelian randomization (MR). RESULTS Baseline sleep disturbance was associated with a faster rate of longitudinal CSF Aβ42 decline. WGCNA identified a protein module associated with sleep disturbance and Aβ42, within which MR supported effects of sleep disturbance on multiple proteins. Among these, interferon γ‐inducible protein 16 is a candidate link between sleep disturbance and Aβ pathology. DISCUSSION Our findings suggest that sleep disturbance accelerates Aβ pathology, and implicate IFI16‐related pathway as a link between disrupted sleep and AD progression.

1 BACKGROUND Alzheimer's disease (AD) is the most common neurodegenerative disorder and the leading cause of dementia. 1 Its pathological cascade begins decades before symptom onset and is characterized by accumulating amyloid beta (Aβ) plaques and neurofibrillary tau tangles. 2 , 3 Reductions in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) Aβ42 represent a core biomarker of the preclinical phase and provide a sensitive indicator of early Aβ pathology. 4 Identifying modifiable factors that influence Aβ dynamics during the prodromal stage is therefore essential for delaying or preventing clinical progression. 5 Among the modifiable risk factors linked to incident dementia and AD, sleep has emerged as a promising target. 6 Sleep disturbances often appear before cognitive decline, and &gt; 60% of individuals with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) or AD experience at least one form of sleep disturbance. 7 Furthermore, in cognitively normal (CN) adults, unhealthy sleep behaviors such as abnormal sleep duration, insomnia, snoring, late chronotype, and excessive daytime sleepiness have been associated with elevated risk of incident dementia. 8 , 9 Moreover, with the progression of AD, sleep disturbances intensify further and significantly exacerbate the deterioration of cognitive function. 6 These observations support a bidirectional relationship between sleep regulation and early AD‐related biological changes. Accumulating evidence indicates that sleep disruption influences multiple AD‐related processes including Aβ deposition, tau propagation, synaptic activity, and neuroimmune reaction. 10 , 11 , 12 Extracellular concentrations of Aβ and tau fluctuate across the sleep and wake cycle. 11 , 13 Acute sleep deprivation increases tau levels in mouse interstitial fluid and human CSF, and chronic sleep disruption accelerates the spread of tau aggregates in neural circuits. 14 In addition, disturbed sleep is associated with impaired Aβ clearance, increased wake‐related Aβ production, and early Aβ deposition on positron emission tomography (PET) imaging in CN adults. 15 , 16 Our prior work demonstrated that chronic sleep deprivation exacerbates Aβ precursor protein dysregulation and cognitive deficits in AD transgenic APPswe/PS1E9 mice. 17 , 18 Despite these findings, several important gaps in knowledge still need further investigation. Many previous studies are cross‐sectional and rely on single time point assessments of sleep behavior. Evidence regarding the association between sleep disturbance and soluble Aβ in CSF remains limited, particularly in large longitudinal cohorts. Given that the sleep‐–tau relationship has been investigated in prior work, 14 , 19 , 20 the current study focuses on sleep‐related changes that may influence early Aβ dynamics. Specifically, we examined CSF Aβ42 as it serves as a more sensitive index of these pathological processes. Furthermore, the molecular candidates connecting sleep disturbance to early Aβ pathology, particularly at the protein network level, remain incompletely understood. High‐dimensional proteomic profiling offers a complementary approach to understanding sleep‐related molecular dysregulation. 21 The SomaScan 7K platform measures thousands of proteins across diverse biological pathways including synaptic signaling, inflammation, extracellular matrix remodeling, and metabolic regulation. 22 Leveraging these proteomic signatures may help identify upstream processes that mediate the relationship between sleep abnormalities and early pathological changes of AD. 23 , 24 In this study, we investigated cross‐sectional and longitudinal associations among self‐reported sleep disturbance, CSF Aβ42, and cognitive decline in CN and impaired individuals from the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI). We further integrated CSF proteomics with bioinformatics analyses to characterize protein networks associated with sleep disruption and the interaction between sleep and CSF Aβ42. By combining longitudinal sleep data, quantitative Aβ42 in CSF, and large‐scale proteomic profiling, this work aims to clarify early molecular candidates through which sleep disturbance may influence Aβ pathology and to identify potential pathways that may serve as targets for early intervention. RESEARCH IN CONTEXT Systematic review : Literature searches have revealed that sleep disturbance is associated with amyloid beta (Aβ) accumulation, tau pathology, and cognitive impairment in Alzheimer's disease (AD). However, most clinical evidence is cross‐sectional, and longitudinal data linking sleep disturbance to cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) Aβ dynamics and related proteomic signatures remain limited. Interpretation : Our findings demonstrate that sleep disturbance is associated with lower CSF Aβ42 level in cross‐sectional analyses and with accelerated CSF Aβ42 decline in longitudinal analyses. The associations between sleep disturbance and faster cognitive deterioration were partially mediated by Aβ pathology and were further linked to a distinct proteomic module. Within this module, interferon‐inducible myeloid differentiation transcriptional activator emerged as a key protein candidate. It was prioritized by Mendelian randomization as being associated with genetically predicted insomnia and served as a statistical mediator linking sleep disruption to Aβ42 pathology. Future directions : Our findings provide insight into how disrupted sleep may accelerate AD pathogenesis and suggest that targeting sleep‐related neuroimmune signaling could represent a promising strategy for early intervention in AD. Future studies with objective sleep measures and experimental validation are warranted to clarify causal mechanisms and evaluate this potential strategy. 2 METHODS 2.1 Study design and participants The study design is schematically presented in Figure 1 . Clinical information included the Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE), Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR), 13‐item Alzheimer's Disease Assessment Scale (ADAS13), and Neuropsychiatric Inventory (NPI) scores. Cognitive status was assigned according to established ADNI criteria: CN individuals had MMSE scores &gt; 24 and a CDR of 0, whereas cognitively impaired participants (MCI or dementia) had MMSE scores of ≥ 24 and a CDR of at least 0.5 with relatively preserved daily functioning. Apolipoprotein E ( APOE ) genotyping was performed using peripheral blood samples. All procedures were approved by institutional review boards at participating ADNI sites, and written informed consent was obtained from each participant or their legal representative in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. 2.2 Sleep assessment Sleep‐related symptoms were evaluated at baseline and during follow‐up visits using the sleep domain of the NPI (NPIK). 25 This instrument, completed by participants or their informants during structured interviews, was selected to ensure reliable characterization of nighttime behaviors while maintaining a feasible assessment burden. After a brief screening procedure to identify the presence of sleep disturbances, informants were asked whether the participant had exhibited any of the following behaviors within the preceding month: (1) Does the patient have difficulty falling asleep?; (2) Does the patient get up during the night? (If the patient only wakes up once or twice to use the bathroom and quickly falls back asleep, this is not considered a disturbance.); (3) Does the patient walk around, pace, or engage in other inappropriate activities at night?; (4) Does the patient wake you during the night?; (5) Does the patient wake up, get dressed, and prepare to go out, thinking it is morning and time to start the day?; (6) Does the patient wake up too early in the morning (earlier than their usual time)?; (7) Does the patient nap excessively during the day?; (8) Are there other disruptive nighttime behaviors not yet discussed? Individuals who endorsed no symptoms received a score of 0, whereas the presence of any disturbance resulted in a score of 1. For those reporting disturbances, interviewers further quantified symptom frequency (NPIK‐Frequency) and severity (NPIK‐Severity). Frequency was rated as: 0 = none; 1 = occasional (≤ once per week); 2 = frequent (approximately once per week); 3 = very frequent (several times per week but not nightly); 4 = extremely frequent (nightly or multiple times per night). Severity was rated as: 0 = none; 1 = mild (symptoms present but not meaningfully disruptive); 2 = moderate (disturbs the sleep of both patient and caregiver); 3 = severe (multiple disruptive behaviors with substantial distress or sleep interruption for the caregiver). For participants exhibiting more than one type of disturbance, ratings were based on the symptom judged most impactful. The composite sleep disturbance score (NPIK‐Total) was calculated as the product of frequency and severity. To characterize specific sleep disturbance phenotypes, symptoms were categorized into three subtypes based on the pattern of questionnaire responses: (1) Insomnia‐predominant subtype (NPIK‐Insomnia): derived from items 1, 2, 4, and 6 (score range: 0–4); (2) Circadian rhythm–predominant subtype (NPIK‐Rhythm): derived from items 5 and 7 (score range: 0–2); (3) Nocturnal behavior–predominant subtype (NPIK‐Nocturnal behavior): derived from items 3 and 8 (score range: 0–2). These subtype scores reflect the extent to which each domain contributed to the overall sleep disturbance profile. To evaluate and control for the potential confounding effects of co‐occurring neuropsychiatric symptoms, we assessed four specific non‐sleep domains from the NPI: depression/dysphoria (NPI‐Depression), anxiety (NPI‐Anxiety), apathy/indifference (NPI‐Apathy), and agitation/aggression (NPI‐Aggression). Consistent with the sleep domain assessment, data were obtained through structured caregiver interviews. 2.3 CSF Aβ42 assessment CSF Aβ42 measurements were obtained from the standardized biomarker datasets provided by the ADNI. All samples across study phases (ADNI‐1, GO/2, and ADNI‐3) were analyzed or re‐analyzed by the ADNI Biomarker Core using the Roche Elecsys electrochemiluminescence immunoassay, ensuring methodological consistency across batches. CSF samples were processed at the University of Pennsylvania/ADNI Biomarker Laboratory following the manufacturer's protocol. Although the Elecsys Aβ42 assay has a validated reportable range of 200 to 1700 pg/mL, values exceeding the upper limit of quantification (&gt; 1700 pg/mL) were not censored but retained using exploratory values derived from extrapolation of the calibration curve. This approach follows the ADNI Biomarker Core recommendations to preserve the continuity of Aβ42 measurements for longitudinal analyses. To ensure longitudinal consistency across manufacturing lots, the Biomarker Core used a standardized “lot rollover” procedure involving extensive test–retest validation. Reported cross‐lot linear regression analyses demonstrated high consistency ( R 2 = 0.93–0.99), supporting the direct combination of data across ADNI phases without additional rescaling ( https://adni.loni.usc.edu/upenn‐csf‐biomarkers‐roche‐elecsysadn1go2‐data‐available/ ). The present study used results from the consolidated UPENN Elecsys batches (BATCH 9–13), covering measurements released between 2017 and 2022. All CSF collection, processing, and storage procedures across ADNI sites followed a harmonized protocol ( https://adni‐lde.loni.usc.edu/quick‐start‐guide‐asset/bioflood.html ). 2.4 Statistical analyses Normality of continuous variables was evaluated with the Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous variables are summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while categorical variables are presented as frequency and percentage ( n [%]). Group differences between CN and cognitively impaired participants at baseline were assessed using independent two‐sample t tests for continuous variables and chi‑squared tests for categorical variables. Cross‑sectional associations between baseline sleep quality, CSF Aβ42 levels, and cognitive performance were examined using multiple linear regression. Longitudinal slopes assessing the rates of change in CSF Aβ42 and cognitive performance over time were all calculated separately using linear mixed‐effects models. Models included a fixed effect of time and random effects for participant slope and intercept. CSF Aβ42 concentrations were log 10 ‑transformed prior to analysis, and regression coefficients (β) are reported as log 10 fold‑change per unit increase in the predictor. All models were adjusted for age, sex, race, education, and APOE ε4 carrier status. To account for multiple comparisons across proteins, P values were corrected using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure. Proteins with FDR‑adjusted P values &lt; 0.05 were considered differentially abundant. Data analyses were performed using R packages including data.table version 1.17.8, dplyr version 1.1.4, lme4 version 1.1‐38, lmerTest version 3.1‐3, and car version 3.1‐3, in R version 4.5.2. 2.5 CSF proteomics CSF proteome data accessed through the ADNI database was generated by the Neurogenomics and Informatics Center at Washington University ( https://neurogenomics.wustl.edu/ ) and the Cruchaga Lab at Washington University School of Medicine ( https://cruch agalab.wustl.edu/). CSF proteome samples were analyzed using the SomaScan 7K assay. The SomaScan 7K platform was used to quantify CSF proteins in the ADNI cohort. Participants with available SomaScan 7K proteomic data, sleep assessments, and CSF Aβ42 measurements were included. CSF proteomic profiling was conducted using the SomaScan v4.1 assay (SomaLogic Inc.), which measures 7596 human protein analytes via slow off‐rate modified aptamers. All CSF samples were stored at −80°C and processed according to standardized pre‐analytical protocols. Thawing, aliquoting, and plate loading were performed under controlled temperature conditions, and sample placement across assay plates was fully randomized. Statistical evaluations confirmed balanced demographic and clinical variables across plates, with no detectable plate‐related batch effects. Raw relative fluorescence unit intensities underwent SomaLogic's standard multi‐step normalization pipeline. Hybridization normalization was applied at the sample level, followed by signal‐based stratification of aptamers, median normalization to correct technical variation, and adaptive normalization by maximum likelihood. Quality control filters were applied uniformly across cohorts. Aptamers with extreme scale factors or high cross‐plate variability were removed. Log 10 ‐transformed values outside 1.5 × interquartile range (IQR) were set to missing, and analytes or samples with call rates &lt; 65% were excluded, followed by a stricter 85% call‐rate threshold. Non‐human targets and aptamers lacking defined protein assignments were removed. The ADNI CSF proteomic dataset used in this study consisted of the normalized relative fluorescence unit values after applying the above quality control and normalization procedures. After quality control and normalization, proteins with excessive missingness were further excluded prior to downstream analyses. Specifically, analytes with missing values in &gt; 20% of samples were removed to ensure robustness of network‐based analyses ( https://adni.loni.usc.edu/cruchaga‐lab‐csf‐somascan7k‐protein‐data‐now‐available/ ). The remaining proteomic matrix exhibited minimal missingness. For analyses requiring a complete data matrix, residual missing values were imputed using k‐nearest neighbor imputation. 2.6 Protein network analysis Weighted gene co‐expression network analysis (WGCNA) was generated using the WGCNA package in R (1.73) as previously published. 26 Pairwise correlations among all quantified proteins were computed from the preprocessed SomaScan v4.1 dataset. To satisfy the assumption of scale‐free topology, we evaluated a range of soft‐thresholding powers (β) from 1 to 20 using the pick Soft Threshold function. We selected a power of β = 4, which was the lowest power where the scale‐free topology fit index ( R 2 ) exceeded 0.90. Using this power (β = 4), the correlation matrix was converted into a signed adjacency matrix. This was then transformed into a topological overlap matrix (TOM) to capture shared connectivity patterns, and hierarchical clustering of TOM‐based dissimilarity was used to generate the protein dendrogram. Modules of co‐expressed proteins were defined with the dynamic tree‐cutting algorithm (deepSplit = 2, minimum module size = 30, mergeCutHeight = 0.25, PAM refinement enabled). Module eigengenes (MEs) were obtained as the first principal component of each module. Proteins were reassigned to the module for which they exhibited the strongest membership, using a reassignment threshold of p &lt; 0.05. Furthermore, associations between MEs and clinical variables (NPIK scores and CSF Aβ42) were quantified using Spearman rank correlation. Multiple comparisons were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure, and modules meeting an FDR &lt; 0.05 were considered significantly associated with the traits. Given the exploratory nature of WGCNA, covariate adjustment was not applied at this stage; however, key associations were further evaluated in covariate‐adjusted models in downstream analyses. 2.7 Functional and cell‐type enrichment analysis of the proteomic module To characterize the biological functions and signaling pathways associated with the sleep disturbance–related proteomic profile, we performed functional enrichment analyses on the 182 proteins comprised in the MEblack module identified by WGCNA. Gene Ontology (GO) analysis, including biological process (BP), cellular component, and molecular function, along with Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis, were conducted using the clusterProfiler package in R. To determine the cellular origin and cell‐type specificity of the proteins within the MEblack module, we used a high‐resolution single‐cell transcriptome atlas of the human brain vasculature and parenchyma. 27 The proteins in the MEblack module were mapped to their corresponding gene symbols. Cell type enrichment analysis was performed using the Expression Weighted Cell‐type Enrichment (EWCE) package in R. Unlike standard overlap methods, EWCE evaluates whether the module proteins have significantly higher cell‐type specificity compared to a background gene set, independent of fixed marker gene thresholds. Statistical significance was assessed using a bootstrap enrichment test with 10,000 permutations. P values were corrected for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. 2.8 Mendelian randomization analysis We performed a two‐sample Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis to investigate the potential associations of sleep disorder on protein levels in human biofluids. Genetic instruments for sleep disorder were obtained from a large‐scale genome‐wide association study (GWAS) meta‐analysis of 1,331,010 individuals of European ancestry. 28 We selected 81 independent single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) as instrumental variables based on a genome‐wide significance threshold of P &lt; 5 /times 10 −8 and a strict clumping process ( r 2 &lt; 0.001 within a 10,000 kb window) to ensure independence. Outcome summary statistics for protein levels were extracted from the deCODE genetics study, 29 with datasets harmonized to align effect alleles. The inverse‐variance weighted (IVW) method was used as the primary analytical approach to investigate the potential links between genetically predicted sleep disorder and protein level. To ensure the robustness of our findings, we further conducted sensitivity analyses using the weighted median method, the MR‐Egger intercept test to detect horizontal pleiotropy, and the Cochran Q statistic to assess heterogeneity, with all analyses performed using the TwoSampleMR package in R. 2.9 Mediation analysis We conducted mediation analyses using the “mediation” package in R. First, we examined whether CSF Aβ42 mediates the relationship between sleep disturbance (NPIK scores) and cognitive function (CDR Sum of Boxes [CDRSB] and ADAS13 scores). Second, we investigated whether specific CSF proteins mediate the effect of sleep disturbance on CSF Aβ42 levels. For each model, the analysis decomposed the total effect of the exposure on the outcome into two components: average indirect mediation effect (ACME), the portion of the association accounted for by the candidate link and average direct effect (ADE), the effect of the exposure on the outcome that is not mediated by the specified variable. The statistical significance of the indirect effects was assessed using a non‐parametric bootstrap approach with 5000 simulations. An effect was considered significant if the 95% confidence interval did not include zero. All models were adjusted for potential confounders, including age, sex, education level, race, and APOE carrier status. An FDR‐adjusted p value &lt; 0.05 was considered statistically significant. 2.10 Protein–protein interaction network and annotation To elucidate the molecular interactome and specific biological roles of the key candidate protein, we used the STRING database (Version 12.0; https://string‐db.org/ ). Only interactions with a combined confidence score ≥ 0.4 (medium confidence) were retained. The resulting protein–protein interaction (PPI) network was visualized using the STRING web interface. Based on the identified PPI network nodes, a targeted GO enrichment analysis was performed to determine the specific BP driven by the key protein interactome. 3 RESULTS 3.1 Demographics and characteristics of participants A total of 1205 participants from the ADNI dataset were included in this study, comprising 481 CN individuals and 724 participants with cognitive impairment (CI; Table 1 ). There was no significant difference in baseline age distribution between the CN and CI groups ( p = 0.334). In contrast, sex distribution differed significantly, with a higher proportion of males in the CI group ( p &lt; 0.001). Participants in the CI group also had fewer years of formal education compared to CN individuals ( p ＜ 0.001), and racial composition differed modestly between groups ( p = 0.013). The frequency of APOE ε4 carriers was significantly higher in the CI group than in the CN group ( p &lt; 0.001). Participants with CI exhibited worse cognitive and neuropsychiatric profiles, including lower MMSE scores and higher CDRSB, ADAS13, and total NPI scores ( p &lt; 0.001). Baseline CSF Aβ42 concentrations were significantly lower in the CI group compared to the CN group ( p &lt; 0.001). Regarding longitudinal follow‐up, 238 CN participants and 242 CI participants had available follow‐up data. The median (IQR) of the follow‐up duration was 2.42 (2.03, 4.52) years in the CN group and 2.04 (2.00, 4.01) years in the CI group. The median number of follow‐up visits was 2 (2, 3) for both groups (Table 1 ). 3.2 Cross‐sectional associations between sleep disturbance and CSF Aβ42 level Cross‐sectional analyses examined whether sleep disturbance was associated with CSF Aβ42 at baseline. In linear regression models adjusted for age, sex, race, education, APOE ε4 status, NPI‐Depression, NPI‐Anxiety, NPI‐Apathy, and NPI‐Aggression, higher overall sleep disturbance as defined by the NPIK‐Total score was significantly related to lower Aβ42 concentrations (β = −0.033, p &lt; 0.0001; Figure 2A ). Similarly, the severity of sleep disturbance (NPIK‐Severity scores) was inversely associated with Aβ42 concentrations (β = −0.279, p &lt; 0.0001; Figure 2C ). In contrast, the frequency of sleep disturbance (NPIK‐Frequency) showed no statistically significant relationship with baseline Aβ42 (β = −0.058, p = 0.104; Figure 2B ). Subtype analyses further indicated that insomnia (NPIK‐Insomnia, β = −0.034, p &lt; 0.05; Figure 2D ), circadian rhythm disorder (NPIK‐Rhythm disorder, β = −0.138, p &lt; 0.01; Figure 2E ), and nocturnal abnormal behaviors (NPIK‐Nocturnal behavior, β = −0.102, p &lt; 0.05; Figure 2F ) were significantly associated with lower CSF Aβ42 at baseline. Aβ, amyloid beta; APOE , apolipoprotein E; CSF, cerebrospinal fluid; NPIK, Neuropsychiatric Inventory sleep domain. 3.3 Longitudinal associations between sleep disturbance and CSF Aβ42 decline To determine the association between sleep disturbance and longitudinal changes in Aβ pathology, 480 participants with repeated CSF Aβ42 assessments were included (Table 1 and Table S1 in supporting information). Sleep disturbance was defined as exposure, operationalized using NPIK assessments obtained at baseline. Linear mixed‐effects models were fitted with CSF Aβ42 as the repeated outcome variable. Time (in years from baseline) was included as a fixed effect, and participant‐specific random intercepts and slopes were incorporated to account for individual variability in CSF Aβ42 change over time. The results showed that higher scores on the NPIK‐Total (β = 0.06 per year, p &lt; 0.001; Figure 2G ), NPIK‐Frequency (β = 0.044, p &lt; 0.05; Figure 2H ), NPIK‐Severity (β = 0.048, p &lt; 0.01; Figure 2I ), and NPIK‐Nocturnal behavior (β = 0.044, p &lt; 0.05; Figure 2L ) subscales were associated with a significantly greater annual decline in CSF Aβ42 across the follow‐up period. In contrast, scores for NPIK‐Insomnia (β = 0.016, Figure 2J ) and NPIK‐Rhythm disorder (β = 0.012, Figure 2K ) did not show significant associations with the rate of Aβ42 decline. Taken together, the longitudinal results indicate that the frequency and severity of sleep disturbance, particularly nocturnal behavioral abnormalities, are predictive of more rapid Aβ progression over time. Furthermore, to address the potential issue of reverse causality, we performed a sensitivity analysis restricted to a subgroup of participants who were both Aβ negative and CN at baseline (Table 1 ). To further ensure that the observed associations were not driven by the immediate prodromal phase of cognitive decline, we additionally excluded the individuals who progressed from CN to MCI within the first 2 years of follow‐up. Notably, the association between baseline sleep disturbance and longitudinal CSF Aβ42 levels remained statistically significant even after this exclusion (Std. β = −0.6, p &lt; 0.05, Table S2 in supporting information). These findings indicate that the observed sleep–Aβ association is unlikely to be merely a reflection of reverse causality arising from the prodromal stage of AD. 3.4 Relationships among sleep disturbance, CSF Aβ42 level, and cognitive decline We further evaluated the relationships among sleep disturbance, CSF Aβ42 levels, and cognitive performance. First, we examined both cross‐sectional and longitudinal associations between sleep disturbance and cognitive performance. Cross‐sectional linear regression models, adjusted for age, sex, race, years of education, and APOE ε4 status, showed that higher baseline NPIK‐Total scores were significantly associated with worse performance on both the CDRSB (β = 0.143, p &lt; 0.0001; Figure 3A ) and ADAS13 (β = 0.405, p &lt; 0.01; Figure 3D ). Furthermore, longitudinal analyses using linear mixed‐effects models demonstrated that sleep disturbance also predicted faster cognitive decline over time (Figure 3B, C, E, F ). Higher NPIK‐Total scores were associated with a steeper annual worsening on both CDRSB (β = 0.134 per year, p &lt; 0.0001; Figure 3B ) and ADAS13 (β = 0.076 per year, p &lt; 0.01; Figure 3E ). Comparable longitudinal effects were observed for sleep disturbance severity, as indicated by the NPIK‐Severity scale (CDRSB: β = 0.146 per year, p &lt; 0.05; Figure 3C ). These findings highlight MEblack as a proteomic signature that may be associated with both sleep disturbance and Aβ pathology. Among the 11 proteins identified in previous MR analysis, IFI16, STMN4, and ribosomal protein L30 (RPL30) exhibited significant indirect effects (Table S6 in supporting information). Specifically, the ACME of IFI16 was estimated at 7.90 (95% confidence interval: 2.25–14.8), which remained statistically significant after FDR correction ( p = 0.002, FDR‑adjusted p = 0.026, Figure 6B ). This indicates a significant indirect effect of sleep disturbance on CSF Aβ42 levels through IFI16. Conversely, the ADE of sleep on Aβ42 was negative ( β = −15.88) and marginally significant ( p = 0.068). The opposing directions of the indirect and direct effects resulted in a non‐significant total effect ( p = 0.336). This pattern is consistent with a statistical suppression effect, in which the variance explained by the IFI16 opposes the direct association. RBBP4, which has been identified in previous steps, also demonstrated a significant indirect effect in the unadjusted model, with an ACME of 2.51 (95% confidence interval: 0.12 to 5.70, p = 0.038). However, this significance did not survive FDR correction (FDR‑adjusted p = 0.124). Notably, IFI16 was highlighted in both the MR and the mediation analysis, although we acknowledge that the GWAS‐defined sleep traits and NPIK‐defined sleep disturbance represent distinct constructs. We further conducted PPI analysis using STRING, which revealed the predicted molecular networks of IFI16. The PPI network revealed that IFI16 forms an interaction cluster with several critical proteins involved in innate immunity and DNA damage response (Figure 6C ). Key interactors included core components of the inflammasome complex, such as PYCARD (ASC), CASP1 (caspase‐1), AIM2 (absent in melanoma 2), and NLRC4 (NLR family CARD domain‐containing protein 4; Figure 6C ). Additionally, IFI16 showed strong connectivity with DNA sensing and signaling molecules like cGAS (cyclic GMP‐AMP synthase) and STING1 (stimulator of interferon genes), as well as tumor suppressors tumor protein p53 and breast cancer type 1 susceptibility protein (Figure 6C ). GO enrichment analysis for BP further corroborated the inflammatory relevance (Figure 6D ). The most significantly enriched processes were related to pyroptosis and the positive regulation of interleukin‐1β production (Figure 6C ). Other prominent terms included pattern recognition receptor signaling pathway, defense response to virus, and activation of innate immune response (Figure 6C ). These functional annotations suggest that IFI16 and its interactors are involved in inflammatory processes that might co‐occur with sleep disturbance and AD pathology. 4 DISCUSSION In this study, we systematically investigated the relationships among sleep disturbance severity, amyloid pathology, and cognitive decline by integrating cross‐sectional and longitudinal clinical data, CSF Aβ42 measurements, and large‐scale CSF proteomics in individuals spanning the cognitive continuum. Our findings demonstrate that baseline caregiver‐reported sleep disturbance is associated with lower CSF Aβ42 levels cross‐sectionally, accelerating longitudinal CSF Aβ42 decline. Importantly, the associations between sleep disturbance and faster cognitive deterioration were partially mediated by Aβ pathology and were further linked to a distinct proteomic module. Within this module, the immune regulator IFI16 emerged as a key protein candidate; it was prioritized by MR as being associated with genetically predicted insomnia and accounted for a significant portion of the statistical association between sleep disruption and CSF Aβ42. Accumulating evidence underscores the role of sleep in regulating Aβ production, aggregation, and clearance. Clinical and experimental studies indicate that acute sleep deprivation elevates interstitial Aβ levels. 10 Consistently, PET studies showed that reduced nighttime sleep duration is associated with greater Aβ deposition in cognitively unimpaired older adults. 16 , 30 Furthermore, cross‐sectional investigations have examined the relationship between sleep and CSF Aβ levels, 31 , 32 indicating that both insufficient and excessive nocturnal sleep durations were associated with higher CSF Aβ level, suggesting a potential optimal sleep window between 6.0 and 6.5 hours. 32 Consistent with these clinical observations, we found that greater overall sleep disturbance and higher symptom severity were associated with lower CSF Aβ42 concentrations. Notably, beyond cross‐sectional associations, our longitudinal analyses provide evidence that sleep disturbance is associated with a faster decline in CSF Aβ42 over time. While we observed a higher retention rate of CN participants among participants with longitudinal CSF follow‐up, this selection bias likely leads to more conservative results. Despite this, these associations were observed across both CN and CI individuals and remained robust after adjustment for cognitive status, suggesting that sleep disturbance may influence amyloid pathology early in the disease course, before overt dementia manifests. Furthermore, considering the higher specificity of the CSF Aβ42/40 ratio for reflecting amyloid pathology, we performed an additional sensitivity analysis within the ADNI‐3 subcohort. However, we did not observe a significant association between baseline sleep disturbance and CSF Aβ42/40 ratio levels in this subset. This null finding is likely attributable to limited statistical power, given the significantly smaller sample size and relatively shorter follow‐up duration of the ADNI‐3 cohort compared to our primary analysis. Additionally, while the CSF Aβ42/40 ratio is known to show high concordance with amyloid PET status, 33 prior research suggests that absolute CSF Aβ42 levels may exhibit dynamic changes at very early stages of amyloid accumulation, potentially making Aβ42 a sensitive marker for short‐term longitudinal dynamics in specific contexts. 34 Therefore, while our findings highlight a link between sleep disturbance and CSF Aβ42 dynamics, validation in larger cohorts with comprehensive Aβ40 data is warranted to fully elucidate these relationships. Our subtype analyses further indicated that insomnia symptoms, circadian rhythm disturbances, and nocturnal abnormal behaviors were associated with reduced CSF Aβ42, whereas symptom frequency alone showed a weaker cross‐sectional association. This pattern suggests that the qualitative burden and physiological disruption of sleep may be more relevant to Aβ dysregulation. Circadian rhythm disruption, in particular, may exacerbate Aβ accumulation by desynchronizing neuronal activity and impairing rhythmic regulation of Aβ clearance pathways. Our previous study revealed that circadian rhythm disturbances in AD mice preceded Aβ deposition. 35 , 36 The circadian rhythm disturbances observed in the early AD might be attributed to the abnormal expression of core clock genes in the brain regions involved in circadian rhythm regulation. 35 , 37 Consistent with previous epidemiological and experimental studies, 38 , 39 sleep disturbance in our cohort was associated with worse cognitive performance and faster cognitive decline across multiple domains. Importantly, mediation analyses demonstrated that CSF Aβ42 partially mediated the association between sleep disturbance and cognitive outcomes, including both global clinical severity and cognitive test performance. These results suggest that amyloid pathology constitutes one mechanistic pathway through which sleep disturbance contributes to cognitive deterioration. However, the mediation was partial, indicating that additional pathways independent of amyloid pathology are likely involved. Sleep disturbance has been linked to tau pathology, synaptic dysfunction, vascular injury, and neuroinflammation, all of which may independently contribute to cognitive decline. 40 Our focus on Aβ42 reflects its sensitivity to early pathological changes, but future studies integrating tau, neurodegeneration markers, and functional imaging will be necessary to capture the full spectrum of sleep‐related neurobiological effects. This study further integrated CSF proteomics with clinical and Aβ42 data to identify molecular pathways linking sleep disturbance and Aβ pathology. Using WGCNA, we identified a specific protein co‐expression module, MEblack, that was uniquely associated with both sleep disturbance and CSF Aβ42 levels. Functional annotation of this module revealed enrichment in immune response pathways, mitochondrial metabolism, RNA processing, and telomere‐related functions, suggesting a convergence of inflammatory and metabolic dysregulation. Cell‐type enrichment analyses further indicated that proteins within this module were predominantly of microglial origin, with additional contributions from vascular cells, oligodendrocyte precursor cells, and cerebellar glial populations. These findings are consistent with growing evidence that sleep disruption alters microglial activation states, promotes neuroinflammation, and impairs metabolic homeostasis, thereby facilitating amyloid accumulation and propagation. 41 The involvement of vascular and glial cell types also raises the possibility that sleep disturbance affects blood–brain barrier integrity and neurovascular coupling, further exacerbating amyloid pathology. Among the proteins within the MEblack module, IFI16 emerged as a particularly compelling candidate. MR analyses provided genetic evidence that sleep disturbance influences IFI16 levels, supporting a directional relationship rather than mere association. Furthermore, mediation analyses demonstrated that IFI16 significantly mediated the relationship between sleep disturbance and CSF Aβ42, indicating its functional relevance in this pathway, although these findings are hypothesis generating and require validation in longitudinal and experimental studies. IFI16 is a DNA sensor and innate immune regulator known to activate inflammasome signaling and type I interferon responses. 42 PPI and GO enrichment analyses revealed that IFI16 is closely connected to inflammasome components, including AIM2, CASP1, and PYCARD, as well as cGAS–STING signaling and DNA damage response pathways. These pathways converge on pyroptosis and interleukin‐1β production, processes that have been implicated in microglial activation, synaptic dysfunction, and amyloid‐related neurotoxicity. 43 , 44 Our findings therefore suggest a model in which sleep disturbance induces IFI16 upregulation, amplifies neuroinflammatory signaling, and accelerates Aβ pathology, ultimately associated with cognitive decline. This study has several limitations. First, regarding the phenotypic assessment of sleep, we relied on caregiver‐reported NPIK scores rather than objective physiological measures. While clinically relevant, this approach may introduce reporting bias and lacks the precision to capture specific sleep architecture metrics measured by polysomnography or actigraphy. Second, concerning the measurement of pathology, although CSF Aβ42 is a sensitive marker for early amyloidosis, it reflects global biochemical changes and does not capture the regional distribution or plaque load dynamics provided by amyloid PET imaging. Third, there are inherent limitations to the genetic analyses. Our MR findings were based on GWAS and proteomics datasets predominantly from individuals of European ancestry; thus, the generalizability of these estimated associations to other ancestral groups remains to be established. Furthermore, MR infers potential links based on lifelong genetic liability to insomnia, which is not directly equivalent to the cross‐sectional, caregiver‐reported sleep disturbance assessed by the NPIK. Finally, despite the use of robust sensitivity analyses, residual horizontal pleiotropy and population stratification cannot be entirely excluded. In conclusion, in this large ADNI‐based study integrating cross‐sectional and longitudinal clinical data, CSF biomarkers, proteomics, and bioinformatics analysis, we demonstrate that sleep disturbance is not merely a clinical correlate but a predictor of accelerated Aβ pathology and cognitive decline. Our findings identify a proteomic network module linking sleep disturbance to CSF Aβ42 reduction and highlight IFI16 as a putative link that may act through inflammasome‐related pathways. These results provide insight into how disrupted sleep may exacerbate AD pathogenesis and suggest that targeting sleep‐related neuroimmune signaling could represent a promising strategy for early intervention in AD.
