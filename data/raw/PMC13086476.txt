AQP4 Drives Gliotic Changes in an APPPS1 Mouse Model of Alzheimer’s Disease

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is the most common form of dementia, accounting for most of the cases, especially in individuals aged 65 and older. While the existence of genetic factors has helped us create animal models of AD that mimic APP and Ab overproduction, sporadic cases represent the bulk casuistry, and most probably their pathology is related to a loss of function towards the clearance of Ab rather than consecutive the overproduction of Ab alone. It is known that aquaporin 4 (AQP4) facilitation amplifies the perivascular Ab clearance route and decreases Ab deposits in animal models of AD, however it is now known how the glial component of the CNS responds to this treatment. We have aimed here to assess the glial response in a APPPS1 transgenic mouse model of AD, after one month of pharmacological facilitation or inhibition of AQP4. To this extent, we have utilized APPPS1 mice of 2 months of age, treated daily for 28 days with either TGN-020 AQP4 inhibitor or the TGN-073 AQP4 facilitator, and compared their GFAP expression in the brain with that of untreated APPPS1 and wild-type animals. Our image analysis of the GFAP immunohistochemical pattern, showed that AQP4 facilitation increases GFAP expression in the brains of APPPS1 animals, compared to untreated APPPS1 animals, but complexity-lacunarity morphological patterns resemble in fact those of wild-type animals, rather than that of the APPPS1 untreated animals, suggesting that this GFAP reactivity might represent a benefic amyloid-clearance astrocytic profile.

Introduction Alzheimer’s disease (AD) represents an increasingly significant global health challenge, with marked variation across age, sex, and geographic regions [ 1 ]. Dementia encompasses a spectrum of disorders characterized by cognitive decline, among which Alzheimer’s disease is the most prevalent, accounting for more than two-thirds of diagnoses in individuals aged 65 years and older [ 2 ]. Alzheimer’s disease is a progressive, irreversible neurodegenerative disorder that severely compromises daily functioning and social independence [ 3 ]. Ageing is the predominant risk factor for Alzheimer’s disease, interacting with genetic susceptibility, vascular pathology, metabolic dysfunction, traumatic brain injury, infections, and environmental exposures [ 4 , 5 , 6 ]. Rare pathogenic variants in APP, PSEN1, and PSEN2 cause early-onset familial Alzheimer’s disease, accounting for approximately 5% of cases, while common APOE polymorphisms-particularly the ε4 allele-modulate risk for a large proportion of late-onset disease [ 7 , 8 , 9 ]. Collectively, genetic factors explain an estimated 30-50% of Alzheimer’s disease heritability. Down syndrome, resulting from trisomy 21 and overexpression of APP, further underscores the central role of amyloid-related pathways in Alzheimer pathogenesis [ 10 , 11 , 12 ]. Vascular and metabolic conditions, including hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, obesity, and cerebrovascular disease, are consistently associated with increased Alzheimer’s disease risk [ 13 ]. Hypertension promotes cerebral hypoperfusion and vascular remodeling, while type 2 diabetes exacerbates neurodegeneration through impaired glucose metabolism, insulin resistance, and mitochondrial dysfunction [ 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 ]. Lifestyle factors such as smoking, alcohol consumption, physical inactivity, and depression further contribute substantially to disease risk [ 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 ]. Traumatic brain injury, infectious agents, and environmental toxicants, including heavy metals and airborne pollutants, act as additional contributors through sustained neuroinflammation, blood-brain barrier disruption, and oxidative stress [ 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 ]. Amyloid plaques and neurofibrillary tangles constitute the two hallmark lesions of Alzheimer’s disease [ 28 ]. Amyloid deposition increases with age and preferentially affects higher-order association cortices [ 29 , 30 ]. Plaques are heterogeneous structures composed of amyloid-β, dystrophic neurites, and reactive glial cells, evolving from diffuse deposits to dense-core and neuritic plaques that correlate most strongly with cognitive decline [ 31 , 32 ]. With disease progression, plaques become increasingly infiltrated by activated microglia and astrocytes, indicating that glial recruitment accompanies plaque maturation rather than initiation [ 33 ]. Growing evidence implicates vascular dysfunction as an early and critical driver of Alzheimer’s disease. Impaired cerebrovascular regulation, reduced cerebral perfusion, and defective perivascular clearance precede overt amyloid and tau pathology [ 34 ]. Age-related failure of perivascular drainage, mediated by a dysfunctional astrocytic aquaporin 4 function, enables amyloid-β accumulation within vascular basement membranes, promoting cerebral amyloid angiopathy and further impairing clearance [ 35 , 36 ]. Across experimental models and human tissue, vascular amyloid deposition disrupts microvascular flow, exacerbates hypoperfusion, and establishes a self-reinforcing cycle linking vascular injury, amyloid retention, and neurodegeneration [ 36 , 37 , 38 ]. Mutation-carrying mice models of AD treated with AQP4 facilitators and inhibitors proved to modulate total amyloid burden and its shape into the brains of these animals, however, the complete morphopathological changes induced in the brains of these animals are not described [ 39 ]. The aim of this study was to asses astrocytic changes in the brains of APPPS1 mice treated with either an inhibitor or a facilitator of AQP4, and compare it with wild-type animals and untreated Materials and Methods We utilized here C57BL/6J-TgN mice with the double human mutant APP (Thy1-APPKM670/671NL) and presenilin (Thy1-PS1L166P)[ 40 , 41 ] profile (N=45), and wild type (N=12) animals of 2 months of age. Transgenic mice were randomized to receive daily intraperitoneal (i.p.) injections of either 200mg/kg of the TGN-020AQP4 inhibitor (N=15), 200mg/kg of the TGN-073 AQP4 facilitator (N=15), or sterile distilled water only (N=15). Moreover, 12 wild type mice were injected with sterile distilled water daily, as the wild type control group. As treatments, each day, 20mg/ml solutions of the TGN-020 [N-(1,3,4-thiadiazol-2-yl) pyridine-3-carboxamide dihydrochloride, M=279.1463] and TGN-073 [N-(3-(Benzyloxy)pyridin-2-yl benzene-sulfonamide, M=340.40, Ukrorgsyntez Ltd., Kiev, Ukraine] were prepared fresh with sterile distilled water and adjusted to a pH of 8.0 as previously described [ 39 ]. Intraperitoneal injections were administrated daily for 28 days, and after this the animals were anesthetized, brains harvested and processed for routine fixation and paraffin embedding. Tissue slides were cut and further processed for simple enzymatic immunohistochemistry for detecting astrocytes, utilizing a rabbit anti-GFAP antibody (Cat no. Z0334, Dako, Glostrup, Denmark). Briefly, the sections were rehydrated to distilled water, and first processed for antigen retrieval by microwaving in 0.1M citrate buffer pH 6 for 20 minutes. After thorough washing, the slides were incubated for 30 minutes in a 1% hydrogen peroxide solution, blocked for further 30 minutes in a 3% skimmed milk solution (Bio-Rad, München, Germany), and then incubated overnight at 4°C with the primary antibody diluted as 1:20.000. Next day, the slides were washed thoroughly and incubated with an anti-rabbit peroxidase-conjugated polymer secondary antibody (Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA, United States), washed further on, then the signal was detected with 3’,3′-diaminobenzidine (ImmPACT DAB, Vector Laboratories), and counterstained with hematoxylin and coverslipped with a permanent mounting medium (DPX, Sigma-Aldrich, Hamburg, Germany). We were also interested to assess the astrocytes in their close proximity to the vascular endothelia, and to this extent we have also double stained a series of sections for GFAP and CD31. The slides were processed for antigen retrieval as described above, blocked in skimmed milk, and incubated overnight with a mixture of mouse anti-CD31 (Cat no. M0823, Dako, 1:50) and rabbit anti-GFAP (Dako, 1:20.000) primary antibodies. The next day, after thorough washing, the signals were simultaneously detected with a mix of goat anti-mouse Alexa Fluor 594 and goat anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 488 secondary antibodies (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA USA; 1:300) for 2 h at room temperature, and finally the slides were coverslipped with an antifade mounting medium containing DAPI (Vectashield, Vector Laboratories). Fluorescently labelled slides were imaged utilizing a Nikon 90i fluorescent microscope equipped with narrow-band specific fluorescence filter sets and a Nikon DS-Ri2 16Mp CMOS camera (Nikon Europe BV, Amsterdam, the Netherlands). Further on, whole tissue slides stained for GFAP were scanned utilizing a Motic EasyScan Pro 6 slide scanner (Motic Digital Pathology, Barcelona, Spain) with a 40× objective, and each image was manually devided into four anatomical regions of interest: neocortex, hippocampus, striatum, and thalamus [ 42 ]. Randomly selected images were next utilized to generate an RGB color profile of the DAB staining in Image ProPlus 11 image analysis package (Media Cybernetics, Bethesda, MD, USA), and then all images have been analysed utilizing the same RGB profile, recording signal areas, fractal dimension and clumpiness of the color binary masks. Data were collected in Microsoft Office Excel 2016 sheets (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA) and further analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9.2 (GraphPad Software LLC, Boston, MA, USA). In order to assess statistical differences between untreated and treated APPPS1 groups, a one-way ANOVA followed by a Bonferroni post hoc test were used, while correlations were sought using Pearson's correlation coefficient. Data are reported as mean±standard error of the mean (SEM). For all analyses, p&lt;0.05 was deemed as threshold for statistical significance. Results We first explored the expression area of GFAP in all animal groups and for the four designated anatomical areas. Immunohistochemistry for GFAP did not reveal reactive gliosis, defined as dense groups of astrocytes with enlarged cytoplasmatic masses and intermingled and overlapped elongations, but merely changes in overall cellular densities per reported area, with the presence of inconspicuous cytoplasmatic masses and rather fine processes (Figure 1 ). Upon observation, for its given relative anatomical area, hippocampus regions seemed to harbor the highest number of astrocytes in all animals group, probably an intrinsic feature of the region and animal model. Upon quantification of the GFAP-expression area, wild type animals showed the lowest gliotic reactivity for all anatomical regions, and this difference attained statistical significance for the cortex [0.04±0.05% versus 0.16±0.10% for untreated APPPS1 animals, 0.22±0.12% for the facilitator-treated APPPS1 animals, and respectively 0.15±0.11% for the inhibitor-treated APPPS1 animals], and for the hippocampus [0.27±0.03% versus 0.40±0.07% for untreated APPPS1 animals, 0.49±0.05% for the facilitator-treated APPPS1 animals, and respectively 0.45±0.08% for the inhibitor-treated APPPS1 animals], p&lt;0.05 (Figure 2A ). Although the cortex still harbored the smallest area percentages, there was no statistically significant difference between the wild type animals and the other groups for thalamus and striatum. A second pattern that emerged was that of the facilitator-treated animals that exhibited the highest GFAP expression, and although non-significant, this was maintained for the cortex, hippocampus and striatum, while the thalamus showed in fact a drop of GFAP area for the facilitator-treated animals. Also, overall, hippocampus yielded the highest GFAP expression per area unit among all other regions, and it was also here that wild type animals exhibited significant differences from all other regions, underlying the high morphological dynamics of this plastic region of the brain. We have next assessed the morphology of the astrocytes, by evaluating their FD values following bidimensional morphometry of their binary silhouettes (Figure 2B ). This analysis revealed a relative uniform pattern for all the four brain areas under investigation, with a massive drop for the untreated APPPS1 animals compared to the wild type group, with increasing FD complexity for the facilitator-treated animals, and with maximal values for inhibitor-treated transgenic mice. For all regions, APPPS1 animals group showed a significant drop compared to wild-type animals, and the inhibitor-treated group had significantly higher FD values compared to the untreated group. Figure 1 Exemplary images of GFAP staining for astrocytes in the four considered anatomical regions (cortex, thalamus, hippocampus and striatum) of wild type, untreated APPPS1, AQP4-facilitator-treated APPPS1 and AQP4-inhibitor-treated APPPS1mice Altogether, both facilitator-treated animals and inhibitor-treated animals had higher FD astrocyte patterns compared to untreated animals, that brought their morphology closer to the wild type group rather than to the untreated APPPS1 group. This revealed complex and incompletely understood mechanisms that drive astrocytes complexity in the same direction despite originating in a completely opposite initiation, respectively inhibition or facilitation of the function of AQP4 water pore. We have also explored the clumpiness of the GFAP binary masks, as a measure of spatial distribution, heterogeneity, or "gapiness" in the staining (Figure 2C ). For the cortex, astrocytes showed higher clumpiness values for the untreated APPPS1 and facilitator-treated mice groups, compared to the wild-type and inhibitor-treated mice, revealing a more packed pattern or more activated-prone astrocytes in these mice. The facilitator-treated groups had the highest values among all, showing significant differences from wild-type and inhibitor-treated mice, but indistinct from untreated APPPS1 mice. For the thalamus and hippocampus, facilitator-treated animals showed values that closely resembled those of wild-type animals, while untreated and inhibitor-treated animals showed consistently lower values, but this trend was significant only for the hippocampus. Lastly, we looked at correlation between fractal dimension and the clumpiness of the binary silhouettes of the astrocytes in the cortex, as the largest anatomical and signal areas overall (Figure 2D ). This revealed the untreated APPPS1 and inhibitor-treated APPPS1 animals showed increasing correlation between complexity and compactness (r=0.184 and r=0.338), reflecting probably a more organized activated-like astrocytic profile. However, for the wild-type animals and for the facilitator-treated APPPS1 group, there was a clear-cut dissociation between the two morphological denominators (r=0.024 and r=0.081), reflecting probably a non-reactive glial pattern, and altogether showing that AQP4 facilitation does not seem to shift the astrocytes to an activated morphological state despite increased GFAP-signal area associated with amyloid pathology in these mice. Significance is showed for one-way ANOVA followed by a post hoc Bonferroni test (*, p&lt;0.05; **, p&lt;0.01). Data are expressed as means±SEM. Figure 3 Exemplary images from cortical areas of the four animal groups double-stained for CD31 and GFAP in order to assess the distribution of astrocyte morphology in relationship with the vascular endothelium Lastly, we have inspected double stained GFAP-CD31 slides in order to appreciate if any observable differences exist between the four animal groups in what it regards the distribution of astrocytes in the neuropil and blood vessels (Figure 3 ). Our observations showed that most astrocyte cell bodies resided in the neuropil, while end-feets surrounded blood vessels, and these morphological arrangements did not seem to differ between the four animal groups, when analyzing the same anatomical regions. Together with the fact that the morphological complexities of the astrocytes in both treated groups resemble those of the wild type group, these data show that the morphology of the brain blood barrier seems still intact while modulation the function of AQP4, and that any treatment option aiming at the blood vessels is feasible despite amyloid accumulation in these animals. Discussions Aquaporin 4 is an essential water transport channel in the central nervous system, which has been deemed essential for the perivascular clearance of the interstitial fluid of the brain, including catabolites of the brain metabolisms and toxins. Based on this hypothesis, we and others have showed that AQP4 facilitating mechanisms are efficient in clearing the amyloid Aβ from overproducing APPPS1 young mice, at the age at which these animals usually start accumulating it in the brain [ 39 ]. Despite this, not much is known about other neuropathological effects that the AQP4 facilitator TGN-073 might exhibit on the normal astrocyte function and structure. Under physiological conditions, intact AQP4 expression supports adaptive astrocytic responses rather than overt pathological gliosis. Neural activity-dependent glial volume changes are preserved in wild-type tissue, microscopic imaging indicating dynamic astrocytic swelling during stimulation, while neuronal metabolic activity remains unchanged [ 43 ]. At the gliovascular interface, endothelial-derived extracellular matrix components and mechanical cues promote enrichment and polarization of AQP4 at astrocytic end feet, supporting the formation of a specialized astrocyte phenotype optimized for dynamic water exchange and process organization [ 44 ]. Pharmacological facilitation of AQP4 activity increases interstitial fluid turnover and glymphatic circulation, establishing water gradients that promote efficient parenchymal fluid movement without suppressing glial engagement, indicating that preserved AQP4 function supports physiological astrocyte activity rather than quiescence phenotype [ 45 ]. Experimental conditions that preserve or restore AQP4 polarization are associated with enhanced astrocytic reactivity coupled to improved clearance capacity in amyloid models. Pharmacological activation of GLP-1 receptor signaling in amyloid-producing mice induces relocation of phosphorylated AQP4 to astrocytic perivascular end feet, particularly in morphologically reactive astrocytes. This redistribution coincides with reduced cortical amyloid-β burden and improved cognitive performance ([ 46 ]). In APPPS1 mice, chronic pharmacological facilitation of AQP4 leads to substantial reductions in total, fibrillar, and soluble amyloid deposition, accompanied by improved behavioral performance [ 39 ]. In wild-type tissue, intact AQP4 expression supports stimulus-dependent astrocytic swelling and calcium-coupled volume regulation, reflecting adaptive morphological plasticity rather than hypertrophic gliosis. Astrocytes exhibit dynamic volume changes in response to physiological ionic and activity-driven stimuli, consistent with preserved structural flexibility [ 43 , 47 ]. AQP4 availability is rate-limiting for astrocyte-mediated water flux, as demonstrated by transgenic overexpression models in which increased AQP4 accelerates astrocytic water influx under acute osmotic stress, confirming a direct link between AQP4 expression and astrocyte volume dynamics [ 48 ]. In amyloid animal models, preserved or facilitated AQP4 function supports astrocyte structural plasticity around pathological lesions. Redistribution of AQP4 from perivascular end feet toward parenchymal astrocyte processes correlates with the formation of peri-plaque astrocytic networks. These networks contribute to plaque containment and reduced neuronal uptake of amyloid-β, limiting neuritic dystrophy [ 49 ]. Astrocytes exhibiting restored AQP4 polarization display a reactive but organized phenotype rather than atrophy, supporting a protective glial architecture within the plaque microenvironment [ 39 , 46 ]. Across experimental systems, AQP4 inhibition or deletion consistently attenuates astrocytic hypertrophy and reactivity. In retinal tissue, absence of AQP4 renders Müller glia susceptible to acute volume dysregulation under osmotic stress, triggering inflammatory signaling and upregulation of pro-inflammatory mediators [ 50 ]. In cortical and subcortical injury models, AQP4 deficiency produces a biphasic response: reduced acute edema and lesion size, followed by impaired astrocyte proliferation, defective glial scar formation, and enhanced microglial accumulation at later stages [ 51 ]. Astrocytes lacking AQP4 show preserved basal morphology but markedly impaired migratory capacity. Both genetic deletion and RNA-mediated suppression of AQP4 reduce astrocyte motility in vitro and in vivo, demonstrating that AQP4 is required for effective astrocyte polarization and recruitment during reactive gliosis [ 52 ]. In Alzheimer-type mouse models, AQP4 loss is consistently associated with suppressed reactive gliosis despite worsened neuropathology, and parallels our finding that AQP4 inhibition leads to decreased GFAP expression areas compared to facilitator-treated animals. In APPPS1 mice, deletion of AQP4 aggravates cognitive deficits and increases amyloid accumulation, cerebral amyloid angiopathy, and synaptic protein loss. Astrocytes display marked atrophy with reduced GFAP expression and decreased cytokine levels, indicating dampened astrogliosis in the presence of enhanced amyloid pathology [ 53 ]. Histological analyses confirm that plaque-associated astrocytes in AQP4-null APP/PS1 mice exhibit reduced size, weak GFAP immunoreactivity, and diminished spatial coverage, while reactive microgliosis around plaques is mildly decreased [ 53 ]. In 5xFAD mice, AQP4 deletion increases cortical amyloid deposition, shifts plaques toward fibrillar morphologies, reduces peri-plaque astrocytic processes, and diminishes microglial plaque coverage, accompanied by increased neuronal amyloid uptake and neuritic dystrophy [ 49 ]. In wild-type contexts, reducing AQP4 expression alters astrocyte morphology in a species-dependent manner. While mouse astrocytes exhibit modest shape changes, AQP4 knockdown induces marked cytoskeletal reorganization, reduced connexin-43 expression, and impaired intercellular coupling, indicating disrupted structural integrity and network connectivity [ 54 , 55 ]. In amyloid models, AQP4 inhibition or deletion shifts astrocyte morphology from hypertrophic, GFAP-rich phenotypes toward atrophic profiles with reduced peri-plaque coverage. This structural attenuation is accompanied by diminished microglial engagement and impaired containment of amyloid aggregates, despite increased plaque burden and neuronal injury [ 49 , 53 ]. Genetic deletion of AQP4 also exacerbates glymphatic dysfunction following injury, promoting tau accumulation, neurofibrillary pathology, and neurodegeneration, indicating that insufficient astrocytic reactivity and impaired clearance mechanisms contribute to disease progression [ 56 , 57 ]. Across experimental systems, AQP4 inhibition or deletion consistently reduces astrocyte hypertrophy, GFAP expression, migration, and scar formation, while often exacerbating long-term neurodegeneration and protein accumulation. In contrast, preservation or facilitation of AQP4 function is associated with enhanced astrocytic reactivity, restored perivascular polarization, and improved clearance capacity, even when accompanied by transient inflammatory signaling. Conclusions Altogether, our data showed that AQP4 facilitation increases astrocytes GFAP overall expression in the brains of APPPS1 animals, compared to untreated APPPS1 animals, but the fractal-lacunarity analysis revealed that in fact their morphological patterns resemble those of the wild-type animals, rather than that of the APPPS1 untreated animals. This suggests that AQP4 facilitation may act in the direction of restoring GFAP structure and function, but with the histopathological end result heavily modulated by the presence of Ab in these animals.
