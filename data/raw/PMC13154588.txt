The neuro-vascular-immune triad: the interactive network in the tumor microenvironment

The heterogeneity of the tumor microenvironment (TME) constitutes a major obstacle in cancer therapy. Emerging evidence reveals that the nervous, vascular, and immune systems form a complex, interactive regulatory network within the TME, termed the “neuro-vascular-immune triad.” This review systematically delineates the architecture and function of this network. Neural activity, via neurotransmitters and neurotrophic factors, promotes angiogenesis and induces immunosuppression. Aberrant vasculature creates hypoxia, which acts as a core hub by stabilizing Hypoxia-Inducible Factor-1α (HIF-1α) to further exacerbate immune suppression and neural remodeling. Immune cells, in turn, secrete inflammatory factors that feedback to nerves and vessels. This self-reinforcing vicious cycle collectively drives tumor progression and therapeutic resistance. Critically, the integrated status of this triad strongly correlates with clinical outcomes, highlighting its utility in prognostic stratification. Key signaling nodes, such as HIF-1α, β2-adrenergic receptor (β2-AR), calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP), and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), emerge as prospective multi-dimensional biomarkers for monitoring TME dynamics and predicting treatment response. We further summarize emerging combination strategies targeting this network, such as the synergy between immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), anti-angiogenic agents, and β-blockers, that aim to disrupt this malignant circuitry. Deciphering this cross-regulatory network provides an integrative new paradigm for overcoming treatment resistance and outlines promising translational avenues for next-generation cancer therapy.

Introduction The tumor microenvironment (TME) is a highly heterogeneous ecosystem comprising malignant cells, stromal components, immune infiltrates, vascular networks, and nerve fibers [ 1 – 3 ]. This complexity poses significant therapeutic challenges, as targeting a single component often leads to compensatory adaptations in others, resulting in treatment failure and disease recurrence [ 4 – 6 ]. Traditionally, cancer has been viewed primarily as a genetic and cellular disease. However, emerging paradigms in neuro-oncology have recognized the nervous system as a critical organ system co-opted by tumors to fuel progression [ 7 , 8 ]. Tumors can be classified based on their “neural dimensions”: those with high neural density and active neurogenesis versus those with sparse innervation that nonetheless rely on neuro-signaling for metastasis [ 7 , 9 , 10 ]. The scenario is particularly profound in tumors of the central nervous system, such as gliomas, where malignant cells not only hijack existing circuits but also exhibit neuronal properties themselves, forming functional synapses with healthy neurons to drive proliferation [ 11 – 13 ]. This neural dependency is not merely a bystander effect but a hallmark of aggressive malignancy. A pivotal question concerns the origin of neural components within the TME. Current evidence suggests a dual mechanism: while many tumors actively recruit peripheral nerve fibers via neurotrophic factors (e.g., Nerve Growth Factor; NGF, Brain-derived Neurotrophic Factor; BDNF) during progression, others possess an intrinsic, latent neurogenic potential [ 14 – 16 ]. This cryptic program can be unmasked under specific pathophysiological stresses, such as chronic psychological stress, therapeutic pressure (e.g., chemotherapy-induced neuropathy), or immune surveillance. Once activated, these programs drive the differentiation of tumor cells into neuron-like states or stimulate the sprouting of new nerve fibers, fundamentally reshaping the TME architecture [ 17 – 19 ]. The TME is not a collection of isolated systems but a dynamic ecosystem defined by triadic crosstalk among neural, vascular, and immune components. This heterogeneity creates multiple layers of regulatory networks that complicate therapeutic intervention. Tumor-nerve interactions involve tumors secreting neurotrophins to attract nerves, while nerves release neurotransmitters that directly stimulate tumor growth and migration [ 20 , 21 ]. Nerve-immune interactions position neural signals as potent immunomodulators, suppressing cytotoxic activity and promoting tolerance. Tumor-immune interactions, the classic axis where tumors evade detection, are now understood to be heavily influenced by neural inputs [ 22 , 23 ]. Crucially, these interactions form a feedback loop; for instance, hypoxia-driven angiogenesis often coincides with neurogenesis, and both processes are regulated by immune-derived cytokines, creating a self-reinforcing network [ 24 – 26 ]. How do such tripartite interactions elicit tumor immune evasion and mount resistance against available immune-based therapies? The answer lies in the creation of an “immune-suppressed” niche. Neural signaling can directly inhibit the infiltration and function of CD8⁺ T cells and NK cells while recruiting immunosuppressive populations like regulatory T cells (Tregs) and M2 macrophages [ 27 , 28 ]. Furthermore, this neuro-vascular-immune triad contributes to therapy resistance. Stress-induced sympathetic activation can diminish the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) by altering the metabolic landscape of the TME. Similarly, anti-angiogenic therapies may inadvertently trigger a compensatory surge in neurogenesis, restoring the immunosuppressive niche and leading to treatment failure [ 29 ]. Thus, the neural dimension acts as a master switch that can override single-modality treatments, explaining much of the heterogeneity observed in clinical responses. Given these complexities, targeting the triadic crosstalk offers a strategic advantage. Monotherapies targeting only the tumor, vasculature, or immune system have shown limited durability due to the compensatory plasticity of remaining components. Disrupting the neural input can “re-sensitize” the TME to immunotherapy and normalize the vasculature [ 30 ]. Potential strategies include blocking specific neurotransmitter receptors, inhibiting neurotrophic signaling pathways, or using neuromodulation techniques to reset the immune tone. By dismantling the structural and signaling scaffold provided by the neuro-vascular-immune triad, we can potentially convert “cold” tumors into “hot” ones, overcoming resistance and improving clinical outcomes [ 31 , 32 ]. In this review, we systematically dissect the molecular mechanisms underlying this triadic network, focusing on the pivotal role of (Hypoxia-Inducible Factor-1α) HIF-1α and other key hubs, and discuss the translational implications for next-generation combination therapies. Regarding axon guidance molecules, studies have shown that placental growth factor not only promotes neurite outgrowth but also, through chemotaxis, guides tumor cell migration towards nerve fibers, establishing a bidirectional tumor-nerve interaction network [ 68 ]. Notably, a single guidance molecule can exert dual effects: for instance, FLRT3 binding to the UNC5B receptor can simultaneously promote tumor growth and suppress T-cell cytotoxic function, whereas blocking the FLRT3-UNC5B interaction can reverse this immunosuppressive effect [ 69 ]. At the molecular level, tumor cells remodel the neural microenvironment through various mechanisms, such as secreting exosomes that directly promote axonal extension from central nervous system neurons [ 60 ], inducing endoplasmic reticulum stress responses that trigger neurite outgrowth and tumor innervation [ 70 ], or expressing neurotrophic markers and axon guidance molecules that both promote axonogenesis and recruit neural precursor cells to the TME [ 71 ]. Simultaneously, distinct nerve types exert significantly different effects on tumors. Sympathetic nerves, through the release of NE, drive angiogenesis [ 71 , 72 ], whereas sensory nerves promote tumor metastasis via the secretion of neuropeptides such as calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) [ 37 , 73 , 74 ]. Furthermore, components within the TME, such as Schwann cells, contribute to forming a niche that supports neural fiber outgrowth by secreting neurotrophic factors and axon guidance molecules [ 37 , 75 , 76 ]. Key neurotrophic factors include NGF, BDNF and glial cell line-derived neurotrophic factor [ 77 , 78 ]. Additionally, Schwann cells secrete axon guidance molecules such as netrins [ 79 ], semaphorins [ 80 ], ephrins [ 81 ], and cell adhesion molecules (e.g., L1-CAM, N-cadherin) that direct neurite extension and pathfinding [ 82 , 83 ]. These findings collectively reveal the complex molecular dialogue underlying tumor innervation, providing a theoretical foundation for developing therapeutic strategies that target tumor-nerve interactions. Regulation of the immune microenvironment by neurotransmitters The TME contains various neurotransmitters, including catecholamines, acetylcholine, and serotonin [ 84 – 87 ]. These molecules act in autocrine and paracrine manners, not only directly influencing tumor progression but also remodeling the tumor immune microenvironment by modulating immune cell functions [ 88 , 89 ]. At the molecular level, neurotransmitters regulate immune cell activity through multiple pathways. For instance, dopamine exerts dual regulatory effects on different immune cell subsets via its receptors: it can promote the immunosuppressive function of Tregs while simultaneously enhancing the anti-tumor activity of CD8 + T cells [ 90 – 92 ]. NE, through β-adrenergic receptor (β-AR) signaling, inhibits pro-inflammatory responses in myeloid cells while boosting the secretion of anti-inflammatory cytokines [ 93 ]. Mechanistically, β-AR activation increases intracellular cAMP levels, which activates PKA [ 94 ]. PKA then phosphorylates the transcription factor CREB, leading to enhanced transcription of anti-inflammatory cytokine genes including IL-10 [ 95 ] and TGF-β [ 96 ]. This signaling cascade also suppresses NF-κB activity, further reducing pro-inflammatory cytokine production [ 97 ]. Conversely, γ-Aminobutyric Acid (GABA) can inhibit T cell proliferation and cytokine production via GABA receptors but promotes macrophage differentiation [ 88 , 98 ]. Notably, these neurotransmitter signals are closely linked to the metabolic reprogramming of immune cells. NE can promote glycolysis by activating the mTOR pathway, thereby influencing T cell activation and differentiation [ 99 , 100 ]. GABA, on the other hand, can alter the levels of tricarboxylic acid cycle intermediates to modulate the energy metabolism of immune cells [ 98 , 101 , 102 ]. In colorectal cancer, aberrant expression of neurotransmitter signaling pathways not only directly stimulates tumor progression by promoting cancer cell proliferation, migration, and angiogenesis but also indirectly facilitates immune escape by reshaping the immunometabolic landscape [ 89 , 103 ]. While the ‘go-or-grow’ hypothesis suggests proliferation and migration may be mutually exclusive at the single-cell level, emerging evidence indicates that β-adrenergic signaling can overcome this constraint through multi-pathway branching mechanisms [ 104 ]. Specifically, parallel signaling cascades allow neurotransmitter signaling to coordinate both processes independently at the tumor population level. Recent research indicates that neurotransmitter release induced by psychological stress forms a “neuro-immune-metabolic” regulatory network through the above mechanisms, wherein immunometabolic reprogramming (e.g., enhanced glycolysis, mitochondrial dysfunction) serves as a critical link connecting neural signals to immune dysfunction [ 105 , 106 ]. This discovery provides a novel perspective for understanding the role of the nervous system in cancer immunoediting and lays a theoretical foundation for developing combination therapies targeting the neurotransmitter-immunometabolism axis. The vascular system: a pivotal hub in the tripartite network Neuro-vascular coupling promotes tumor angiogenesis A growing body of evidence indicates that the nervous system within the TME significantly promotes tumor angiogenesis through neuro-vascular coupling mechanisms [ 47 , 107 , 108 ]. Neurotransmitters and neuropeptides released from nerve terminals bind to G protein-coupled receptors on the surface of adjacent vascular endothelial cells, activating classical signaling pathways such as adenylate cyclase/cAMP/PKA and PLC/PKC, thereby upregulating the activity of transcription factors HIF-1α and NF-κB [ 47 , 109 ]. These transcription factors specifically bind to the promoter regions of pro-angiogenic factors such as VEGFA, fibroblast growth factor2 (FGF2), and platelet-derived growth factor, significantly enhancing their expression levels [ 110 , 111 ]. Under hypoxic conditions, β-catenin secreted by neurons and glial cells undergoes nuclear translocation and interacts with the TCF/LEF transcription factor complex, further synergistically boosting VEGFA gene transcription efficiency [ 112 , 113 ]. Concurrently, neurogenic signals also regulate the Dll4-Notch1-Hey2 signaling axis by activating the Notch pathway in endothelial cells, precisely controlling the spatiotemporal dynamics of vascular sprouting [ 114 , 115 ]. This multi-layered signaling network forms a positive feedback loop: neural innervation not only promotes angiogenesis, but newly formed vascular endothelial cells also secrete BDNF and axon guidance molecules (Semaphorin3A), which in turn enhance nerve fiber extension via TrkB and Neuropilin-1 receptors [ 116 , 117 ]. Notably, under sustained hypoxia, angiopoietin-like protein 2 activates the PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling pathway, inducing the expression of the glycolytic enzyme HK2 and triggering metabolic reprogramming [ 118 , 119 ]. This shift in energy metabolism not only sustains high-level VEGFA secretion but also stabilizes a long-term pro-angiogenic phenotype through lactate-mediated histone deacetylation and epigenetic modifications [ 120 , 121 ]. Vascular abnormalities and the formation of an immunosuppressive microenvironment Structural and functional abnormalities in the tumor vasculature are hallmark features of most solid tumors, directly contributing to the establishment of an immunosuppressive microenvironment [ 122 , 123 ]. Specifically, the aberrant vascular network exacerbates immunosuppression through multiple coordinated mechanisms. On one hand, its structural disorganization leads to tissue hypoxia, which in turn upregulates immunosuppressive molecules such as VEGF and PD-L1 via HIF-1α [ 124 – 126 ]. On the other hand, vascular dysfunction causes acidosis and elevated interstitial fluid pressure, which not only hinders the infiltration of effector T cells into tumor tissues but also promotes the accumulation of immunosuppressive cells like Tregs and M2-type TAMs [ 127 , 128 ]. In this process, key signaling molecules such as VEGF, TGF-β, and IL-10 act as critical bridges: VEGF promotes vascular abnormalities while simultaneously inhibiting dendritic cell maturation; TGF-β participates in both angiogenesis stimulation and Treg differentiation; and IL-10 suppresses T-cell function while exacerbating vascular leakage [ 122 , 129 , 130 ]. Further research indicates a dynamic inverse correlation between vascular abnormalities and immune activity [ 124 , 131 , 132 ]. The pro-angiogenic microenvironment maintains an immunosuppressive state through ANG2/TIE2 and vascular endothelial growth factor receptor (VEGFR) signaling pathways, whereas anti-angiogenic therapy can downregulate these pathways, promoting a shift in the TME towards immune activation [ 129 , 133 ]. This bidirectional regulation creates a vicious cycle: abnormal vasculature upregulates immune checkpoint molecules such as PD-L1 and cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (CTLA-4) via hypoxia-induced factors, while immunosuppressive cells secrete pro-angiogenic factors like FGF2 and placental growth factor, further exacerbating vascular structural and functional dysfunction [ 131 , 134 – 136 ]. It is particularly noteworthy that metabolic alterations within the immune microenvironment, such as the upregulated expression of indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO) and arginase 1, can lead to local tryptophan depletion and arginine deficiency, directly inhibiting T-cell function [ 123 , 129 ]. The core of this vicious cycle lies in the reciprocal regulation between the vasculature and the immune system, involving several key nodes: dysfunctional vascular endothelial cadherin impairs the transendothelial migration of immune cells; dysregulated chemokine gradients, particularly an imbalance in the CXCL12/CXCR4 axis, disrupt the directional homing of immune cells; and aberrant expression of adhesion molecules such as ICAM-1 and VCAM-1 restricts effective lymphocyte adhesion [ 132 , 137 , 138 ]. Recent studies have further revealed that some tumor vascular endothelial cells can form inhibitory synapses with immune cells via epithelial-mesenchymal transition related molecules (e.g., Twist and Snail), thereby locally establishing an immune-privileged microenvironment [ 134 , 139 ]. Integrative mechanisms of vascular regulators in TME remodeling Vascular regulators play an integrative role in TME remodeling by orchestrating angiogenesis, vascular normalization, and immune modulation, thereby influencing tumor progression and therapeutic response [ 122 , 131 , 140 , 141 ]. The aberrant morphology and dysfunction of tumor vasculature represent hallmark features of TME, where pathological vascular networks induce hypoxia and foster an immunosuppressive milieu that facilitates immune evasion and treatment resistance [ 140 , 141 ]. For instance, vascular regulators like nitric oxide can improve vascular function through normalization, enhancing perfusion and promoting immune cell infiltration to remodel the immune landscape [ 127 , 142 ]. Moreover, tumor-derived pro-angiogenic factors drive vascular remodeling via the IGF1/IGF1R axis in endothelial cells, supporting rapid regeneration of therapy-resistant tumors [ 143 ]. Exosomes, as critical bioactive carriers secreted by tumor cells, play pivotal roles in shaping the immune-metabolic-vascular triad of TME and can be harnessed for delivering chemotherapeutic agents and tumor antigens [ 144 ]. The dynamic crosstalk between vasculature and immune cells reveals that immune reprogramming and vascular normalization are interdependent processes, with anti-angiogenic immunotherapy demonstrating remarkable synergistic potential in both preclinical and clinical settings [ 145 , 146 ]. However, this multi-layered regulation also explains the limited efficacy of standalone vascular-targeted therapies—the tumor vasculature acquires “immunological memory” through transcriptional reprogramming, enabling rapid reversion to immunosuppressive states post-treatment [ 123 , 146 ]. Based on these findings, we propose a theoretical framework of “Vascular-Immune Microenvironment Homeostasis Reconfiguration”: tumor vasculature is not merely a passive pathological structure but an active, dynamic platform that actively shapes the immune landscape. Effective anti-tumor strategies require simultaneous targeting of three regulatory tiers: first, disrupting the mechanical basis of angiogenesis using agents such as VEGFR inhibitors; second, intervening in the immunomodulatory functions of endothelial cells by blocking PD-L1 or IDO; and third, reprogramming the metabolic state of the vascular microenvironment by modulating lactate transporters or amino acid metabolism. Such a multi-target combination intervention strategy may fundamentally disrupt the vicious cycle between vascular abnormality and immunosuppression, offering a new direction for next-generation cancer therapy [ 123 , 127 , 147 , 148 ]. Vasculogenic mimicry: a non-endothelial vascularization program within the triad Vasculogenic mimicry (VM) represents a critical yet often overlooked vascular adaptation strategy within the neuro-vascular-immune triad. VM is defined as the formation of perfusable, vessel-like structures by highly plastic tumor cells, independent of endothelial cell involvement. This process is strongly driven by hypoxia and HIF-1α activation, which induce epithelial–mesenchymal transition and upregulate VM-associated molecules such as VE-cadherin, EphA2, and MMPs. VM lies at the intersection of the three systems emphasized in this review. First, as a vascular alternative program, VM provides sustained tumor perfusion when classical angiogenesis is inhibited, contributing to resistance against anti-angiogenic therapies. Second, VM contributes to immune suppression by creating physical barriers to immune cell infiltration and secreting immunosuppressive factors, which collectively promote an ‘immune-excluded’ phenotype. Third, VM is closely linked to stress- and hypoxia-related signaling pathways that overlap with neural regulation; for instance, sympathetic activation can enhance tumor cell plasticity and EMT, potentially facilitating VM formation. Importantly, VM frequently emerges as an adaptive escape mechanism in response to anti-angiogenic therapy, explaining part of the network compensation and therapeutic resistance highlighted in later sections. The presence of VM correlates with poor clinical prognosis across multiple cancer types, including glioblastoma, melanoma, and hepatocellular carcinoma. Targeting VM represents a promising strategy to disrupt the neuro-vascular-immune triad. Potential approaches include inhibiting VM-associated molecules (e.g., EphA2 blockers), disrupting tumor cell plasticity (e.g., EMT inhibitors), or combining anti-angiogenic agents with VM-targeting therapies to prevent compensatory vascularization. Furthermore, normalizing the hypoxic niche through oxygenation strategies or HIF-1α inhibition may simultaneously suppress both classical angiogenesis and VM, offering a dual-targeting approach within the triadic framework. Immune system dynamics and functional remodeling Regulation of TAM polarization TAMs represent one of the most abundant and biologically significant cell populations within the TME [ 149 – 151 ]. In malignant tumors, TAMs predominantly exhibit an M2-like phenotype, with their polarization state being closely linked to metabolic reprogramming. Studies demonstrate that M1 macrophages primarily rely on glycolysis for energy production, whereas M2 macrophages preferentially utilize oxidative metabolism [ 152 , 153 ]. This metabolic divergence directly determines TAM functional specialization—glycolytic reprogramming leads to metabolite accumulation in the TME that not only promotes tumor progression but also reinforces M2 polarization through signaling pathways such as Hippo [ 154 , 155 ]. Notably, this metabolic reprogramming process is profoundly influenced by extrinsic signals from the TME, including neurotransmitters. Research indicates that neurotransmitter imbalances (e.g., glutamate/GABA dysregulation) can indirectly modulate macrophage polarization by altering cellular oxidative stress levels and inflammatory responses [ 156 , 157 ]. For instance, aberrant neurotransmitter metabolism caused by altered monoamine oxidase activity exacerbates oxidative damage, thereby promoting TAM conversion to a pro-tumor phenotype [ 156 ]. Furthermore, gut microbiota-derived metabolites can modify central and peripheral neurotransmitter levels via the “gut-brain axis”, subsequently influencing TAM immunoregulatory functions [ 158 , 159 ]. Metabolic reprogramming thus serves as a pivotal mechanism shaping TAM phenotypes, with molecular and metabolic circuits collectively determining their functional states [ 160 ]. Immunosuppressive mechanisms mediated by neuro-immune interactions Neuro-immune crosstalk constitutes a critical regulatory network within the TME [ 161 , 162 ]. As pivotal components of the innate immune system, TAMs engage in bidirectional communication with the nervous system to participate in primary tumor growth, immunosuppression, angiogenesis, and metastasis [ 163 – 165 ]. Within this neuro-immune axis, TAMs exhibit dual roles as both mediators and responders in inflammatory processes. Recent studies reveal that sensory neurons can directly form neuroimmune synapses with adaptive immune cells through neuropeptide secretion, bypassing myeloid cell-mediated indirect regulation [ 166 ]. Specifically, nociceptive sensory neuron-derived CGRP binds to receptor activity-modifying protein 1 (RAMP1) on CD8 + T cells, triggering calcium influx and upregulating IL-15R/IL-18R expression to enhance cytotoxic activity. However, in the TME, this signaling pathway paradoxically induces an exhausted CD8 + T cell phenotype characterized by reduced proliferative capacity, diminished IL-2 secretion, and impaired effector function [ 167 , 168 ]. Single-cell RNA sequencing has confirmed that high RAMP1 expression in tumor-infiltrating CD8 + T cells correlates significantly with poor clinical outcomes [ 168 ]. Notably, in viral infection models, the CGRP-RAMP3 axis promotes CD8 + T cell differentiation into IFN-γ + antiviral subsets, demonstrating that neuropeptide-mediated immune regulation is highly context-dependent [ 169 ]. This microenvironment-specific duality underscores the need for precise therapeutic targeting of neuro-immune pathways in cancer. This complex interplay fosters the establishment of an immunosuppressive microenvironment, where cytokines such as IL-10 and TGF-β play critical roles in promoting tumor growth and immune evasion [ 170 , 171 ]. Mechanistically, the direct neuronal regulation of T cells involves multi-layered crosstalk: on one hand, TRPV1 + dorsal root ganglion neurons suppress the population of intestinal ROR-γ + Tregs via CGRP, linking nociceptive signaling to local immunosuppression [ 169 ]; on the other hand, skin commensal bacteria-specific CD8 + T cells utilize the CGRP-RAMP1 axis to inhibit IL-17 responses, thereby maintaining epithelial homeostasis [ 166 ]. Furthermore, neurotransmitters and neuropeptides can reinforce immunosuppressive effects by modulating the polarization state of TAMs [ 172 ]. Experimental evidence demonstrates that blocking CGRP signaling significantly restores the anti-tumor activity of CD8 + T cells and prolongs survival in melanoma models [ 168 , 173 ], providing a rationale for therapeutic strategies targeting the neuro-immune axis. Regulation of immune checkpoint molecule expression in the tripartite network The expression of immune checkpoint molecules is subject to multi-layered regulatory mechanisms. Research indicates that neuronal activity promotes immune evasion in glioma cells through α-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazolepropionic acid receptor signaling, establishing an “immune-excluded” phenotype. This neuroelectrical activity-mediated mechanism is closely associated with the efficacy of ICIs [ 67 ]. Several key immune checkpoint molecules are directly modulated by neural inputs. PD-L1 expression on tumor cells and antigen-presenting cells can be upregulated through β-adrenergic signaling, which activates the MAPK pathway to inhibit MHC-I expression and promote PD-L1 translation [ 174 ]. Chronic sympathetic activation promotes the development of CD8⁺ T cell exhaustion via the β-AR signaling pathway. This process is often accompanied by high Programmed cell Death protein 1 (PD-1) expression and leads to impaired T cell function [ 175 , 176 ]. Furthermore, both NE and acetylcholine can regulate immune cell function in the TME through their respective receptor-mediated signaling pathways (e.g., β-AR, M3R), affecting the expression of immune checkpoints such as PD-1, PD-L1, and LAG-3 on tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes [ 177 – 179 ]. Notably, β2-AR activation in regulatory T cells increases their suppressive functions by upregulating CTLA-4 expression [ 179 ]. Additionally, β2-AR signaling in myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) enhances their immunosuppressive capacity, as evidenced by the resulting significant inhibition of T cell proliferation by wild-type MDSCs when compared to β2-AR–/– MDSCs [ 180 ]. However, these mechanisms require further investigation within the context of the tripartite network. Concurrently, sustained IFN-γ stimulation induces significant gene expression alterations and immunophenotypic shifts in vascular endothelial cells. This vascular signaling can modulate the expression of various immunoregulatory molecules, including immune checkpoints [ 181 ]. Within the TME, hypoxic conditions particularly induce the expression of multiple immunosuppressive factors and immune checkpoint molecules—including VEGF—via the HIF-1 signaling pathway, thereby suppressing both innate and adaptive immunity [ 182 ]. HIF-1α directly binds to the PD-L1 promoter under hypoxia, creating a molecular link between oxygen sensing and immune evasion. VEGF signaling from the vascular component also contributes to PD-L1 upregulation through the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway [ 183 ]. Neuro-immune-vascular interactions involve a complex bidirectional communication network encompassing the autonomic nervous system, immune cells, and vascular endothelium. These interactions are regulated by key signaling pathways such as NF-κB, MAPK, mTOR, STAT3, and cAMP [ 184 ]. This tripartite regulatory network constitutes a sophisticated bidirectional system that significantly influences tumor development, metastasis, patient immune status, and treatment response [ 162 ]. Understanding these mechanisms provides a theoretical foundation for developing novel combination strategies—such as co-administering α-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazolepropionic acid receptor antagonists with ICIs [ 67 ], or integrating targeting of neuro-immune cell interactions with advanced technologies like brain–machine interfaces and nano-delivery systems [ 162 ]. These insights offer new avenues to overcome challenges associated with current immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy, including suboptimal response rates. Synergistic integration and vicious cycle of the neuro-vascular-immune triad HIF-1α-mediated orchestration of the triadic network The nervous, vascular, and immune systems form a dynamic positive-feedback loop within the TME, collectively driving tumor progression (Fig. 2 ). The nervous system directly promotes angiogenesis through the release of neurotrophic factors and neuropeptides [ 185 , 186 ]. However, newly formed tumor vessels are often structurally disorganized and functionally abnormal. Their hypoperfusive characteristics directly lead to tissue hypoxia—a central driving factor in the TME [ 140 , 187 ]. Hypoxia, in turn, activates the core transcription factor HIF-1α, positioning it as a critical environmental sensor and master regulator that integrates signals across all three systems. Activation of HIF-1α establishes a core positive-feedback loop: hypoxia stabilizes HIF-1α, which further upregulates factors such as VEGF, exacerbating vascular abnormality [ 188 , 189 ]. These aberrant vessels not only impede the infiltration of cytotoxic T cells by acting as a physical barrier but also upregulate immune checkpoint molecules like PD-L1 on endothelial and tumor cells through HIF-1α-dependent mechanisms, directly suppressing T-cell function [ 190 – 192 ]. Moreover, both neurotrophic factors and immune-derived inflammatory cytokines can further enhance HIF-1α activity via pathways such as NF-κB and cAMP/PKA, forming an amplification circuit described as the “neuro/immune–HIF-1α–vascular abnormality” axis [ 184 , 193 , 194 ]. HIF-1α triggers multiple interconnected positive feedback loops through multidirectional regulatory signals, driving the three systems to synergistically promote tumor progression. Firstly, HIF-1α significantly upregulates the expression of pro-angiogenic factors such as VEGFA and FGF2, which further exacerbates vascular structural disorders and functional abnormalities, forming a positive cycle of “hypoxia-HIF-1α-angiogenesis-aggravated hypoxia”. In this process, tumor-associated endothelial cells respond to pro-angiogenic factors by expressing adhesion molecules at low levels, directly hindering the effective infiltration of immune cells into the tumor site; meanwhile, these endothelial cells directly inhibit the activation and cytotoxic function of CD8⁺ T cells by expressing inhibitory molecules such as PD-L1 and IDO, remodeling the immunosuppressive microenvironment. Additionally, TAMs not only interact with tumor-associated endothelial cells and immunosuppressive cells to promote tumor growth but also secrete cytokines including TGF-β, IL-10, and CCL2. These cytokines not only enhance neuronal excitability but also induce the directional growth of nerve axons into tumor tissues, further reinforcing the neuro-vascular-immune vicious cycle. For instance, under HIF-1α drive, TAMs release chemokines such as CCL2 and CXCL12, which attract sympathetic nerve fibers toward the tumor core [ 193 , 195 ]. These infiltrating nerve fibers release neurotransmitters including NE, activating the β2-AR signaling pathway. This leads to direct inhibition of CD8 + T-cell function and promotes the recruitment of MDSCs, thereby exacerbating immune escape [ 184 , 186 ]. Beyond transcriptional regulation, emerging evidence suggests that such tripartite interactions are also epigenetically regulated. DNA methylation and histone modifications dynamically control the expression of genes involved in neural innervation, angiogenesis, and immune modulation. Histone acetyltransferases, including p300, CBP, and TIP60, promote HIF-1α downstream gene transcription via acetylation of histones H3 and H4 [ 196 ]. Furthermore, HDAC family members can either promote or inhibit HIF-1α transcriptional activity through multiple regulatory mechanisms [ 196 ]. Beyond HIF-1α-centric mechanisms, non-coding RNAs represent another layer of regulation. As discussed earlier, neuron-derived exosomes shuttle miR-31a to sustain tumor growth, and tumor-derived exosomes deliver miRNAs that reprogram immune cell function [ 44 ]. Additionally, neurotransmitters themselves can induce epigenetic changes [ 197 ]; for example, β-adrenergic signaling may activate HDACs that alter chromatin accessibility in immune cells, promoting an immunosuppressive phenotype [ 198 , 199 ]. While direct evidence linking epigenetic regulation to the complete neuro-vascular-immune triad remains limited, these mechanisms represent promising therapeutic targets, as epigenetic drugs may sensitize tumors to combination therapies by reversing the immunosuppressive epigenetic landscape. In summary, HIF-1α functions as a central “molecular hub” within this network: it directly responds to hypoxic signals resulting from vascular abnormalities while also being activated by immune inflammatory factors and neurotransmitters, thereby coordinately driving angiogenesis, immunosuppression, and neural remodeling. This mechanism tightly couples the nervous, vascular, and immune systems, forming a synergistic network that promotes tumor progression and is resistant to disruption by single-target strategies. The potential role of cancer stem cells in triadic regulation Cancer stem cells (CSCs) are widely acknowledged for their key involvement in tumor initiation, metastasis, and disease recurrence. Emerging evidence suggests they may also play a critical role in reshaping the neuro-vascular-immune triad. CSCs demonstrate immunomodulatory potential and mount immunotherapy resistance through multiple mechanisms [ 200 ]. Notably, CSCs can secrete neurotrophic factors that promote tumor innervation [ 201 ], express elevated HIF-1α under hypoxia to drive angiogenesis and neurogenesis [ 202 , 203 ], and recruit immunosuppressive cell populations through cytokine secretion [ 46 , 204 ]. Conversely, neural signals can maintain CSC stemness through deterministic​ neurotransmitter-mediated signaling, thereby​ creating a positive feedback loop [ 205 – 207 ]. While direct evidence remains limited, this CSC-triad axis represents a compelling hypothesis warranting further investigation. Targeting CSC-specific pathways (e.g., Notch, Wnt, Hedgehog) may offer a strategy to disrupt the triadic crosstalk and overcome treatment resistance. Advances in diagnostic and therapeutic strategy research Development of biomarkers based on the neuro-vascular-immune triad The emergence of combination therapies targeting the neuro-vascular-immune triad has driven increased focus on developing biomarkers to guide targeted treatment selection [ 208 ]. Current biomarkers relying on single-system indicators show limited predictive power, largely because they fail to capture the synergistic or antagonistic effects arising from the interactions among these three systems [ 209 – 211 ]. Future breakthroughs will require a shift from “single-parameter biomarkers” to “multidimensional composite biomarkers.” In spatial omics, digital pathology and spatial transcriptomics can be used to quantitatively analyze the spatial co-localization relationships within tumor regions—such as nerve fiber density (neural), vascular maturity index (vascular), and the cytotoxic T cell/regulatory T cell ratio (immune)—to construct a “tripartite niche index” predictive of therapeutic resistance [ 212 , 213 ]. This integrated approach has proven effective in revealing coordinated features of immune cell polarity in the TME [ 214 ]. In liquid biopsy, combined detection of circulating catecholamines (neural), angiopoietin-like protein 2 (vascular), and PD-L1 + exosomes (immune) enables dynamic monitoring of the network’s overall response during combination therapy. This strategy, similar to approaches used in cerebrovascular disease research to validate inflammatory biomarker networks [ 215 ], can provide real-time evidence for adjusting treatment strategies. This network-based biomarker approach aims to upgrade biomarkers from “road signs” predicting response to a single drug to “dynamic operational maps” guiding multi-system synergistic intervention. Targeted intervention strategies The clinical value of emerging therapeutic technologies lies primarily in their role as “network disruptors” capable of multi-target regulation, enabling precise intervention at key synergistic nodes of the neuro-vascular-immune triad (Fig. 3 ). This strategy overcomes the limitations of traditional single-target approaches focused solely on cancer cells, achieving system-level therapeutic effects through spatiotemporally specific modulation [ 162 , 216 ]. In the field of nanotechnology, smart responsive nanoparticles enable precise drug delivery based on TME characteristics, with efficacy validated across multiple tumor models [ 217 – 219 ]. Innovative designs include hierarchical drug-loading structures—an outer shell encapsulating β-AR antagonists to block neural signaling, and a core co-loaded with VEGFR2 and CSF-1R inhibitors. This multimodal synergistic approach simultaneously disrupts neuro-vascular coupling and reverses the immunosuppressive microenvironment [ 124 , 220 , 221 ]. Research confirms that such combinatorial strategies effectively dismantle the positive feedback loops sustaining the tumor ecosystem [ 222 ]. In cell therapy, next-generation CAR-T technology has evolved beyond simply targeting tumor antigens to create “smart effector cells” capable of remodeling the microenvironment [ 223 ]. Genetic engineering allows CAR-T cells to secrete NE-degrading enzymes or express VEGF trap receptors, concurrently blocking both the “neuro-immune” and “vascular-immune” suppression axes [ 172 ]. This multi-pathway intervention significantly enhances tumor infiltration and persistence of effector cells. The core mechanism of these innovative therapies lies in leveraging molecular precision to achieve triple synergy—neuromodulation, vascular normalization, and immune reprogramming—thereby systematically dismantling the tumor’s adaptive ecosystem. Optimization and challenges of combination therapies Building upon an in-depth understanding of the neuro-vascular-immune triad interactions, modern combination therapies have progressed from simple drug combinations to precise intervention strategies based on the principle of “synchronous disruption of network nodes” (Fig. 3 ) [ 222 , 224 ]. The core of this strategy is to maximize therapeutic effects through multi-target synergy, as exemplified by triple-combination regimens of ICIs, anti-angiogenic drugs, and β-blockers [ 225 , 226 ]. The scientific rationale for this combination is reflected in three levels of synergy: first, anti-angiogenic drugs significantly improve the tumor infiltration capacity of immune cells by promoting tumor vascular normalization [ 227 ], a mechanism successfully validated in landmark trials like IMbrave150 (atezolizumab + bevacizumab) and IMpower150, which demonstrated significant overall survival benefits in HCC and NSCLC, respectively [ 228 , 229 ]; second, β-blockers effectively block catecholaminergic neural signaling triggered by immune activation, preventing stress responses from counteracting immunotherapeutic efficacy [ 222 , 230 ]; and finally, ICIs fully exert their immune-activating effects within the optimized microenvironment [ 231 , 232 ]. Furthermore, cutting-edge technologies such as smart nanodrug delivery systems (enabling cooperative delivery of multiple agents) and functionally enhanced CAR-T cells (engineered to secrete NE-degrading enzymes or express VEGF decoy receptors) facilitate multi-level, coordinated attacks on the network, ultimately aiming to reverse immunosuppression and suppress tumor progression. A key current challenge lies in determining the optimal spatiotemporal sequence of drug administration [ 233 , 234 ], which requires validation through dynamic biomarker monitoring [ 235 , 236 ]. Furthermore, overlapping toxicities pose a significant hurdle; combining vasodilators, neural modulators, and immune activators may exacerbate cardiovascular or autoimmune adverse events, necessitating careful dose optimization. It is also critical to address patient heterogeneity, as not all tumors exhibit the same degree of innervation or vascular abnormality. This highlights the urgent need for predictive biomarkers to stratify responders. Finally, resistance mechanisms in combination therapy often manifest as compensatory reprogramming of the network. When one pathway is inhibited, compensatory activation may occur in other related pathways [ 226 , 237 ]. This challenge manifests across three interrelated dimensions. First is the organ-specific network heterogeneity. Different tumor types exhibit significant heterogeneity in neuro-immune-vascular interactions, stemming from unique bidirectional regulatory networks within their respective TMEs [ 15 , 162 ]. For instance, studies in glioma have shown that neuron-tumor interactions drive intratumoral heterogeneity via signaling pathways like NF-κB, MAPK, and mTOR [ 184 , 239 ], while other solid tumors may utilize distinct dominant pathways shaped by interactions between the peripheral nervous system and the TME [ 240 , 241 ]. The molecular basis of such tissue-specific crosstalk remains poorly understood, particularly how neural activity coordinates vascular remodeling and immunosuppression through hub molecules like STAT3 and cAMP [ 184 , 242 ]. Second is the technical bottleneck in dynamic monitoring. Current technologies struggle to resolve the real-time interactions among these three systems. On one hand, deciphering the dynamically coupled relationships between neural activity, angiogenesis, and spatiotemporal immune cell distribution requires advanced tools like brain-computer interfaces [ 162 , 243 ]. On the other hand, current organoid models cannot fully recapitulate the complex networked connections among neural, vascular, and immune cells within the TME, especially the intercellular communication mediated by tumor microtubes [ 244 , 245 ]. This technological gap impedes precise assessment of how targeted interventions affect network dynamics [ 32 , 246 ]. Third is the therapy resistance mediated by multi-mechanism synergy. Vascular abnormalities, nerve-mediated immunosuppression, and metabolic reprogramming collectively drive therapy resistance by forming a compensatory network [ 247 , 248 ]. Research indicates that the autonomic nervous system can tilt the balance between pro-tumor inflammation and anti-cancer immunity, enabling immune escape [ 249 ]. Of particular clinical significance, the degree of intratumoral innervation significantly correlates with therapeutic prognosis, suggesting neuro-immune crosstalk is a key node in resistance mechanisms [ 184 , 250 ]. Therefore, key breakthroughs in future research depend on systematic and coordinated progress across three interconnected fronts: technology, platform development, and therapeutic strategy. First, there is a critical need to advance high-resolution spatial multi-omics technologies. These tools are essential for precisely mapping the spatial distribution features of neuro-immune-vascular circuits across different tumor types, thereby revealing the heterogeneity of key regulatory nodes at single-cell and even subcellular resolution [ 184 , 251 ]. Second, the field must develop integrated research platforms that combine nanobiosensors, organoid-on-a-chip systems, and high-resolution real-time dynamic imaging. Such platforms would enable continuous, precise observation of the interactions between neural activity, vascular dynamics, and immune responses, providing the technical foundation for dynamically assessing network function [ 252 ]. Building on these capabilities, a fundamental innovation in therapeutic strategy design is urgently needed. This involves promoting the development of multi-system synergistic targeted therapies. Examples include combining ICIs with specific neural-signaling modulators, with the aim of simultaneously intervening at multiple key network nodes to effectively disrupt the tumor’s compensatory survival network [ 162 , 244 , 253 ]. Achieving this research framework necessitates deep integration and collaboration across multiple disciplines, including neuroscience, immunology, cancer biology, and bioengineering [ 35 , 249 ]. Only by deciphering the hierarchical regulatory principles of the neuro-vascular-immune triad can we establish a solid theoretical foundation for the next generation of precision oncology paradigms and pioneer novel therapeutic avenues [ 254 ]. Summary and core conclusions This review provides a systematic analysis of the complex interactions within the neuro-vascular-immune triad in the TME and its central role in cancer progression. Synthesizing current evidence, we posit that the neuro-vascular-immune triad operates not merely as a passive backdrop but as a dynamic, self-reinforcing regulatory hub that dictates tumor aggressiveness. In this network, neural inputs function as upstream drivers, actively remodeling the microenvironment via neurotransmitter-mediated signaling and neurotrophic factor secretion to initiate invasion and metastasis. Concurrently, vascular abnormalities create a hypoxic-permissive niche that not only sustains metabolic demand but also enforces immune exclusion through HIF-1α-dependent pathways and aberrant perfusion. Ultimately, immune plasticity within this context serves as the final checkpoint, where the balance between immunosurveillance and suppression determines therapeutic response and disease progression. Crucially, dismantling this interdependent axis requires moving beyond single-modality approaches; instead, the simultaneous disruption of neural signaling, vascular normalization, and immune reactivation represents a strategic imperative to overcome therapy resistance and durably suppress tumor progression. A deeper understanding of this tripartite network provides novel insights and targets for overcoming current limitations in cancer therapy. Research demonstrates that the peripheral nervous system contributes to tumor progression through a dual mechanism: directly activating tumor cell proliferation and invasion programs via the secretion of neurotrophic factors and neurotransmitters [ 48 , 255 ], and participating in the construction of tumor innervation networks by inducing axonogenesis—a process of neural remodeling significantly correlated with malignant progression [ 241 , 256 ]. Notably, these neurogenic signals can profoundly influence tumor cell evolution through epigenetic modifications and metabolic reprogramming [ 15 , 32 ]. The vascular system functions as a dynamic hub within this network: it drives pathological angiogenesis through neuro-vascular coupling mechanisms [ 47 ], while its structural and functional abnormalities directly contribute to the formation of an immunosuppressive “cold” microenvironment [ 124 , 145 ]. At the molecular level, hypoxia-inducible factors such as HIF-1α play a central regulatory role in sustaining the abnormal vascular network [ 123 , 140 ]. Furthermore, the topological relationship between vascular spatial distribution and nerve fiber density is not merely coincidental [ 257 , 258 ]. This spatial co-alignment enables neuro-vascular units to function as integrated ‘conduits,’ simultaneously delivering metabolic supplies and trophic signals, thereby creating specialized niches within the TME. This structural interdependence provides a mechanistic framework for understanding their functional synergy. Recognizing this structure-function coupling shifts our perspective from viewing nerves and vessels as independent entities to seeing them as a unified regulatory axis, implying that therapeutic strategies must target their intersection to effectively disrupt tumor progression. Immunoregulation within this network exhibits remarkable plasticity. The functional polarization of myeloid-derived immune cells is dually regulated by both neurotransmitters and vasoactive factors [ 240 , 259 ]. It is particularly noteworthy that sympathetic nerves can induce the upregulation of immune checkpoint molecules such as PD-L1 through β-AR signaling pathways [ 161 , 162 ]. This neuro-immune interaction provides a novel mechanistic framework for understanding resistance to immunotherapy [ 131 , 251 ]. From a translational medicine perspective, deciphering this network yields multiple breakthroughs: Key network nodes identified via multi-omics data can serve as integrative biomarkers for predicting responses to immunotherapy [ 260 ]; combination strategies targeting innervation show synergistic effects in preclinical models [ 34 , 145 ]; and responsive nanocarrier technology offers new tools for achieving spatiotemporally specific modulation of the neuro-vascular-immune system [ 144 , 261 ]. However, three major challenges require urgent resolution: tumor-type-specific network heterogeneity [ 262 ], technical limitations in dynamically monitoring the microenvironment [ 241 ], and therapy-induced compensatory network remodeling [ 263 ]. Future research directions should focus on the following priorities: (1) utilizing spatial transcriptomics and mass cytometry to decipher the heterogeneous topological architecture of the network [ 264 ]; (2) developing dynamic organoid models that integrate neuronal, vascular, and immune components [ 265 ]; and (3) designing sequential therapeutic strategies targeting critical network nodes to circumvent compensatory mechanisms [ 131 ]. Through interdisciplinary innovation and clinical translation research, a deeper understanding of the TME ecosystem will drive the establishment of next-generation precision therapy paradigms.
