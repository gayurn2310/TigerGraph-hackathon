Mandarin speech‐based early detection of SCD: a feature‐fusion residual network method

Abstract INTRODUCTION Alzheimer's disease (AD) poses a global health challenge. Early intervention during the stage of subjective cognitive decline (SCD) – a potential window for delaying disease progression – is crucial. This study aims to assess an exploratory speech‐based model for rapid SCD screening. METHOD This study included 459 participants, comprising individuals with AD, mild cognitive impairment (MCI), SCD, and normal controls. We used Pic‐Talk clips and Mandarin speech with residual network features for SCD screening. RESULTS In this cross‐sectional study, our model achieved high performance with accuracy, recall, precision, F1, and area under the curve of 81.77 ± 2.78%, 80.53 ± 2.64%, 82.27 ± 2.38%, 81.39 ± 1.85%, and 82.85 ± 2.01%, respectively, outperforming other speech models. DISCUSSION This non‐invasive exploratory approach to SCD assessment shows potential, revealing acoustic differences at the group level between SCD and other diagnostic groups. It is expected that the future integration of biomarkers will enhance the model's accuracy and expand its applicability.

1 INTRODUCTION As the global aging process accelerates, Alzheimer's disease (AD) has emerged as a major health challenge among older adults. 1 The number of people living with dementia is projected to increase from 55 million in 2019 to 139 million by 2050, with the global economic burden rising from $1.3 trillion to $2.8 trillion by 2030. 2 AD is characterized by amyloid beta (Aβ) plaque deposition and tau pathology, leading to progressive neuronal loss; by the stage of mild dementia, approximately 40% of neurons have already degenerated, rendering disease progression largely irreversible. 3 , 4 , 5 Subjective cognitive decline (SCD) refers to a self‐experienced decline in cognitive capacity that is not yet detectable by standardized neuropsychological testing and does not meet criteria for mild cognitive impairment (MCI) or dementia. 6 SCD affects a substantial proportion of cognitively unimpaired older adults, with an estimated prevalence of 23.8% to 25.6%. 7 Although annual conversion rates to MCI (∼7%) or AD dementia (∼2.3%) appear modest, 1 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 long‐term studies indicate substantial cumulative risk: Up to 61.36% of individuals with SCD progress to MCI or AD over 7 years, representing a 4.5‐fold higher risk than individuals without SCD. 10 , 12 These findings highlight the importance of early identification of SCD in the detection of emerging cognitive vulnerability. At present, the identification of SCD largely relies on exclusion‐based clinical assessments and individuals’ subjective reports, a process that is time‐consuming, resource‐intensive, and highly dependent on clinician expertise. 13 , 14 , 15 Although electroencephalogram‐ and magnetic resonance imaging‐based approaches can moderately differentiate SCD from cognitively normal individuals, their reliance on expensive equipment and research‐oriented protocols restricts practical use in large‐scale screening. 16 , 17 , 18 Therefore, there is a clear need to develop simpler, scalable, and non‐invasive behavioral or cognitive screening tools, potentially supported by artificial intelligence (AI), for population‐level application. Language, particularly spontaneous speech, has emerged as a non‐invasive and ecologically valid indicator of cognitive function. 19 Linguistic changes associated with cognitive decline follow a progressive pattern, with early semantic alterations preceding syntactic and discourse‐level impairments as disease advances from MCI to AD. 20 , 21 Accordingly, speech‐based studies using tasks such as the Cookie Theft picture description, in combination with machine learning or deep learning models, have achieved good classification performance in MCI and AD, demonstrating the sensitivity of speech markers once objective impairment is present. 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 In contrast, speech‐based approaches have rarely been applied to SCD. Emerging evidence indicates that individuals with SCD already exhibit subtle but measurable alterations in spontaneous speech, primarily manifested as reduced semantic specificity and lower information density, 26 suggesting that semantic changes may represent the earliest and most sensitive linguistic markers of cognitive decline. However, progress in this field remains constrained by multiple factors, including the exclusion‐based nature of SCD diagnosis, the limited availability of well‐characterized datasets, and the predominance of English‐language studies, with Mandarin Chinese populations remaining markedly underrepresented. 27 Within this continuum framework, capturing and quantifying subtle language‐level changes in naturalistic settings remains a key challenge. Although linguistic impairments originate at the cognitive and language‐processing levels, spontaneous speech – shaped by tightly coupled cognitive, linguistic, and motor systems – provides an ecologically valid behavioral screening signal. Early speech‐based studies relied on handcrafted acoustic and prosodic features, but recent work has shifted toward end‐to‐end and self‐supervised pre‐trained speech models, such as wav2vec 2.0 and HuBERT. These models learn high‐level representations directly from raw audio and achieve improved performance in dementia‐related tasks without explicit linguistic annotations. 23 , 24 , 25 Architectures such as Emphasized Channel Attention, Propagation and Aggregation Time Delay Neural Network (ECAPA‐TDNN) and Conformer further enhance the modeling of speaker‐level abnormalities and long‐range temporal dependencies in pathological speech. 28 , 29 , 30 Despite these advances, most speech‐based approaches have focused on MCI or AD and have been developed primarily using English‐language datasets. 31 Speech‐based screening for SCD – particularly in Mandarin‐speaking populations – remains underexplored, partly due to the exclusion‐based nature of SCD diagnosis and the scarcity of high‐quality labeled data. To address the limitations, we propose a Picture Description Dialogue (Pic‐Talk)‐based residual framework for early‐stage SCD screening, highlighting the potential of task‐driven, data‐efficient, and interpretable speech‐based approaches as scalable and cost‐effective tools for the early detection of cognitive decline. 2 METHODS 2.1 Study participants This study recruited a total of 586 participants from May 2021 to October 2023. The participants ranged in age from 45 to 98 years, with an equal gender ratio. All participants underwent cognitive assessments by cognitive evaluators. Neurology deputy chief physicians and above strictly adhered to the National Institute on Aging‐Alzheimer's Association (NIA‐AA) diagnostic criteria 32 for diagnosing AD and MCI, as well as the International Working Group on Subjective Cognitive Decline 10 for diagnosing SCD. Diagnoses were defined as follows. SCD was diagnosed when participants reported a persistent self‐experienced decline in memory while showing normal objective performance across cognitive domains and preserved daily functioning, operationalized as test scores within 1.5 standard deviations of age‐ and education‐adjusted norms and a normal activities of daily living (ADL) score. MCI was diagnosed based on NIA‐AA criteria, 32 requiring objective impairment in one or more cognitive domains with largely preserved functional independence. AD was diagnosed as probable AD dementia per NIA‐AA criteria, 32 characterized by significant cognitive decline in at least two domains with clear interference in daily living. This study strictly adheres to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and has been reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Guangdong Medical University (Ethical Approval Number: KT2022‐120‐01). All research procedures comply with national laws, regulations, and ethical standards. All participants, or their legal representatives (for those with cognitive impairments), voluntarily participated in this study and signed written informed consent forms. Participants have the right to withdraw unconditionally at any stage of the study without affecting their subsequent medical services. All personal information and clinical data are strictly confidential and anonymized by means of coding. The data will only be used for the purpose of this study and will not be used for other purposes without the consent of participants. RESEARCH IN CONTEXT Systematic review : We systematically reviewed the literature on SCD and AD screening, with a particular focus on AI‐based speech recognition approaches. Prior studies largely concentrated on MCI and AD, where AI‐driven speech analysis has shown promising diagnostic value by capturing changes in speech fluency, rhythm, and semantic coherence. Reported accuracies of 70% to 82% have been achieved using tasks such as Cookie Theft picture description or multimodal linguistic analysis. However, most existing datasets are in English, with limited studies applying AI‐based speech recognition to SCD, especially in Mandarin‐speaking populations. Furthermore, current SCD diagnosis is still exclusion‐based, resource‐intensive, and lacks sensitive, scalable tools. This highlights a major gap in leveraging AI‐driven speech biomarkers for ultra‐early detection of cognitive decline. Interpretation : Our study introduces a Mandarin speech‐based residual network fusion model for rapid SCD screening. By combining AI‐driven local acoustic feature extraction (phoneme clarity, speech rhythm) with global semantic coherence analysis, the model achieved 81.77% accuracy, outperforming traditional scales and existing speech models. Importantly, this tool can complete screening within only a few minutes, making it suitable for large‐scale community or clinical applications. These results demonstrate that AI‐powered speech recognition can sensitively capture subtle language network dysfunction in SCD, while offering a fast, non‐invasive, and scalable solution for early AD detection. The findings support the clinical feasibility of AI voice analytics as a digital biomarker that complements conventional neuropsychological testing and imaging. Future directions : Future research should validate the prognostic value of AI speech biomarkers in longitudinal cohorts, particularly their ability to predict SCD‐to‐MCI/AD conversion. Integrating AI speech recognition with biological markers (e.g., Aβ PET/CT, plasma p‐tau) could enhance specificity and interpretability. Expanding to diverse languages, dialects, and recording conditions will ensure generalizability. Moreover, developing multimodal AI frameworks that integrate speech, wearable‐derived digital phenotypes, and cognitive scales could yield dynamic, cost‐effective, and scalable screening platforms for community and clinical use. 2.2 Inclusion and exclusion criteria Inclusion criteria: adults aged ≥18 years; although the study primarily targeted individuals aged 18 to 80 years, no upper age limit was imposed, and a small number of participants older than 80 years were therefore included, all of whom met the aforementioned diagnostic criteria for AD dementia, MCI, or SCD. Exclusion criteria: other related organic lesions of the brain nerves and other brain dysfunction, such as frontotemporal dementia, Lewy body dementia, cerebrovascular disease, Parkinson's disease, depression, hearing impairment, and other diseases that may affect the evaluation and speech. 2.3 Data acquisition This study employed the following neuropsychological assessment tools to systematically evaluate the cognitive functions of the participants, specifically: (1) the Montreal Cognitive Assessment Scale (MoCA‐B) 33 as the core screening tool, used to quickly assess overall cognitive function; (2) the Shape Matching Test (STT‐A and B) to evaluate executive function; (3) Boston Naming Test (BNT) and Verbal Fluency Test to assess language ability; (4) Auditory Verbal Learning Test (AVLT) to assess memory ability; (5) ADL to assess daily living activities; (6) Subjective Cognitive Decline Questionnaire (SCD‐Q) to understand participants ‘self‐assessment of their cognitive decline; (7) in Pic‐Talk, 34 , 35 the “Cookie Theft” task was used to assess language ability and record speech. The process of this study begins with the screening of subjects, where only those who meet the inclusion criteria will proceed to complete a comprehensive set of cognitive function tests and be categorized into AD, SCD, MCI, or normal control (NC) groups based on the test results. Subsequently, subjects in these groups undergo assessments such as the ADL, MoCA, SCD‐Q, and a picture description task. The collected voice audio, after processing, is divided into training and testing groups for model training and performance evaluation, with a screening model ultimately being constructed to achieve early identification and diagnosis of cognitive impairments (Figure 1 ). This chart shows the steps from audio data collection to processing and segmentation, including tone separation, sampling rate adjustment, and segmentation processing, in order to prepare the data for subsequent analysis. 2.4 Acoustic feature extraction and configuration In this study, log Mel filterbank (FBank) features were adopted as the sole input representation for the proposed model. Although Mel‐spectrograms and Mel‐Frequency Cepstral Coefficients (MFCCs) are mentioned in related work for comparison, all experiments reported in this paper were conducted using log Mel filterbank features only in order to ensure consistency and reproducibility. Speech signals were resampled to 16,000 Hz and processed using Kaldi‐style FBank extraction. Specifically, a short‐time Fourier transform was applied with a window length of 25 ms (corresponding to n_fft = 400) and a hop size of 10 ms (hop_length = 160). A total of 80 Mel filter banks (n_mels = 80) were used to cover the frequency range of 0 to 8 kHz. Logarithmic compression was inherently applied, as the Kaldi FBank pipeline outputs log‐compressed Mel filterbank energies by default. After feature extraction, utterance‐level mean normalization was performed along the temporal dimension to reduce inter‐speaker variability, formulated as X = X − mean ( X , axis = 1 ) where X denotes the time–frequency feature matrix. The resulting input representation is a two‐dimensional time–frequency feature map of size T ×80 T , where T depends on the duration of the speech segment. This standardized acoustic representation serves as the input to the subsequent feature fusion and network architecture modules. 2.5 Overall framework Despite significant progress in voice‐based AD screening research, existing models still face two major challenges. First, the separation and extraction of local acoustic features (such as phoneme‐level pronunciation ambiguity) from global language patterns (such as semantic coherence degradation) can lead to the loss of critical pathological information. Second, single‐scale temporal modeling struggles to capture the dynamic cognitive decline characteristics across time in AD patients' speech (such as the correlation between long‐term semantic repetition and short‐term pronunciation abnormalities). 36 To address these issues, this study proposes a dual‐module collaborative speech feature fusion framework (Figure 3 ). This framework optimizes the representation of AD‐related pathological speech patterns through a hierarchical fusion mechanism of local and global features, enhancing the audio's discriminative ability and improving the robustness of audio embedding. 36 , 37 FIGURE 3 Fusion framework of speech features for Alzheimer's disease. The figure shows the entire processing flow of the model from speech input to output, including local feature extraction, local feature fusion, global feature fusion, and final classification output. The model extracts features through multiple basic modules, then integrates the features through block fusion and global fusion, and finally generates the final output through pooling layers, fully connected layers, and Softmax activation functions. To improve the multi‐scale representation ability of the model, this module achieves this goal by expanding the range of receptive fields. In each residual block, it uses a hierarchical class‐residual connection to extract multi‐dimensional features 38 , 39 in the channel dimension. 2.5.1 Local feature fusion In the local feature fusion process, multiple “BasicBlocks” (which serve as units for extracting local features, such as phonemes and short‐term speech features) are utilized. By leveraging a multi‐layer convolutional module, these blocks extract fine‐grained acoustic information from speech, including phoneme clarity, formant positions, and fundamental frequency changes, through different scales of receptive fields, either sequentially or in parallel. Additionally, multi‐level residual connections and non‐linear activation mechanisms are introduced to enhance the stability and representativeness of the features. Following this, “Block_Fusion” integrates the local features extracted by the BasicBlocks, focusing on local pathological information such as phoneme‐level ambiguity and short‐term speech anomalies, to effectively detect minor speech abnormalities and accurately capture these local pathological features. 2.5.2 Global feature fusion After passing through Step N, the speech information enters the Fusion stage of the Global Feature Fusion module. To achieve good integration of features at different levels, the Global Feature Fusion module combines a contextual attention mechanism with an adaptive feature adjustment strategy. This approach enhances the integration of lower‐level acoustic representations through bottom‐up multi‐scale feature fusion and captures long‐term dependencies and contextual structural information in the speech signal rather than semantic content. Using the attention guidance mechanism, the module highlights global acoustic patterns related to AD, such as long‐range temporal dependencies and contextual acoustic structures, from a higher‐dimensional feature space. This effectively compensates for the information loss caused by the locality of local features, improves the recognition of dynamic cognitive changes in speech sequences, and integrates global acoustic dependency features across time dimensions. The proposed method is purely based on acoustic features and does not rely on Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) or textual transcription. The fused features then go through a “Pooling layer (compressing feature dimensions) + Fully connected layer (mapping features) + Softmax activation (output classification probabilities)” process, ultimately leading to the output for screening results (e.g., AD positive/negative judgment). 2.5.3 Layer‐wise network architecture and parameter configuration We adopt a residual convolutional neural network with adaptive multi‐level feature fusion. The network consists of four residual stages with a block configuration of [3, 4, 6, 3][3, 4, 6, 3][3, 4, 6, 3]. The initial number of channels is set to “channels = 32,” and the channel width is progressively increased by a factor of 2 at each stage (32, 64, 128, and 256). After downsampling and feature fusion, the maximum channel dimension reaches 512. Each residual block uses an expansion ratio of 2, with a base width of 32 and a scale parameter of 2. The primary activation function is a bounded Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) implemented as Hardtanh (0, 20), while the Adaptive Feature Fusion (AFF) module employs SiLU and tanh activations to enable dynamic weighting across feature scales. Temporal Statistics Pooling (TSTP) is applied to aggregate frame‐level features, followed by a projection layer producing a 192‐dimensional embedding. 2.6 Confirmation and performance indicators To evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed speech analysis framework in AD and related cognitive state classification tasks, we designed a variety of comparative experiments that covered different feature extraction methods and model structures. All experiments were conducted at a uniform audio sampling rate of 16,000 Hz and used a five‐fold cross‐validation strategy to ensure the robustness and reproducibility of the results. Key evaluation metrics included overall classification accuracy (ACC), recall (Recall), precision (Precision), F1 score (F1 Score), and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). The experimental results are summarized in Table S2 . Specifically, we designed six variant models for comparison: the baseline Res2Net architecture; +Grouped Residuals: adding only the multi‐scale grouped residual structure; +Local Feature Fusion: adding only the local feature fusion module (Block_diffAFF); +Global Feature Fusion: adding only the Global Feature Fusion module (AFF); Local + Global Fusion: incorporating both Local and Global Feature Fusion modules without grouped residuals; Full Model: combining grouped residuals with both Local and Global Feature Fusion modules. As shown by the ablation results, the full model achieves the highest accuracy of 81.77% on the four‐class classification task, demonstrating the effectiveness of the synergistic design of the multi‐scale grouped residual structure and the local–global feature fusion modules. Notably, removing the local feature fusion module (without [w/o] Local Feature Fusion) has the most significant impact on performance, resulting in a 7.61% drop in accuracy. This indicates that local feature fusion (Block_diffAFF) plays a critical role in capturing fine‐grained speech characteristics. An interesting phenomenon is observed in the experimental results: The model without both fusion modules (w/o Both Fusion Modules), achieving 80.98%, performs better than the variants that remove only the global fusion module (76.23%) or only the local fusion module (74.16%), and even outperforms the baseline Res2Net (77.83%). This behavior may be attributed to the following reasons: Removing both fusion mechanisms simplifies the network structure, thereby avoiding interference caused by incomplete feature fusion; When local and global fusion modules are not jointly optimized, using either one independently may introduce feature conflicts; Under certain data distributions, overly strong feature interactions may lead to overfitting in the grouped residual structure. Furthermore, the ablation analysis on the grouped residual component shows that removing this module results in a 6.73% performance degradation, confirming the importance of the multi‐scale grouped design for extracting speech time–frequency features. Contrary to expectations, removing the global feature fusion module (w/o Global Feature Fusion) leads to only a 5.54% decrease in performance, suggesting that, for the current task, fine‐grained local feature modeling is more discriminative than global contextual integration. 4 DISCUSSION As far as we know, there is no similar approach to speech processing that builds a similar screening tool that can screen for SCD so quickly and efficiently. This study proposes an innovative exploratory model, based on the fusion of residual network features from Mandarin phonetics. The results show that this method excels in multi‐classification tasks, particularly achieving an 81.77 ± 2.78% accuracy rate in SCD recognition, significantly outperforming traditional scales and existing speech models. This achievement not only validates the effectiveness of the multi‐scale speech feature fusion framework in the early identification of cognitive disorders but also paves a new way for AI‐assisted non‐invasive early screening of neurodegenerative diseases. 4.1 The pathological relationship between speech features SCD and AD The clinical manifestations of AD extend beyond memory impairment, also including abnormalities in speech rate and other acoustic features. 44 , 45 , 46 , 47 These speech changes are linked to medial temporal lobe atrophy, and similar alterations can be observed even in the preclinical stages of AD, such as the SCD phase. 44 , 47 Notably, SCD‐Q scores were relatively similar across clinical groups in the present study, which is consistent with prior findings that subjective complaints may plateau or even decrease in later disease stages due to reduced insight or anosognosia. 10 , 48 This observation highlights the limited sensitivity of self‐reported measures in capturing progressive neurocognitive decline. Further analysis reveals that patients’ speech characteristics, including the frequency, periodicity, and amplitude of voiced and voiceless segments, can become abnormal due to neurophysiological changes. For example, while damage to key regions in the medial temporal lobe (such as the hippocampus) primarily affects episodic memory, atrophy of the adjacent anterior temporal cortex impairs the fluent retrieval of semantic memory. 49 , 50 These speech features, known as rhythmic variables, including intonation, volume, and speech rate, not only reflect cognitive deficits in language planning and speech production but also serve as biomarkers to reveal patients’ emotional states (such as anxiety and depression) and physiological conditions (such as fatigue and disease progression). 51 , 52 Therefore, this study used a residual network model to comprehensively analyze local phoneme characteristics and global semantic coherence in audio, which can sensitively detect subtle abnormalities in speech production that are not yet identified by conventional scales in SCD patients. This approach holds promise for delaying disease progression through medication or cognitive training before widespread neurodegeneration, ultimately improving patient outcomes. 51 4.2 Clinical translational potential of this study's results The results of this study show that the analysis technology based on phonetic features (rhythmic variables) can not only reveal the pathomechanism but also provide a non‐invasive and dynamic clinical tool for ultra‐early screening (SCD stage) and precise intervention. Its advantages are reflected in the following aspects: The proposed multimodal classification model demonstrated the highest accuracy (81.77 ± 2.78%) in predicting SCD, which has significant clinical implications. SCD is an extremely early stage of AD, where patients only exhibit subjective memory complaints without significant abnormalities in objective cognitive tests, and traditional scales (such as MoCA, Mini‐Mental State Examination [MMSE], STT‐A and B, BNT, Verbal Fluency Test (VFT), AVLT) and imaging examinations lack sensitivity. 53 , 54 By analyzing subtle variations in acoustic speech features, the proposed model is able to identify acoustic differences in speech production among individuals with SCD. 55 , 56 The combination of Fbank features extracted at a 16‐kHz sampling rate with a 4×4 dilated CNN enables the detection of subtle acoustic variations in the speech of individuals with SCD, highlighting the potential of acoustic speech analysis for characterizing early cognitive status differences. 57 Nevertheless, given the cross‐sectional design of this study, the observed findings represent associations between speech features and SCD status and cannot be used to infer longitudinal disease progression or stage transitions. These acoustic speech features hold potential value as tools for monitoring cognitive changes; however, their practical application still requires further longitudinal studies to systematically verify their stability and reproducibility in long‐term monitoring. Meanwhile, future studies should further track the transition from SCD to MCI, evaluating the sensitivity and specificity of relevant speech features in predicting conversion risk in order to clarify their utility as screening and early warning indicators in the preclinical stage. This study achieved an accuracy rate of 81.77 ± 2.78% in SCD recognition by screening for speech biomarkers and constructing a predictive model, surpassing the performance limitations of traditional screening tools (such as MMSE with a sensitivity of 69%). 58 The speech‐based AD assessment model, which integrates local and global feature fusion techniques, significantly improved the classification accuracy across four categories: AD, MCI, SCD, and NC. 4.3 The significance of early identification of SCD and AD prevention The integration of speech data features provides quantitative insights into the early pathological mechanisms of language network degeneration in AD and offers a highly sensitive tool for precise SCD identification and intervention. It drives the shift from single biomarkers to multi‐dimensional dynamic assessments in AD diagnosis. SCD accuracy is 81.77 ± 2.78%, higher than common clinical scales like MMSE (69% sensitivity, 78% specificity). 58 Although scale‐based assessments involve a degree of subjectivity, speech – as an objective biological feature 59 – suggests its potential as a biomarker for detecting early cognitive decline. The AI‐based speech tool employed in this study enhances the accuracy of screening for SCD by analyzing these vocal characteristics to identify high‐risk individuals during the asymptomatic stage, thereby providing an optimal window for early intervention. Unlike traditional SCD screening, which is resource‐intensive due to complex diagnostics and costly tests, these AI tools are non‐invasive and automated and can operate remotely, significantly reducing medical resource consumption. Speech recognition can identify SCD within 60 to 120 s, expanding screening scope and aiding AD prevention. In the future, AI screening tools could become a crucial supplement for SCD screening, especially in resource‐limited communities. The study suggests a three‐tier diagnostic pathway for SCD: initial voice screening, cognitive assessment, and biomarker confirmation, aligning with new guidelines that emphasize clinical AD stages rather than “at risk of AD.” 14 This research provides an economic and practical technical solution for large‐scale early SCD screening in the future. 4.4 Advantages and limitations of the study Despite achieving a SCD screening accuracy of 81.77 ± 2.78% using speech analysis, this study has notable limitations. First, the distribution of the subject sample across subgroups such as SCD, MCI, and AD was uneven, which could have affected the model's generalization despite stratified sampling. Second, this study followed the 2020 Expert Consensus on the Diagnostic Process and Standards for SCD in the Preclinical Stage of AD in China, using ADL, MoCA, SCD‐Q, and the picture description test to screen for SCD. This “exclusionary diagnosis” approach aligns with community screening's simplicity, but, compared to the 2024 Chinese Expert Consensus on the Diagnosis and Treatment of Alzheimer's Disease‐Induced Mild Cognitive Impairment, which recommends validating biomarkers (e.g., ApoE gene and cerebrospinal fluid Aβ42/phosphorylated tau [p‐tau]), it is more subjective and lacks sufficient pathological specificity. For example, the scale screening may confuse cognitive complaints related to AD‐induced SCD with those from other causes (e.g., vascular or depression‐related conditions), and the lack of objective validation through laboratory tests (e.g., cerebrospinal fluid Aβ42/p‐tau ratio) may affect the accuracy of stratifying high‐risk populations. Third, conducted in Mandarin, the study ignored linguistic diversity. Fourth, this study only explored the relationship between speech‐level features and the AD screening scale and did not examine the associations between age, education level, and speech features. This represents a limitation of the present study, which will be further investigated in future research. Future work should include underrepresented languages and races to ensure universality. Speech assessments in diverse populations and languages, incorporating cross‐database tests and varied recording conditions, are essential to evaluate the general applicability of speech analysis to a broader population. The establishment of cross‐database tests and the use of multiple speech tasks and different speech recording conditions to embed heterogeneity into the study population and speech records will be crucial. However, despite these limitations, the study's innovative speech screening technology offers significant clinical value for early AD detection. Its core advantages are threefold. First, traditional biological tests, such as cerebrospinal fluid analysis and positron emission tomography imaging, are invasive and costly. Therefore, in community screenings, reducing the initial use of these tests can minimize resource waste. This study's non‐invasive speech‐based exploratory model overcomes the limitations of traditional biological tests, establishes a pathway for rapid identification of cognitive impairment risk, and provides a viable solution for large‐scale high‐risk population screening in communities and remote areas, Second, this study's framework identified the risk of early cognitive impairment by analyzing the correlation between speech features and cognitive impairment, achieving an accuracy of 81.77 ± 2.78% in recognizing SCD. Third, combining speech features (e.g., speaking rate, intonation) with cognitive scales (MoCA, SCD‐Q) enhances accuracy. Speech data reveal subtle changes that scales might miss (e.g., early language fluency decline), while scales validate the clinical significance of speech features, reducing misjudgments. 4.5 Future direction In the future, we will integrate multi‐scale speech features with blood biomarkers (e.g., Aβ42/p‐tau) to create a “behavior–molecular” screening framework, reducing misjudgment risks. For initial positives, we will prioritize low‐cost blood tests (e.g., Simoa) over cerebrospinal fluid exams to control costs and improve specificity. We will also extend the process to treatment evaluation by incorporating multimodal data (e.g., eye movement, gait) to build a longitudinal predictive model, ultimately creating an intelligent decision‐making system covering the entire AD course.
