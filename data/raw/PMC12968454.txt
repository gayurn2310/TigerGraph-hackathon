Impact of APOE ε4 Genotype Load on Cognitive Function and Lipid Metabolism in Patients With Cerebral Small Vessel Disease

ABSTRACT Background Apolipoprotein ε4 ( APOE ε4) is a potent genetic risk factor for Alzheimer's disease (AD). However, its role in cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD) remains unclear. Given the clinical and pathological similarities between CSVD and AD, this study aimed to investigate the associations of APOE ε4 gene dosage with cognitive function and dyslipidemia in CSVD patients, and to explore whether lipid disturbances mediate the effect of APOE ε4 on white matter hyperintensity (WMH) burden and cognitive impairment. Methods The study was a retrospective study. A total of 717 inpatients with CSVD admitted to the Department of Neurology, Wuhan No.1 Hospital, from January 2023 to January 2025 were enrolled. Associations among APOE ε4 gene dosage, cognitive function, lipid metabolism, and WMH were analyzed. Results The number of APOE ε4 alleles showed a significant negative correlation with MMSE and MoCA scores ( p &lt; 0.001), and a dose‐dependent positive correlation with the risk of cognitive impairment and WMH severity, independent of traditional vascular risk factors. Lipid analysis indicated that APOE ε4 was an independent risk factor for elevated low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C), but had no significant associations with high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C) or triglycerides (TG). Mediation analysis suggested that LDL‐C may mediate the indirect detrimental effect of APOE ε4 on cognitive function. Conclusion APOE ε4 gene dosage is an independent risk factor for cognitive decline and WMH in CSVD patients, independent of traditional vascular risk factors. APOE ε4 may indirectly impair cognitive function by elevating LDL‐C levels to exacerbate atherosclerosis and cerebrovascular injury.

1 Introduction Cerebral Small Vessel Disease (CSVD) refers to a spectrum of clinical, imaging, and pathological syndromes caused by various etiologies affecting the intracerebral arteries and their branches, arterioles, capillaries, venules, and small veins. Its common imaging manifestations include white matter hyperintensities (WMH), lacunar infarcts, cerebral microbleeds (CMB), enlarged perivascular spaces (EPVS), and brain atrophy [ 1 ]. Among them, cognitive impairment represents the most prevalent clinical manifestation induced by CSVD. In recent years, with the deepening of research, the association between CSVD and Alzheimer's disease (AD) has gradually attracted attention. A growing body of evidence indicates that CSVD is not only linked to the early pathogenesis of AD but also shares a substantial overlap in risk factors [ 2 ]. WMH in cerebral white matter is defined as a lesion with brighter signal intensity than surrounding white matter on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) sequences [ 3 ]. Prins et al. found that WMH volume was associated with AD, CSVD, and cognitive decline [ 4 ]. Apolipoprotein E ( APOE ) is a secretory glycoprotein [ 5 ], consisting of three common subtypes: APOE ε2, APOE ε3, and APOE ε4 [ 6 ]. In previous studies, APOE has been confirmed as the strongest universal genetic risk factor for AD, with the risk of AD in APOE allele carriers reported in the order: ε4 &gt; ε3 &gt; ε2 [ 7 , 8 ]. The primary mechanisms by which it increases AD risk involve promoting earlier and more abundant amyloid pathology in the brains of APOE ε4 carriers, affecting amyloid‐β (Aβ) metabolism and deposition, disrupting normal neuronal function, and thereby influencing cognitive function [ 9 , 10 ]. Among these emerging putative pathways, cerebrovascular pathology is a potential key player in linking APOE ε4 to cognition [ 11 ]. Additionally, APOE plays a significant role in lipid transport and metabolism [ 12 ]. Studies have shown that APOE ε4 carriers tend to have higher total cholesterol (TC) and low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C) levels [ 13 ], as well as lower high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C) levels [ 14 ]. However, the role of the APOE ε4 allele in CSVD remains poorly understood. Given the clinical and pathological similarities between CSVD and AD, we aimed to investigate the association between APOE ε4 genotype load and the severity of WMH lesions, the degree of dyslipidemia, and cognitive impairment in CSVD patients. We also sought to examine whether the APOE ε4 genotype exacerbates WMH burden by inducing lipid metabolism disturbances, thereby leading to aggravated cognitive impairment. 2 Materials and Methods 2.1 Study Population This is a retrospective study of inpatients in the Department of Neurology at Wuhan No.1 Hospital. A total of 717 inpatients from the Department of Neurology, Wuhan No.1 Hospital, from January 2023 to January 2025 were selected as study subjects. These patients were from three subspecialties, namely the CSVD subspecialty, the Headache and Cognitive Impairment subspecialty, and the Dizziness and Balance Disorder subspecialty, and primarily sought medical attention due to CSVD‐related symptoms or signs, including dizziness, headache, lacunar infarction, and memory decline. Inclusion criteria: (1) All patients met the diagnostic criteria for CSVD specified in the Expert Consensus on the Diagnosis and Treatment of Cerebral Small Vessel Disease in China [ 15 ]; (2) All patients had completed APOE genotype testing, cognitive function assessment, WMH scoring, and lipid metabolism index detection; (3) Age 45–75 years old; (4) Not using lipid‐lowering medications. Exclusion criteria: (1) Comorbid diseases affecting cognitive function (e.g., Alzheimer's disease, Parkinson's disease, epilepsy, etc.); (2) Receiving lipid‐lowering medications; (3) History of definite mental disorders; (4) Inability to complete neuropsychological tests due to various reasons (e.g., severe language, hearing, or visual impairments); (5) Missing clinical data (Figure 1 ). FIGURE 1 Flowchart of patient inclusion. Relevant clinical data of patients were collected, including age, gender, history of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidemia, atherosclerosis, years of education, smoking, and drinking history, as well as levels of apolipoprotein A‐1 (ApoA‐1), apolipoprotein B (ApoB), high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C), low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C), triglycerides (TG), and total cholesterol (TC). 2.2 MRI of the Brain The patients were scanned on an MRI scanner (Siemens/MAGENTOM Vida 3.0 T) in the Department of Radiology of Wuhan No.1 Hospital. All MRI images were analyzed jointly by a chief neurologist and a chief neuroradiologist, both of whom were completely unaware of all patient data during the grading period. Scanning routine sequences include T1‐weighted sequences, T2‐weighted sequences, and FLAIR sequences. WMH mainly presents as punctate or patchy lesions with high signals on T2 and FLAIR sequences (excluding flow‐void signals), and is predominantly found in the deep subcortical white matter of the brain, such as periventricular, corona radiata, and centrum semiovale regions. WMH lesions are usually bilateral and symmetrical, with blurred margins. The modified Fazekas scale was used to grade cerebral WMH: (1) Fazekas grade 1: Punctate lesions; (2) Fazekas grade 2: Patchy lesions (partial fusion of punctate spots); (3) Fazekas grade 3: Confluent lesions (large‐area fusion of lesions) [ 16 , 17 ]. 2.3 Cognitive Function Assessment All patients were assessed for cognitive function using the Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE) and the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) [ 18 ]. (1) The total score of the MMSE ranges from 0 to 30 points, with cutoff scores related to educational attainment: ≤ 17 points for the illiterate group, ≤ 20 points for primary school education (schooling duration ≤ 6 years), and &lt; 24 points for secondary education and above (schooling duration ≥ 6 years) [ 19 ]. It consists of five defined cognitive domains: orientation (10 points), immediate memory (3 points), calculation and attention (5 points), recall (3 points), and language (9 points). (2) The total score of the MoCA ranges from 0 to 30 points. For individuals with years of education ≤ 12, a 1‐point adjustment is added to the total score. A total score ≥ 26 indicates normal cognition, 18–25 suggests mild cognitive impairment, 10–17 indicates moderate cognitive impairment, and &lt; 10 signifies severe cognitive impairment. The cognitive domains assessed include: visuospatial and executive functions (5 points), naming (3 points), attention (6 points), language (3 points), abstraction (2 points), delayed recall (5 points), and orientation (6 points). The criteria for determining cognitive function status were as follows: For the MoCA, the education‐adjusted total score was used—a score of ≥ 26 indicated normal cognitive function, while a score of &lt; 26 suggested the presence of cognitive impairment. For the MMSE, the determination was strictly based on the aforementioned education‐stratified cutoff values (illiteracy: ≤ 17 points; primary education: ≤ 20 points; secondary education and above: &lt; 24 points). 2.4 APOE Genotype APOE genotyping was performed using a human APOE genotyping detection kit (fluorescence PCR‐restriction enzyme digestion method, Guangzhou Baochuang Biotechnology Co. Ltd.), with the Roche cobas z 480 platform used as the detection system. The specific procedures were as follows: (1) Sample requirements: Ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA)‐ or sodium citrate‐anticoagulated whole blood samples were collected from the study participants. After genomic DNA extraction, the samples were analyzed using a UV spectrophotometer to ensure the DNA concentration was ≥ 10 ng/μL and the OD₂₆₀/OD₂₈₀ ratio ranged from 1.6 to 2.0. (2) Reaction system: The 8‐tube preloaded PCR reaction mixture provided in the kit (containing PCR Buffer, deoxyribonucleoside triphosphates [dNTPs], primers, probes, and enzyme solution) was used. A 5 μL aliquot of sample DNA (or negative/positive controls) was added to each tube, followed by brief centrifugation before loading onto the instrument. The 8‐tube PCR reaction set included 4 detection systems (Tube 1–Tube 4), each designed with bispecific probes targeting the rs429358 and rs7412 loci. The VIC channel was used to detect the target gene signal, while the FAM channel was used to detect the internal control signal (to monitor reaction validity). (3) Amplification protocol: Pre‐denaturation at 95°C for 2 min; followed by 10 cycles of denaturation at 95°C for 10 s and extension at 72°C for 30 s; and subsequently 30 cycles of denaturation at 95°C for 10 s, annealing/extension at 60°C for 1 min (signal collection), and extension at 72°C for 30 s. (4) Result interpretation: Genotypes of the rs429358 and rs7412 loci, and thus the APOE genotype, were determined based on the cycle threshold (Ct) value differences between the VIC and FAM channels (ΔCt1 = VIC Ct value of Tube 1 − VIC Ct value of Tube 2; ΔCt2 = VIC Ct value of Tube 3 − VIC Ct value of Tube 4), combined with verification using negative/positive controls (for negative controls: FAM Ct value ≤ 23.0 and no VIC amplification; for positive controls: FAM and VIC Ct values of Tubes 1–4 were within the range specified by the kit). 2.5 Statistical Analysis Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 25.0. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test and Levene's test were applied to assess data normality and variance homogeneity, respectively. For normally distributed data with homogeneous variance, one‐way ANOVA was used for group comparisons (presented as M ± SD), while the Kruskal‐Wallis H test was applied to non‐normally distributed data [presented as M (Q1, Q3)]. Chi‐square tests analyzed qualitative data; Fisher's exact test corrected for expected frequencies &lt; 5. To explore the association between APOE genotypes and cognitive impairment, MMSE and MoCA scores were set as dependent variables, with non‐ε4 carriers as the reference group. Linear regression adjusted for age, sex, and other confounders to estimate β coefficients and 95% CIs. Binary logistic regression evaluated the risk of cognitive impairment, and ordinal logistic regression analyzed WMH severity, complemented by goodness‐of‐fit and parallel lines tests. For lipid associations, HDL‐C, LDL‐C, and TG served as dependent variables in multivariable linear regression models. Mediation analysis (Hayes' PROCESS Model 4, 5000 bootstrap samples) examined the role of lipids in the genotype‐cognitive function relationship. Sensitivity analysis, excluding double ε4 carriers, validated the results' robustness. Statistical significance was set at p &lt; 0.05. 3 Results 3.1 Patient Characteristics Among the total cohort of 874 patients with CSVD, 717 CSVD patients met the inclusion criteria. Among them, 427 cases were in the non‐ε4 carrier group, 203 cases in the single ε4 allele carrier group, and 87 cases in the double ε4 allele carrier group. There were no statistically significant differences among the three groups in terms of age, sex ratio, educational level, smoking rate, alcohol consumption rate, cardiac disease, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, atherosclerosis, TC, ApoA‐1, and ApoB (all p &gt; 0.05). HDL‐C levels were significantly higher in non‐ε4 carriers compared with ε4 carrier groups, with double ε4 allele carriers exhibiting the lowest levels. LDL‐C showed significant intergroup differences, with notably elevated levels in ε4 carriers (3.43 ± 0.70 vs. 3.53 ± 0.87 mmol/L). Moreover, the proportion of cognitive impairment demonstrated a distinct dose‐dependent increase with escalating ε4 allele load. MMSE scores showed a significant stepwise decline with increasing ε4 alleles, paralleled by a similar trend in MoCA scores. WMH severity was more pronounced in ε4 carriers, with 79.2% of non‐carriers presenting with mild (grade 1) WMH versus 67.8% of double ε4 carriers exhibiting severe (grade 3) WMH ( p &lt; 0.001) (Table 1 ; Figure 2 ). In the binary logistic regression analysis, with non‐ε4 carriers as the reference group, the APOE genotype was significantly associated with the risk of cognitive impairment. In Model 1 without adjustment for confounding factors, the effects of carrying single and double ε4 alleles on the presence of cognitive impairment were statistically significant (OR = 5.021, 95% CI: 3.498–7.208, p &lt; 0.001; OR = 10.846, 95% CI: 5.994–19.626, p &lt; 0.001) (Table S2 ). After further adjustment for age, sex, years of education, vascular risk factors, and blood lipid levels in Model 2, the risk effect was further enhanced. The odds ratios (ORs) for participants carrying single or double ε4 alleles did not show a significant decrease, with values of 4.450 (95% CI: 2.913–6.798, p &lt; 0.001) and 9.410 (95% CI: 4.938–17.934, p &lt; 0.001), respectively. These results suggest that APOE ε4 carriage is significantly associated with the risk of cognitive impairment in a dose‐dependent manner, and this association is independent of traditional vascular risk factors (Table 2 ). 3.3 Association Between APOE Genotype and WMH Ordinal logistic regression was performed to analyze the effect of APOE genotypes on WMH, with non‐e4 allele carriers set as the reference group. Without covariate adjustment, both carriers of single or double ε4 alleles showed a significantly higher risk of WMH lesions than non‐carriers (all p &lt; 0.001) (Table S3 ). After adjusting for 11 covariates, including age, sex, and lipid levels, the effect sizes showed no significant attenuation and remained statistically significant (OR = 7.060, 95% CI: 4.680–10.520, p &lt; 0.001; OR = 27.000, 95% CI: 15.440–47.304, p &lt; 0.001) (Table 2 ). 3.4 Association Between APOE Genotype and Blood Lipids In patients with CSVD, there were significant differences in the association between the APOE ε4 genotype and blood lipid levels, with details as follows: The APOE ε4 genotype was an independent risk factor for elevated LDL‐C ( p &lt; 0.001), and a dose effect was observed. Carriage of a single ε4 allele ( β = 0.931) or double ε4 alleles ( β = 1.021) was significantly associated with increased LDL‐C levels, and the magnitude of the effect of double alleles was slightly greater than that of a single allele (Table 2 ). The APOE ε4 genotype had no significant effect on HDL‐C levels (all p &gt; 0.05). There was partial heterogeneity in the association between the APOE ε4 genotype and TG. Carriage of a single ε4 allele was associated with increased TG levels ( p = 0.015, β = 0.157), whereas this association did not reach statistical significance in those carrying double ε4 alleles ( p = 0.131). This inconsistency may be attributed to limited sample size or the complexity of metabolic processes (Table 2 ). Additionally, we found that smoking was an independent risk factor for decreased HDL‐C levels ( β = −0.062, p = 0.027). Furthermore, the presence of cognitive impairment was associated with lower HDL‐C levels ( β = −0.085, p = 0.001) (Table S4 ). 3.5 Mediating Effect of Blood Lipids Results of the mediation effect analysis clearly showed that LDL‐C exerted a significant partial mediating role in the association between the APOE genotype and cognitive function (assessed by the MMSE and MoCA). Specifically, the indirect effect on MMSE was −0.025 (95% CI: −0.0336 to −0.016), and the indirect effect on MoCA was −0.024 (95% CI: −0.033 to −0.015) (Table 3 ). In detail, the APOE genotype significantly and positively predicted LDL‐C levels ( β = 0.333, p &lt; 0.001), whereas elevated LDL‐C levels were significantly negatively correlated with cognitive function scores (for MMSE: β = −0.236, p &lt; 0.001; for MoCA: β = −0.208, p &lt; 0.001) (Table S5 ). This indicates that the APOE genotype may partially mediate cognitive impairment indirectly by upregulating LDL‐C levels. In contrast, although HDL‐C showed a statistically significant mediating pathway, its indirect effect size was extremely small (for MMSE: −0.003; for MoCA: −0.004), lacking biological significance (Table 3 ). After excluding subgroups with small sample sizes, the APOE genotype was significantly associated with the risk of cognitive impairment. Using ordinal logistic regression to analyze the effect of APOE genotype on WMH, after excluding subgroups with small sample sizes, compared with the non‐ε4 carrier group, carriers of one ε4 allele showed significantly higher WMH severity (OR = 0.141, 95% CI: 0.092–0.216, p &lt; 0.001). Additionally, the level of LDL‐C was associated with WMH (Table S9 ). 4 Discussion This study systematically investigated the impact of APOE ε4 genotype load on cognitive function, WMH, and lipid metabolism in patients with CSVD. The results demonstrated that APOE ε4 genotype load was dose‐dependently associated with cognitive impairment and WMH severity, and this association was independent of traditional vascular risk factors. Additionally, the association between the APOE ε4 genotype and lipid profiles in CSVD patients showed heterogeneity. Carriers of the APOE ε4 gene had significantly higher LDL‐C levels than non‐carriers, exhibiting a clear dose–response effect where higher genetic load was associated with more pronounced LDL‐C elevation. Furthermore, LDL‐C was found to play a partial mediating role in the impact of APOE ε4 on cognitive function. Studies have shown that the breakdown of the blood–brain barrier (BBB) may be one of the earliest pathogenic events in cognitive decline and AD pathology [ 20 ]. The APOE ε4 genotype has been shown to exacerbate cholesterol dysregulation and enhance BBB disruption, thereby promoting cerebrovascular injury and increasing the risk of vascular cognitive impairment (VCID) and AD [ 21 ]. Meanwhile, the APOE ε4 genotype has been demonstrated to accelerate cognitive decline by promoting amyloid‐β (Aβ) deposition and inducing tau protein phosphorylation [ 22 , 23 ]. A large body of imaging and pathological evidence has shown that various features of CSVD are part of the AD disease course [ 24 ]. Therefore, we propose that the above‐mentioned mechanisms may also be involved in the role of the APOE ε4 genotype in CSVD. We confirmed that CSVD patients carrying the APOE ε4 gene had significantly lower cognitive scores than non‐carriers, and the decline in cognitive scores was more pronounced in patients with double ε4 alleles. After adjusting for confounders, the association between APOE genotype and cognitive function was independent of traditional vascular risk factors and lipid metabolism disorders. Mediation analysis revealed that LDL‐C exhibited a mediating effect between APOE genotype and cognitive scores, suggesting that APOE ε4 may indirectly impair cognitive function by elevating LDL‐C levels to exacerbate atherosclerosis and cerebrovascular injury. Notably, in our further investigation, we focused on the core role of LDL‐C in the association between the APOE genotype and WMH—a key marker of cerebrovascular injury. Ordinal logistic regression analysis showed that elevated LDL‐C levels were a significant independent risk factor for the progression of WMH severity. More importantly, LDL‐C exerted a significant partial mediating role in the association between the APOE genotype and WMH burden. While HDL‐C exhibited a partial mediating effect, the effect size was insufficient, and the mechanism may be related to APOE ε4, reducing the Aβ clearance capacity of HDL‐C [ 25 , 26 ]. By contrast, the mediating effect of TG was not significant, indicating a weak role of TG in the association between APOE ε4 genotype and cognition. These findings provide quantitative evidence for the pathological network of “ APOE —lipid –WMH—cognition,” particularly identifying LDL‐C as a key mediating molecule linking the APOE genotype to WMH burden. But it should be noted that the proportion of indirect effects in the total effect is low, implying that APOE ε4 may have lipid‐independent action pathways and that lipid metabolism is not the dominant factor in its effect on cognitive function. In both the general population and patients with CSVD, carrying the APOE ε4 allele was positively associated with WMH burden and cognitive ability [ 27 , 28 ]. In this study, APOE ε4 genotype load was dose‐dependently associated with WMH severity in CSVD patients, and this association was independent of age, gender, cardiovascular diseases, and lipid metabolism. These findings are consistent with those reported by Schilling et al. [ 27 ] and Mirza et al. [ 28 ]. The evidence for the association between LDL‐C and WMH identified in this study further confirms the notion that APOE ε4 may directly promote WMH formation by increasing LDL‐C levels. In addition, in the linear regression analysis examining the association between APOE ε4 allele dosage and cognitive function, we found that after adjusting for covariates in the model, WMH was the strongest predictor of cognitive impairment—validating its core role as a key pathological marker of CSVD. Therefore, the aforementioned results reveal that APOE ε4 may affect CSVD‐related cognition through a dual mechanism: On one hand, APOE ε4 promotes the aggregation of Aβ to form senile plaques, impairs neural synapses, and directly damages cognitive function; on the other hand, it can exacerbate cerebrovascular endothelial cell injury, induce oxidative stress and increased BBB permeability, and promote the formation of WMH, thereby indirectly leading to cognitive decline [ 29 ]. Existing studies have shown that APOE ε4 increases plasma LDL‐C levels [ 30 ]. This study has confirmed a dose‐dependent positive correlation between the number of APOE ε4 alleles and LDL‐C levels, which aligns with its physiological function—the ε4 isoform demonstrates lower affinity for low‐density lipoprotein receptors (LDL‐R), leading to reduced clearance efficiency of LDL‐C particles and consequent elevation in plasma LDL‐C [ 31 ]. HDL‐C is known as a “protective lipoprotein”. We found that HDL‐C levels were positively correlated with cognitive scores (Table 2 ) and showed a protective effect in the cognitive impairment model (OR = 0.386), which is consistent with the conclusions of Wei S et al. [ 32 ]. In this study, while HDL‐C levels in ε4 allele carriers showed a downward trend, no statistically significant difference was observed, which is speculated to be associated with the effect of ε4 on receptor affinity or cholesterol reverse transport pathways [ 33 , 34 ]. Furthermore, although theoretically, APOE ε4 might affect TG metabolism, this study did not observe significant differences, possibly because strong confounders such as hyperlipidemia and diabetes mellitus diluted the independent effect of the APOE genotype. This study has certain limitations. First, the study subjects were only recruited from a single hospital, which may introduce selection bias and limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, the sample size of patients carrying double ε4 alleles was relatively small ( n = 87). Although sensitivity analysis showed that this subgroup had a limited impact on the results, it may still affect the precision of effect estimation. Finally, this study was retrospective, precluding the clarification of a causal relationship between APOE genotype and cognitive function. Longitudinal cohort studies are warranted to further validate these findings. This study has several limitations: First, the study participants were recruited exclusively from a single hospital, which may introduce biases related to geography, socioeconomic status, and healthcare‐seeking behavior, thereby limiting the generalizability of the study results. Second, most participants were patients with overt symptoms, potentially excluding those with asymptomatic or mild CSVD who did not seek medical attention. This may lead to an overrepresentation of patients with severe WMH and more pronounced cognitive decline in the study population, which could overestimate the strength of the association between the APOE ε4 genotype and these outcomes. Therefore, the study results may be more applicable to CSVD patients with clinical symptoms, and future community‐based prospective studies are recommended to validate their applicability in a broader CSVD population. Third, the sample size of patients carrying two ε4 alleles was relatively small ( n = 87). Although sensitivity analysis indicated that this subgroup had a limited impact on the overall results, it may still affect the precision of effect estimation. Fourth, this study was retrospective and lacked a priori power calculation. To assess the reliability of the main analytical results under the current sample size, we performed a Post hoc Power Analysis using G*Power 3.1.9.7 ( File S1 ). Additionally, the absence of multiple testing correction represents another limitation of this study, which may increase the risk of Type I errors. Readers should consider this factor when interpreting the results, particularly for findings with p ‐values close to 0.05. This study confirms that APOE ε4 allele carriage is an independent risk factor for cognitive decline and WMH in CSVD patients, with LDL‐C exerting a partial mediating effect. These findings suggest that intensive LDL‐C management, particularly in double ε4 carriers, may represent a potential strategy to delay cognitive decline. APOE genotyping in clinical practice could aid in identifying high‐risk individuals to optimize personalized intervention strategies. The results provide a theoretical basis for early risk stratification and precision intervention in CSVD‐related cognitive impairment. Future multicenter prospective studies are warranted to further elucidate the underlying mechanisms and explore therapeutic strategies targeting APOE ‐related pathways.
