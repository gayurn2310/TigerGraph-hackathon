Development of a community home-based dual-supplier cooperation care model for people with dementia based on an intelligent decision-assistance system: protocol of a 1-year randomised controlled trial in China

Abstract Introduction Dementia imposes significant care and financial burdens on families and countries globally. While high-quality home-based care is crucial, the current supply chain of care—relying on family caregivers and community healthcare workers—remains fragmented and lacks effective integration. Furthermore, although artificial intelligence (AI) holds promise, existing applications predominantly focus on diagnosis or monitoring rather than holistic care delivery and quality of life (QoL). This study aims to evaluate the effectiveness of the community home-based dual-supplier cooperation (CHDSC) care model in older people with dementia. Methods and analysis A 1-year, assessor-blinded, parallel-group, superiority randomised controlled trial with 1:1 allocation will be conducted. A total of 200 pairs (community-dwelling people with dementia aged ≥65 years and their primary family caregivers) will be recruited from four community health centres in Guangzhou, China, and randomly assigned to either the CHDSC care model intervention or usual care. The intervention comprises monthly home visits following a structured six-step cycle (problem assessment, personalised recommendation generation, case seminar, training, task execution with progressive handover and reassessment), delivered collaboratively by community healthcare workers and family caregivers and guided by an intelligent decision-assistance system. The primary outcome is the QoL of patients assessed using the Quality of Life in Alzheimer’s Disease scale. Secondary outcomes include caregiver burden, quality of home-based care, behavioural and psychological symptoms, cognitive function, ability to perform activities of daily living, adverse event rate and unplanned readmission rate. A comprehensive process evaluation embedded in the randomised controlled trial will be taken. Ethics and dissemination Ethics approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of Jinan University. Written informed consent will be obtained from all participants. The study findings will be disseminated through publication in a peer-reviewed journal and presented at conferences. Trial registration number ChiCTR2300075393: Implementation and effectiveness evaluation of a home-based dual-supplier cooperation care model for community-dwelling patients with dementia based on intelligent decision assistance system

STRENGTHS AND LIMITATIONS OF THIS STUDY The model will be developed through a systematic, evidence-based synthesis of the latest international and national guidelines on dementia care. The model will incorporate an intelligent decision-assistance system to connect the two primary providers of dementia care: families and communities. The implementation process will employ a multi-community rollout, multi-tool evaluation and multi-timepoint assessment to comprehensively analyse the model’s actual effectiveness. Blinding of participants and intervention deliverers is not possible due to the nature of the home-based, collaborative intervention. Introduction Dementia affects over 55 million people worldwide, with China bearing the largest burden (more than 13 million cases). 1 2 Given the limited capacity of institutional care, home-based care provided by an unpaid spouse or adult child family member(s) remains the dominant model, supporting over 96% of people with dementia. 3 5 However, the quality of current home-based care is often suboptimal, characterised by a lack of professional support, which results in high rates of adverse events and substantial economic costs. 6 8 Community home-based care effectively provides continuous and comprehensive medical care, social support and daily life services to individuals with disabilities or chronic diseases in the home setting. 9 The quality of this care depends critically on its two primary sources: family caregivers and community healthcare workers. This system can be conceptualised through the ‘dual-channel supply chain’ framework, in which integration is essential, as cooperative models produce substantially superior service outcomes compared with non-cooperative approaches. 10 However, in the current Chinese context, this supply chain remains fragmented. Family caregivers, serving as the ‘natural supply’, often lack professional knowledge and face immense burdens, 11 whereas community healthcare workers, the ‘basic supply’, frequently operate without targeted service provisions tailored to the complex needs of patients with dementia. 12 14 This structural disconnection prevents the two entities from providing cohesive support and means that community healthcare workers often lack the accurate evidence needed for proper problem evaluation and intervention strategy development, 12 15 ultimately exacerbating the imbalance between the high demand for specialised care and the low utilisation of professional services. 16 17 Internationally, established models such as the Programme of All-Inclusive Care for the Elderly (PACE) and Home-Based and Community-Based Services (HCBS) in the USA, Integrated Care for Older People (ICOPE) in the UK and Japan’s Dementia Care Model have successfully mitigated supply chain fragmentation. 18 20 These initiatives function as effective service integrators, proactively coordinating medical and social resources to provide continuous support. However, direct transplantation of these resource-intensive, human-centric models to China is challenging. Under the specific resource constraints of the Chinese context, an evidence-based and cost-effective home-based care support model is currently lacking. In recent years, artificial intelligence (AI)-powered clinical decision support systems have emerged as a promising approach to improve dementia care. Despite growing international interest, real-world implementation and user acceptability data remain limited. First, although AI has the potential to support the quality of life (QoL) of people with dementia, current approaches are predominantly focused on monitoring systems (eg, fall detection and tracking behavioural competence) rather than on holistic, personalised care interventions. Most existing studies are small-scale feasibility studies that lack active involvement of people with dementia and fail to demonstrate clinical effectiveness in real-world settings. 21 Second, AI applications in dementia care are heavily skewed towards diagnosis, with limited attention to care and support. 22 Furthermore, existing smart elderly care products often suffer from a ‘disconnection from services’, failing to achieve the genuine ‘product-service integration’ required to improve efficiency. 23 To bridge these gaps, Clinical Decision Support Systems can function as a technological integrator by matching patient information with knowledge databases to enable comprehensive, rapid and structured assessments. Simultaneously, such systems can output personalised lists of recommended interventions, aiding effective decision-making for both family caregivers and community healthcare workers. 24 A novel community home-based dual-supplier cooperation (CHDSC) care model, supported by an intelligent decision-assistance system, was developed during a preparatory phase (2023–2025; full details in online supplemental file 1 ). The present randomised controlled trial (RCT) evaluates the effectiveness of this pre-existing model, delivered over 12 months through collaboration between community healthcare workers and family caregivers, compared with usual care in China. Methods and analysis Study design This study is a multi-centre, single-blind, parallel-group RCT with a 1:1 allocation ratio. Participants will be randomly assigned to either the 12-month CHDSC care model intervention group or the usual care group. The trial will be conducted in four community health centres in Guangzhou, China. Each participating community health centre will have a dedicated intervention team, consisting of two community nurses, two general practitioners, one neurologist, one geriatric psychiatrist, one rehabilitation physician and two research assistants. The community nurse with the higher rank in the team will serve as the team lead, responsible for managing and coordinating the overall intervention process, whereas the other members will be tasked with intervention implementation. The detailed trial procedure is shown in figure 2 . Figure 2 Randomised controlled trial design. Patient and public involvement None. Recruitment and setting Recruitment for this study will take place from September 2026 to December 2026, with a goal of recruiting 200 pairs of older people with dementia and their respective caregivers in four community health centres in Guangzhou. Participants will be recruited by community nurses and general practitioners when people with dementia and their caregivers come to the community health centre for a physical examination. Prior to formal enrolment, the study participants will be given a detailed description of the purpose, content, duration and possible risks of the study and will sign an informed consent form. Eligibility criteria The sample population will comprise people with dementia and their caregivers. Care recipients aged ≥65 years with a confirmed clinical diagnosis of dementia with National Institute on Aging–Alzheimer's Association diagnostic criteria will be included. 25 Conversely, people with life-threatening illnesses or mental disorders will be excluded. Caregivers must be adults (aged ≥18 years) providing care to the recipient for a minimum of 3 h weekly over at least 3 months. Households must have internet and either tablet or computer access, and they need to provide consent. Family caregivers will be excluded if they self-report cognitive impairments or disorders in their medical records or if they have undergone psychotherapeutic or psychopharmacological interventions within the preceding 3 months. Both members of the dyad must anticipate residing in the community for the subsequent year without plans for relocation and possess functional hearing and speaking. Randomisation and blinding Patients will be stratified according to their dementia severity (mild/moderate/severe) to control for this key prognostic factor. The severity of dementia was determined using the Global Deterioration Stages between Normal Ageing and Alzheimer’s Disease. 26 Within each stratum, an independent statistician will use IBM SPSS Statistics software (version 25.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) to generate a random allocation sequence with varying block sizes (4 or 6), assigning participants to either the intervention or control group in a 1:1 ratio. The random allocation sequence will be placed in opaque, sealed envelopes. These envelopes will be opened only after each participant has completed all baseline assessments. Outcome assessors and data analysts will be blinded to group allocation. All research staff and participants will be instructed not to discuss any intervention details with members of the other group. Intervention process Intervention group The intervention group will receive care following the 12-month CHDSC care model. This model addresses the four core domains of dementia care (cognitive dysfunction, behavioural and psychological symptoms change, reduced in activities of daily living and caregiver support) through monthly home visits and collaborative delivery by community healthcare workers and family caregivers, guided by an intelligent decision-assistance system. The system follows Alzheimer’s Association dementia care practice recommendations 27 and the Chinese expert consensus on the rehabilitation management of Alzheimer’s disease. 28 An interdisciplinary team (including specialists in geriatric psychiatry, rehabilitation, informatics and design) will provide oversight. Detailed procedures for the development and planned implementation of the intelligent decision-assistance system are shown in online supplemental file 1 and figure 3 . Figure 3 Technical roadmap for the new home-based care model. The intervention is supported by a web-based platform designed to provide evidence-based decision support for community healthcare workers and family caregivers. The platform comprises six modules: (1) personal centre (health records and information exchange), (2) comprehensive assessment (anomaly detection and alerts), (3) care problems (identified needs), (4) care measures (intervention recommendations with multimedia resources), (5) task list (task assignment between suppliers) and (6) intelligent training (self-training and caregiver education). Full technical details and development procedures are provided in online supplemental file 1 . The intervention will follow a structured six-step cycle repeated over 12 monthly home visits (full details in online supplemental file 1 and figure 4 ): (1) problem assessment, a comprehensive evaluation of the needs of both the person with dementia and the caregiver will be conducted, with data entered into the intelligent decision-assistance system; (2) generation of recommended interventions, the system automatically will generate a personalised list of evidence-based care measures; (3) case seminar, the intervention team and the primary family caregiver review the recommended list, modify it based on available resources and caregiver capacity and assign specific tasks; (4) training, community healthcare workers will complete self-training via the system’s intelligent modules and deliver tailored training to the family caregiver; (5) task execution, high-expertise tasks will initially be performed by healthcare workers, with progressive handover to the family caregiver through ongoing demonstration and support; and (6) evaluation and reassessment, caregiver adherence will be monitored, patient status reassessed and the cycle adjusted as needed. Figure 4 Flow chart of the intervention implementation. Control group The control group will receive routine community-based care as currently provided in Guangzhou, China. This comprises enrolment in the family doctor signing service and establishment of health records, telephone follow-ups approximately once every 3 months, invitations to community-organised health education lectures (typically 4–6 per year, focused on general chronic disease management rather than dementia care or caregiver support) and access to on-demand consultations with community healthcare workers for health-related concerns during clinic hours. Crucially, unlike the intervention group, routine care in this locality does not involve a multidisciplinary team, case conferencing, scheduled home visits or AI-assisted decision support. 29 Currently, formal community care resources for dementia are limited. 30 As a result, care remains primarily the responsibility of family caregivers (typically a spouse or adult child family members) with medical support being reactive rather than proactive. Community healthcare services primarily serve as supplementary resources, providing support only when initiated by the family or patient, rather than through integrated, comprehensive case management. Sample size The sample size calculation formula for comparing the means of two independent samples based on a completely randomised design (using a two-sided test) is as follows: n = 2 μ α + μ β 2 δ 2 δ 2 . An RCT employing a personalised care model was conducted by Yang Siyuan et al for community-dwelling older individuals with dementia and their caregivers. The outcome indicators and measurement tools used in that study are consistent with those employed in the present study. 31 As reported, the QoL measures showed a standard deviation (σ) of 3.12 and a mean difference (δ) between the two groups of 2.27. To achieve 90% power for detecting a specified effect size at a two-sided alpha level of 0.05, the sample size calculation yielded a requirement of 52 participants per group. With reference to previous literature, a 30% attrition rate was assumed. 32 33 Accordingly, the sample size was inflated, resulting in a requirement of 75 participants per group. To expand the application coverage of the CHDSC care model, this study finally plans to include 200 pairs of older individuals with dementia and their respective caregivers (100 per group). Outcome measures Throughout the study, data will be collected on four separate occasions: baseline data collection at the onset of the study and subsequent data collection at 6, 9 and 12 months later. Each assessment will include primary and secondary outcome measures. Sociodemographic characteristics and disease-related data will only be collected at the baseline assessment. The questionnaire will be collected to a special unified custody to protect patient privacy. Sociodemographic characteristics and disease-related data Participants will report sociodemographic characteristics (13 items including education level, co-morbid conditions and family history of dementia, etc.) and disease-related data (six items including the time of disease diagnosis, disease severity, blood pressure, blood cholesterol levels, fasting blood glucose levels and oral glucose tolerance test results). Sociodemographic characteristics will be assessed using a self-reported questionnaire, and disease-related data will be obtained from medical examination reports. All self-reported items will be available in Chinese. Primary outcome Quality of life of patients The QoL-AD scale, which combines patient self-evaluation and caregiver evaluation, is used to measure the QoL of life of people with Alzheimer’s disease. 34 The scale consists of 13 items across four domains: objective environment, behavioural competence, domain-specific perceived QoL and psychological well-being. A 4-point Likert scale is used, where higher scores reflect better QoL. The Chinese version has demonstrated a test–retest reliability coefficient of 0.835, and Cronbach’s α of 0.659 for the patient version and 0.869 for the caregiver version. 35 Secondary outcomes Caregiver burden The Caregiver Burden Inventory includes 24 items across five dimensions: time-dependence burden, developmental burden, physical burden, social burden and emotional burden. 36 Higher scores (0–96) reflect a greater caregiver burden. The Chinese version has a Cronbach’s α of 0.92, test–retest reliability of 0.93, and criterion-related validity of 0.85. 37 Quality of home-based care The Family Caregiver Consequences Inventory contains 21 items across three subscales: patient, family and caregiver. 38 Higher scores (21–63) indicate better family caregiving quality. The internal consistency is 0.83, with a test–retest reliability of 0.80. Behavioural and psychological symptoms The Neuropsychiatric Inventory-Questionnaire is a caregiver-reported scale assessing 12 neuropsychiatric symptoms, initially answered with ‘yes’ or ‘no’ responses. 39 For ‘no’ responses, the assessment proceeds to the next question. For ‘yes’ responses, severity is rated using a 4-point frequency scale and a 3-point severity scale. The product of severity (0–3) and frequency (0–4) yields a symptom score ranging from 0 to 12, with total scores ranging from 0 to 144. The Chinese version has a Cronbach’s α of 0.85, split-half reliability of 0.83 and test–retest reliability of 0.86. It is suitable for use by the Chinese population in the assessment of behavioural and psychological symptoms. 40 Cognitive function The Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) assesses eight aspects of cognitive function: visuospatial executive function, naming, memory, attention, language fluency, abstract thinking, 5-min delayed memory and orientation, with a total score range of 0–30. 41 This study will use the Beijing version of MoCA. To account for the educational bias, 1 point is added for individuals with &lt;12 years of education. Higher scores indicate better cognitive function, with a cut-off value of 26 points used to define normal cognitive function. The test takes approximately 10 min to complete. The Beijing version has a Cronbach’s α of 0.818, and the correlation coefficients between individual item scores and the total score range from 0.5 to 0.9. 42 Ability to perform activities of daily living The Barthel Index (BI) and the Functional Assessment Questionnaire (FAQ) will be used to evaluate the patient’s ability to perform activities of daily living. The BI evaluates 10 daily living functions, with a score range of 0–100. Higher scores indicate greater self-care ability. Its Cronbach’s α is 0.87, with test–retest reliability of 0.85. 43 The FAQ measures 10 functions related to daily living. Scores reflect the patient’s degree of dependence, ranging from 0 (normal) to 3 (dependent). The total score ranges from 0 to 30, with higher scores indicating a higher degree of dependence. Its Cronbach’s α is 0.82, with test–retest reliability of 0.80. 44 Adverse event rate The adverse event rate is the percentage of overall cases with adverse events and includes falls, bed drops, being lost, pressure sores, infections, cardiovascular events and fractures. This information will be collected through caregiver reports and medical record reviews. Unplanned readmission rate The unplanned readmission rate is defined in this study as the percentage of participants with unplanned hospital readmissions or emergency service use, obtained from medical record reviews. Process evaluation To evaluate the quality of intervention implementation and its potential impact on study outcomes, a comprehensive process evaluation will be conducted, encompassing various aspects. 45 This evaluation is embedded in the RCT to monitor feasibility of delivery, including recruitment and eligibility feasibility, software development stability and acceptability by families and community healthcare workers. Eligibility and recruitment feasibility Eligibility and recruitment feasibility will be documented by recording rates of eligibility screening, recruitment, retention and specific reasons for withdrawal or dropout across both study arms. Software development stability System stability will be monitored through uptime logs (target≥95%), bug reports and technical failure rates during the trial. Intervention acceptability Data on intervention acceptability will be collected from caregiver training logs, system usage monitoring data, supervisory records, adherence logs and satisfaction surveys including qualitative interviews with families and healthcare workers. Adherence will be assessed based on criteria including number of dropouts, frequency of home visits completed (target≥10 of 12), total time spent on AI-assisted tasks (logged in the decision-assistance system) and caregiver engagement with training modules. Non-adherence will be defined as completing ≤6 home visits, average system usage ≤1 session per week over a continuous period of 4 weeks, or no activity for two consecutive months. Temporary pauses (eg, due to health issues) will be permitted if participation resumes thereafter. Participants meeting non-adherence criteria will be encouraged to complete follow-up assessments. Family and staff acceptability will be evaluated through satisfaction surveys and semi-structured qualitative interviews to explore perceptions of the model’s utility and user-friendliness. Outcome acceptability Outcome acceptability will be assessed by rates of questionnaire completion at baseline and at 6, 9 and 12 months. Adverse events Adverse events (as defined in secondary outcomes) may occur. Participants can report events at any time. Community healthcare workers will prompt for reports during monthly visits (intervention group) or routine follow-ups (control group), with weekly telephone supervision if needed. Events will be documented in an electronic database, with immediate notification to authorities for serious adverse events. Withdrawal will not be required if the event does not exacerbate the participant’s condition and adequate rest/treatment allows continuation. Intervention sustainability Maintenance of the intervention beyond 12 months will be assessed through caregiver self-reported continuation logs and qualitative interviews at 12 months. Statistical analysis Analyses will be performed using SPSS V.29.0 and R V.4.4.0 with two-sided tests at α=0.05. All analyses will be conducted by two independent statisticians blinded to allocation. No formal statistical testing of baseline group differences will be conducted. The primary analysis will follow the intention-to-treat principle, including all randomised participants. A per-protocol dataset (excluding major protocol deviations) will be used in sensitivity analyses. Missing data will be handled primarily using multiple imputation by chained equations under a missing-at-random assumption. Sensitivity analyses will explore missing-not-at-random scenarios using pattern-mixture models. The primary outcome (QoL-AD) and continuous secondary outcomes will be analysed using linear mixed-effects models accounting for repeated measures. Fixed effects will include treatment arm, time (categorical), treatment-by-time interaction and baseline outcome value; pre-specified adjustment covariates will be limited to age, sex and dementia severity. Participants will be included as a random effect. Treatment effects will be reported as adjusted mean differences with 95% CIs. Secondary outcomes will be analysed using the same framework, with p-values adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure to control the false discovery rate. Adverse events and unplanned readmissions will be summarised descriptively (counts and percentages) and compared between groups (eg, χ2 test). Rates will be reported both overall and at the individual participant level (events per participant, by severity and relatedness). Process evaluation data will be analysed separately: quantitative data (eg, recruitment rates, adherence rates, system uptime and acceptability surveys) will be analysed descriptively (eg, means with SD, medians with IQRs or counts with percentages, as appropriate). Qualitative data will be analysed using thematic analysis to identify key facilitators and barriers to the implementation of the CHDSC care model. Exploratory analyses will include pre-planned subgroup analyses (by dementia severity, age, sex and primary caregiver type) and structural equation modelling to investigate potential mediating pathways (eg, via reduced behavioural symptoms or improved home-care quality). A detailed statistical analysis plan and process evaluation plan is provided in online supplemental file 2 . Ethics and dissemination This study has undergone review and approval from the Institutional Review Board of Jinan University (#JNUKY-2023-007), and its trial registration number is ChiCTR2300075393. This study will refer to the regulatory requirements outlined by the National Health Commission of China in the Guidelines for Good Clinical Practice in Drug Clinical Trials. 46 For older patients with dementia lacking the capacity for providing informed consent, written consent will be obtained from their legal guardians. In instances where the legal guardians of these older patients with dementia lack literacy skills, the inclusion of an impartial witness will be requested to oversee the entirety of the informed consent process. The impartial witness will be selected from individuals unrelated to the study and possessing civil capacity. Prior to the signing of the informed consent form, the study details will be explained to the participants verbally and in writing, emphasising the voluntary nature of participation, the right to withdraw at any time without providing a reason and the measures taken to maintain the confidentiality of collected data. Written informed consent will be required from all participants (a sample of the participant consent form is provided in online supplemental file 3 ). All collected data will be anonymised and consolidated into a unified database. Encryption technology will be employed to safeguard the database. Access to the data will be strictly controlled according to research objectives and responsibilities. The findings from this study will be disseminated through publication in a peer-reviewed journal and presented at conferences. Discussion Home-based care for people with dementia is a significant medical and economic concern. There is currently a substantial disparity between the demand and supply for dementia care, resulting in inadequate home-based support and a range of adverse outcomes. 8 47 48 The present RCT will evaluate a novel CHDSC care model supported by an intelligent decision-assistance system. If the model proves effective, it will offer an innovative approach to delivering evidence-based dementia care directly into the home environment while leveraging the complementary strengths of community healthcare workers and family caregivers. Trial findings will be disseminated through abstracts at international conferences and publications in peer-reviewed medical and nursing journals. Should the intervention demonstrate effectiveness, a subsequent health-economic evaluation will be required to quantify its cost-effectiveness. Future studies could then build on these results to examine long-term sustainability and applicability to other chronic conditions. Strengths The CHDSC care model will offer several potential advantages over existing approaches. First, by using an intelligent decision-assistance system, it will minimise reliance on individual clinical intuition, thereby reducing variability and human error in assessment and care planning. Second, its structured workflow will ensure standardised yet personalised problem identification and evidence-based intervention recommendations. Third, the dual-supplier design will mobilise existing community resources to complement family caregiving, potentially improving both efficiency and quality of care. Finally, unlike traditional mechanical cooperation (eg, occasional telephone contact or lectures), the model will enable real-time information sharing and collaborative decision-making between community teams and families through the digital platform. Crucially, the CHDSC care model represents a departure from the current landscape of AI applications in dementia care. Although AI is predominantly utilised for diagnosis or low-level monitoring systems, as highlighted by Andargoli et al 22 and Steijger et al , 21 our system is specifically designed as a care management and decision-support tool focused on evidence-based, complex care interventions and QoL outcomes. The rigour of the trial will be enhanced by pre-specified intervention manuals, standardised training of all intervention staff, computer-generated randomisation with allocation concealment and blinding of outcome assessors. Limitations Blinding of participants and intervention deliverers will not be feasible owing to the nature of the complex intervention; only outcome assessors will be blinded. Although stratified block randomisation with opaque sealed envelopes will be used, complete allocation concealment cannot be guaranteed and may introduce modest selection bias. 49 Furthermore, the development and deployment of complex intelligent decision-assistance systems face inherent challenges related to data availability, cost considerations and algorithmic reliability, which must be acknowledged. Finally, the trial will be conducted exclusively in Guangzhou, China; differences in healthcare infrastructure, cultural attitudes toward filial piety and long-term-care insurance policies may limit direct generalisability to other countries or regions. Our findings may need to be adjusted in the future; however, the data are worthy of reference and further study. Implications for practice and research If the CHDSC care model proves effective and feasible, it could offer a scalable framework for delivering AI-supported, collaborative home-based dementia care, particularly in settings like China where family caregivers bear the primary responsibility. By integrating evidence-based decision support with structured cooperation between community healthcare workers and families, the model has the potential to improve patient QoL, reduce caregiver burden and enhance overall care quality. The embedded process evaluation will identify key implementation challenges and facilitators, providing valuable lessons for wider adoption and refinement. Subsequent research could explore long-term outcomes, cost-effectiveness, adaptability to other regions or chronic conditions and strategies to address current technical limitations of the intelligent decision-assistance system. Overall, this trial may generate high-quality empirical evidence on AI-supported interventions in community dementia care—an area currently dominated by theoretical models in China. Findings could inform policymakers, healthcare providers and researchers in developing or refining digital health tools and cooperative care models, with broader implications for chronic disease management. Progress to date The study officially commenced in January 2023. To date, the project has successfully completed the knowledge base construction and the algorithmic logic design for the intelligent decision-assistance system. The system, which underpins the entire CHDSC care model, is currently undergoing iterative software development in collaboration with technical engineers. As of June 2025, preliminary system development is complete. However, limitations remain, including the need for continuous refinement of the evidence base and optimisation of personalised recommendation algorithms. Formal AI performance evaluation has not yet commenced, as this assessment requires the finalisation of the integrated system. Usability testing of iterative prototypes is ongoing. The comprehensive performance evaluation will be conducted once these developmental stages are finalised. We anticipate finalising system development and commencing participant recruitment for the pilot study by September 2026, followed by the implementation of the fully powered RCT. Lessons learned Although the RCT has not officially commenced, the preparatory phase has yielded valuable insights for refining the intervention protocol and system design. Bridging evidence and practice: during the initial construction of the knowledge base, we observed that clinical guidelines derived solely from literature reviews may not fully align with the practical feasibility and ecological validity required for effective home-based care. This experience underscored the critical importance of integrating evidence with expert consensus regarding practical home and community environments. Enhancing intervention fidelity via design: intense group discussions concerning the presentation of intervention measures within the intelligent decision-assistance system led to the realisation that multimedia content is crucial. We have consequently decided to present intervention content in various user-friendly forms (including text and images) to enhance the fidelity of delivery and support primary caregivers’ comprehension. Necessity of multidisciplinary collaboration: we have confirmed the necessity of establishing a multidisciplinary and multi-level remote research team. The comprehensive discussions among team members have proven essential for improving the robustness of the study protocol, particularly in bridging clinical expertise, technical development and implementation science aspects.
