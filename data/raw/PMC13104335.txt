Analysis of CGG repeat numbers in the FMR1 gene among women of childbearing age in Northeast Sichuan

Objective To determine CGG repeat numbers in the Fragile X messenger ribonucleoprotein 1 ( FMR 1) gene among 1,146 women of childbearing age in Northeast Sichuan, China, and to describe regional carrier prevalence. Methods A total of 1146 cases of women of childbearing age, voluntarily participating in FMR 1 gene detection, were enrolled in this study from April 2021 to January 2024 in our outpatient clinic for regular prenatal check-ups, genetic counseling, and medical treatment for adverse pregnancy history. A triple-primer PCR approach was utilized to determine the CGG repeat numbers in the FMR 1 gene among normal subjects, followed by a further examination of the CGG repeat numbers in fetuses from premutation female carriers. Result In a cohort of 1,146 women, two individuals had premutation alleles (57 and 97 repeat sizes), corresponding to a carrier frequency of 0.17% (2/1,146). Eight individuals had intermediate (gray zone) alleles (repeat sizes 45–54), resulting in a carrier frequency of 0.70%. The combined frequency of premutation and intermediate (gray zone) alleles was 0.87% (10/1,146). No full mutation alleles were identified. Conclusion This cross-sectional study provides baseline data on the regional distribution of the FMR 1 CGG repeat among women of childbearing age in Northeast Sichuan. The carrier rate of the premutation was 0.17%, and the frequency of intermediate alleles was 0.70%. These numbers represent regional prevalence only. Therefore, larger multicentre studies will be necessary to better characterize the distribution of carriers among Chinese populations. Introduction There is a high rate of birth defects in China, with a reported prevalence of 122.54/10,000 perinatal births from 2000 to 2021 [ 1 ]. Genetic factors, particularly single-gene mutations, are a major contributor to this burden. Fragile X syndrome (FXS) is one of the most common single-gene causes of intellectual disability and autism spectrum disorder [ 2 ], with an estimated prevalence of approximately 1/4000 in males and 1/8000–1/5000 in females. Nearly 99% of cases of FXS are due to expansion of the CGG repeat in the Fragile X messenger ribonucleoprotein 1 ( FMR 1) gene, and no disease-modifying treatments currently exist [ 3 ]. In accordance with internationally accepted guidelines, the number of CGG repeats in the FMR 1 gene can be classified as normal (6–44 repeats), intermediate/gray zone (45–54 repeats), premutation (55–200 repeats), and full mutation (&gt; 200 repeats) [ 4 ]. The term “abnormal allele(s)” is used to refer to intermediate and premutation ranges for the purposes of reporting epidemiologically; only the intermediate alleles are not considered pathogenic. While the majority of carriers of intermediate/gray zone or premutation alleles do not present with symptoms, they may have reproductive implications, and full mutation alleles are known to be directly attributed to Fragile X syndrome. Currently, there is a lack of regional data on the prevalence of FMR 1 carriers among women of childbearing age in Northeast Sichuan. Therefore, the current descriptive, cross-sectional study analyzed CGG repeat numbers in FMR 1 using triplet-primed PCR in women of childbearing age in this region. This study will provide regional baseline prevalence data for Northeast Sichuan, for which no previous reports of FMR1 CGG repeat distribution exist. The primary aim of the study was to provide regional carrier prevalence. Given the relatively high incidence of birth defects in this region, establishing baseline carrier frequencies may benefit local genetic counselling services. By identifying the frequencies of intermediate (gray zone) and premutation allele carriers, this study provides descriptive regional prevalence data. Further clinical follow-up is required to determine if identified carriers may have health implications. Materials and methods Study participants 1,146 women of reproductive age who opted to undergo FMR 1 genetic testing between April 2021 and January 2024 at the outpatient clinic for routine prenatal examinations, genetic testing, and assessment of prior pregnancy complications were recruited for the study. Study inclusions consisted of: (1) aged 18–45 years, and (2) did not have any significant medical, surgical, or autoimmune disorders. Exclusionary criteria included: (1) any endocrinological disorder attributable to breastfeeding, surgery, hormonal contraception or another disorder (e.g., breast cancer, PCOS); (2) any serious acute or chronic liver, kidney or systemic disorder or acute infection with rubella, cytomegalovirus, herpes or toxoplasmosis virus; (3) any malignancy; (4) received allogeneic blood transfusion or organ transplant within the past 12 months; (5) received allogeneic cell transplant within 30 days; (6) any other disorder that the physician determined will compromise the accuracy of any testing. Written Informed Consent was obtained from all participants. Study approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the North Sichuan Medical College Affiliated Hospital. Sample collection For the study, 2 mL of venous blood from a fasting participant was collected in an EDTA anticoagulant tube and gently inverted 4–5 times before being stored at 4 °C until processing. DNA isolation Genomic DNA was isolated using a commercial blood DNA extraction kit (QIAGEN51106/51104QIAamp) in accordance with the manufacturer’s specifications. Isolation was completed, and the DNA quantity and purity were verified using a NanoDrop 1000 spectrophotometer. CGG repeat determination of the FMR 1 gene CGG repeat numbers were obtained by the AmplideX™ FMR 1 assay (Asuragen, USA). The FMR1 assay uses a three-primer PCR system comprising FMR1 upstream and downstream primers and a CGG-specific primer (Table 1 ). PCR amplification was performed using an initial denaturation period of five minutes at 98 degrees (C) followed by 25 cycles of denaturation at 97 degrees for 35 s, annealing at 62 degrees for 35 s, extension at 72 degrees for four minutes with a final extension of 72 degrees for 10 min. PCR products were analyzed by capillary electrophoresis using an ABI3730 Genetic Analyzer, and the fragment data were analyzed with GeneMapper 4.0 software. If two alleles were obtained, the allele with the lower number of repeats was referred to as Allele-1 and the allele with the greater number of repeats as Allele-2. If both alleles had the same number of repeats, the assignment of which allele was Allele-1 and which was Allele-2 was made at random for the purpose of achieving consistent statistics [ 6 ]. Consistent with international guidelines, only the number of repeats for Allele-2 was used for the statistical analysis [ 7 ]. Amniotic fluid sample collection and analysis In amniotic fluid sample collection, ultrasound localization was used to collect amniotic fluid at 18–22 + 6 weeks of gestation for anticipated premutation carriers. Approximately 10 mL of amniotic fluid was collected and tested using the same PCR-based method. To confirm the measurement of CGG repeats, each fetal sample was assayed in triplicate. Statistical analysis Given the limited number of intermediate and premutation carriers, analyses were descriptive only. Participant characteristics and CGG repeat distributions were summarized using counts and percentages. Age-based comparisons were presented descriptively without inferential statistical testing. All data management was performed using SPSS software. Results Participant characteristics This study included 1,146 women of childbearing age (mean age: 30.27 ± 4.38 years). For age-based analyses, participants were dichotomized into &lt; 30 years and ≥ 30 years groups to evaluate the distribution of abnormal CGG repeats. FMR1 CGG repeat distribution Twenty-five distinct CGG repeat numbers were identified, ranging from 24 to 97 repeats. The most common alleles were 30 repeats (329 cases, 28.7%) and 31 repeats (267 cases, 23.3%). Two premutation alleles were detected (57 and 97 repeats), corresponding to a premutation carrier frequency of 0.17% (2/1,146; approximately 1:573). Eight intermediate (gray-zone) alleles (45–54 repeats) were identified (frequency 0.70%). The combined frequency of intermediate and premutation alleles was 0.87% (10/1,146). Intermediate alleles were observed at 45, 46, 47 ( n = 4), 48, and 52 CGG repeats, spanning a range of 45–54 repeats. Neither premutation carrier reported a known family history of Fragile X–related disorders, and both were asymptomatic at the time of testing. Abnormal CGG alleles (intermediate plus premutation) were identified in 3 of 549 women aged &lt; 30 years (0.55%) and in 7 of 597 women aged ≥ 30 years (1.17%). Given the limited number of abnormal cases ( n = 10), analyses were descriptive only, and no formal statistical comparisons were performed. Due to the small number of abnormal cases ( n = 10), analyses were descriptive only, and no inferential statistical testing was performed Discussion This study analyzed FMR1 CGG repeat numbers among 1,146 women of childbearing age in Northeast Sichuan, identifying 2 premutation carriers (0.17%) and 8 intermediate carriers (0.70%), yielding an overall abnormal allele prevalence of 0.87%. These data provide regional baseline prevalence estimates for Northeast Sichuan, where FMR1 CGG repeat distributions have not previously been reported. Previous studies among reproductive-age women in northern China reported premutation carrier frequencies of approximately 1/410 [ 8 , 9 ], which is higher than that observed in our cohort. The lower prevalence detected here may reflect regional genetic background, clinic-based recruitment, or sampling variation. The intermediate allele frequency in this study falls within the ranges reported in other Chinese populations [ 9 ]. Similar variability in carrier prevalence across populations has been documented in European and multinational cohorts [ 10 – 12 ], underscoring the influence of ethnicity and study design on prevalence estimates. In this cohort, the most frequent normal alleles clustered at 29–31 repeats, with additional enrichment at 37 repeats, consistent with patterns reported in other populations [ 13 – 15 ]. Intermediate alleles ranged from 45 to 54 repeats, with clustering at 47 repeats, rather than uniformity at a single repeat size. This distribution likely reflects the mutational stability of specific repeat lengths rather than pathogenic significance. Although premutation carriers may face reproductive implications and a risk of transmitting expanded alleles to offspring [ 16 – 20 ], this study was designed to characterize carrier prevalence rather than clinical outcomes. The identification of premutation and intermediate carriers highlights the relevance of targeted genetic counseling, particularly for women with suggestive personal or family histories [ 21 – 25 ]. One prenatal diagnostic case confirmed fetal premutation status; however, this single prenatal case demonstrates technical feasibility only. Consistent with current ACMG and ACOG recommendations, FMR1 carrier screening is advised primarily for women with relevant clinical or family histories, rather than universal population screening [ 21 ]. Our findings support this targeted approach by providing region-specific carrier prevalence data to inform counseling practices. Age-related analyses were underpowered due to the small number of carriers. Although abnormal alleles appeared numerically more frequent in older participants, this difference was not statistically significant and should not be interpreted as evidence of an age effect. Several limitations warrant consideration. This was a single-center, clinic-based descriptive study, limiting generalizability. The number of carriers was small ( n = 10), restricting subgroup analyses and the precision of prevalence estimates. Clinical reproductive phenotypes were not systematically assessed, and epigenetic features such as AGG interruptions or methylation status were not evaluated. Full-mutation alleles were not observed. Future multicenter studies with larger sample sizes, standardized reproductive phenotyping, and longitudinal follow-up are needed to better elucidate clinical implications [ 26 ]. Conclusions The carrier rates of FMR1 premutation and intermediate alleles were 0.17% and 0.70%, respectively, yielding an overall abnormal allele rate of 0.87% among 1,146 women of childbearing age in Northeast Sichuan. These findings provide baseline regional prevalence data for FMR1 CGG repeat distributions. Due to the small number of identified carriers in this study, age-related effects could not be reliably evaluated. This study was descriptive in nature and focused on carrier frequency rather than clinical outcomes. While these data may help inform genetic counseling for women with relevant personal or family medical histories, no conclusions regarding the utility or feasibility of population-based FMR1 screening can be drawn from the present results.
