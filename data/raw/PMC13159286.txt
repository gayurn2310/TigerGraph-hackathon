Weekly fluctuations of plasma amyloid-beta in Alzheimer’s disease: implications for biomarker reliability

Background Alzheimer’s Disease (AD), the most prevalent age-related dementia, is characterized by accumulation of amyloid-beta (Aβ) and hyperphosphorylated tau proteins within the brain. Accurate monitoring of AD progression is crucial for patient management and the evaluation of clinical trials but often involves invasive and expensive methods like cerebrospinal fluid analysis and PET scans. Consequently, minimally invasive plasma biomarkers such as the ratio of Aβ42 and Aβ40 are often used as endpoints in clinical research. This study investigates the variability in plasma Aβ levels on a micro-longitudinal scale to determine if they remain stable over short periods of time. Methods Aβ42 and Aβ40 peptides were quantified in plasma by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay in a cohort of 73 AD patients with AD8 scores ≥ 3. Our cohort was 62% Black/African American and 38% White/Caucasian, 66% female and 34% male. Samples were collected weekly over a five-week micro-longitudinal study. We examined week-to-week Aβ peptide levels within-participant as well as between-patient variation. Impacts both of sex and race were examined as covariates. Results Significant week-to-week fluctuations were observed in the levels of Aβ40, Aβ42, and Aβ42/Aβ40. We observed Aβ42/40 ratio to shift by more than 50% in 60% of participants. Further participants had an average of 20–50% fluctuations in readings on a week-to-week basis. This week-to-week fluctuation appeared to be driven primarily by sudden episodic shifts in Aβ content with only a few individuals showing sustained shifts. We report no significant difference in fluctuations in Aβ content by sex or race of participants. However, we do find plasma amyloid content (Aβ42, Aβ40, and Aβ42/Aβ40) to be elevated in male participants. Finally, we show some participants to present higher levels of amyloid flux than others at least on a micro-longitudinal scale. These findings highlight the dynamic nature of plasma Aβ levels, contrasting with the expected stability over such a short period. Conclusions The substantial levels of weekly variability in plasma Aβ levels we observed are comparable to the 18-month rate of change reported in recent pharmaceutical trials, and suggests that relying solely on these biomarkers for AD monitoring or outcome assessment could be misleading without considering their temporal fluctuations. This study emphasizes the need for frequent sampling and cautious interpretation when using plasma Aβ levels as an outcome measure. Introduction Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder and the leading cause of dementia worldwide. The World Health Organization projects that by 2050, over 13.8 million people will be affected by AD [ 1 ]. AD is neuropathologically defined by the accumulation of amyloid-beta (Aβ) plaques and hyperphosphorylated tau tangles and clinically characterized by a gradual decline in cognitive and functional abilities [ 2 ]. As the global burden of AD continues to rise, there is an urgent need for reliable, accessible biomarkers to support diagnosis, monitor disease progression, and evaluate therapeutic response. Considerable progress has been made in developing and validating blood-based measures. Blood-based assessment of the ratio between Aβ42 and Aβ40 levels, as well as phosphorylated tau, are being increasingly used as surrogate markers for cerebral amyloidosis and tauopathy, respectively, in diagnosis, clinical trials, and research [ 3 ]. However, it is unclear how stable these markers are over short periods, an important consideration for their use as biomarkers. AD is not officially diagnosed until postmortem examination; however, physicians can infer an AD diagnosis using neuropsychological assessments and various biomarkers. AD pathophysiology is measured by the accumulation of Aβ and tau in addition to clinical measures needed to confirm neurological deficits [ 4 , 5 ]. These two biomarkers must be present along with a neuropsychological assessment for final confirmation of diagnosis postmortem [ 6 , 7 ]. Clinical advances have made progress in identifying and tracking the progression of AD using Aβ and tau biomarkers in vivo, with positron emission tomography (PET) being the gold standard for measuring the two pathogenic proteins. With PET scans, AD pathophysiology is tracked by measuring Aβ and tau concentrations in the brain. This allows clinicians to image the spatial deposition of large aggregates formed from the two proteins directly in the brain, which, in the case of amyloid, allows for the confirmation and monitoring of amyloid status, and in the case of tau, allows for Braak staging in vivo. However, taking these measures involves highly invasive procedures that are expensive and technically demanding, creating barriers to their use. The ability to track AD progression is important for outcome assessment in clinical research. The technical and economic demands, as well as the invasive nature of PET and cerebral spinal fluid (CSF) collection, make blood-derived plasma measures a highly desirable alternative [ 8 , 9 ]. Compared to PET scans or lumbar punctures for CSF extraction, drawing blood from patients and isolating the plasma is significantly easier for clinicians and researchers, minimally invasive, well tolerated for most patients, and many times cheaper. Plasma Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio has been shown to be highly predictive of PET amyloid status both through mass spectroscopy and immunoassay approaches [ 8 – 13 ]. Further, plasma Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio has been shown to accurately predict brain amyloid positivity and to be predictive of future brain amyloid positivity in amyloid-negative individuals [ 14 ]. In addition, studies have also reported correlations in plasma Aβ38, Aβ40, and Aβ42 in patients with hereditary (but not sporadic) cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA), a cerebrovascular amyloidosis that often coincides with AD [ 15 , 16 ]. For the purposes of identifying individuals at risk of developing AD, predicting biological and symptomatic disease onset, tracking disease progression, and measuring the target engagement of anti-amyloid interventions, there is significant interest in understanding how plasma amyloid levels change over time. Many clinical trials have utilized measures of plasma Aβ as a biomarker of AD progression to support a drugs’ potential effect on disease modification. Trials for semagacestat, solanezumab, and the FDA-approved lecanemab are notable examples [ 17 – 19 ]. Many longitudinal and clinical studies of plasma Aβ take measurements every 1 to 1.5 years, often alongside more direct CNS measures such as PET imaging or CSF sampling [ 17 , 18 , 20 – 22 ]. These sampling schedules reflect many considerations, such as logistical considerations if blood draws are done alongside CSF draws or PET imaging, or an interest in long-term rather than short-term outcomes, as AD is known to be a slow and progressive disease. Some studies have estimated yearly rates of change in both amyloid PET and plasma Aβ42/Aβ40 in AD patients. At its fastest, plasma Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio decreases at a rate of 7.9% per year, and amyloid PET measures increase at a rate of 8.8% per year [ 23 ]. The purpose of this analysis is to determine the weekly variability in these biomarkers as a preliminary step in a larger project evaluating the relationship between dynamic changes in biomarkers and AD behavioral symptom presentations. Methods Recruitment and sample collection Participants were recruited from two sites: The University of Alabama at Birmingham (UAB) (Institutional Review Board protocol number: IRB-300004828) and The University of Texas Health Science Center at Houston (UTH) (Institutional Review Board protocol number: HSC-SN-23-1000). Participants were recruited from geriatrics and memory disorder clinics at both sites. A diagnosis of AD was based on clinician diagnosis, based on their clinic protocols. Participants were recruited based on ICD codes found in their EHR. Inclusion criteria included (1) persons with a EHR diagnosis of AD with or without vascular involvement; (2) with a AD8 score &gt; = 3 (screener for dementia vs. mild cognitive impairment); (3) who are either Caucasian or African American; and (4) reside in the community (not a nursing home). Participants were excluded if the person with AD also had a diagnosis of Dementia with Lewy-Body, ALS, Parkinson’s disease or Pick’s (2) had a co-occurring terminal illness with &lt; 6 months life expectancy, (3) had taken systemic steroids, anabolic steroids, growth hormone or immunosuppressants within the last 6 months (4) had a chronic infection (e.g., HIV, Hepatitis), (5) had taken probiotic supplements within the last 2 weeks (6) had a feeding tube or other gastric bypass surgery within the last 3 years and (7) could not speak in English. All research activities took place in the participant’s home. On the first home visit, consent was obtained from the dementia patient. If the dementia patient was unable to provide informed consent, their legally authorized representative was able to sign the informed consent document for them if the dementia patient assented to be a part of the study. The University of California, San Diego Brief Assessment of Capacity to Consent (UBACC) [ 24 ] was used to determine decisional capacity to consent to research. This tool demonstrates reliability in samples of people with ADRD ( r = 0.56, p &lt; 0.001). Following previous studies, we will interpreted scores of &gt; 14 as meaning the participant has capacity to consent to participate in the research. Study visits from the research nurse occurred over the next five weeks, at the same time and day each week. At each visit, the nurse obtained blood (approximately 10 ml of blood per visit) for a total of about 50 ml of blood collected at the end of the 5 weeks. Blood was drawn at the patient’s home using standard phlebotomy procedure and equipment, following universal precautions. Blood draws were made into EDTA containing tubes (BD Vacutainer K2 EDTA Additive). Immediately upon draw, the blood tube was placed in a cooler and transported to the laboratory for analysis. Samples were processed &lt;2 h after collection per standard guidelines. Plasma isolation The blood collection tube was centrifuged at 4°C for 20 minutes at 400g. Plasma was then extracted and stored at -80 o C. Aβ40 and Aβ42 immunoassays High Sensitivity Human Amyloid β40 ELISA (Millipore, EZHS40) and High Sensitivity Human Amyloid β42 ELISA (Millipore, EZHS42) were utilized to quantify plasma Aβ42 and Aβ40 content. Importantly both kits are validated for use in human plasma. All ELISA kits were performed according to manufacturer instructions and absorbance was quantified using a Spectramax ID3 instrument. Briefly, samples were incubated on antibody coated plates for 16hr at 4°C, washed 5 times and treated with secondary antibody at room temperature for 1 hours. The plate was washed 5 times and incubated with stabilized chromogen. The HRP reaction was terminated through the addition of sulfuric acid and absorbance quantified at 450nm and 590nm. Participant samples were assayed in batches with all samples from a single participant in the same batch. Each batch included two internal quality control samples (IQC). IQC reading were within an acceptable range within all batches (Fig S1). Data analysis Plasma concentrations of Aβ42 and Aβ40, as well as their ratio (Aβ42/Aβ40), were analyzed across five weekly timepoints for each participant. Summary statistics were calculated to describe central tendency and variability within and between subjects. For comparisons across demographic groups (sex and race), we employed two-way ANOVA models with interaction terms, and post hoc Tukey tests for multiple comparisons where applicable. For total level of variability, we calculated the percent change from the participant’s median score. For week-to-week fluctuations we calculated variation from the prior week. This allowed us to track both episodic and progressive trends. In a secondary analysis, we also calculated week-to-week change relative to the previous week to assess the magnitude and frequency of short-term fluctuations. Linear regression models were used to assess the relationship between baseline levels of Aβ peptides and magnitude of fluctuation over time. In these models, sex and race were included as covariates, as well as their interaction terms. P -values &lt; 0.05 were considered significant. Of the 73 participants who completed the study 13 had missing weeks. Missing weeks at the start or end of the study were excluded. Missing weeks in the middle of the study were imputed as the average of the two adjacent weeks. In total we imputed weeks in 11 participants. Imputed weeks are highlighted in Fig. S4. Calculation of CV, identification of outliers, as well as determination of normality and steady state was performed following established guidelines [ 25 ]. R [ 26 ] statistical software was used to perform these analyses. For data manipulation tidyr [ 27 ] and dplyr [ 28 ] packages were used. In order to test for the steady state, lme4 [ 29 ] was used. Outliers were handled using the outliers [ 30 ] package in R. Other simple analyses including one-way and two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), Analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) and linear regression were performed using the VassarStats statistical package. Graphs depict both % change from prior week ‘Week-To-Week’ and difference between highest and lowest reading over 5-week study course for each participant ‘Study course’ Discussion Our findings present an intriguing look into the weekly fluctuations of plasma Aβ concentrations, specifically Aβ42 and Aβ40, and more importantly their ratio over a five-week micro-longitudinal scale. In summary, plasma measurements of Aβ42, Aβ40, and their ratio, each show natural within-subject fluctuations ranging from less than 10% to more than 50% on a week-to-week basis and over the course of 5 weeks in AD patients. These fluctuations are largely episodic in nature, with baseline levels often being restored shortly after large shifts and not correlating with progressive increases or decreases compared to baseline within the time frame sampled. Though these sudden shifts in plasma amyloid content appear episodic in the 5-week time window, it cannot be discounted that they may be part of a pattern that requires weekly monitoring during a larger time window to detect. Larger time windows with this frequency of sampling is an area for future studies to improve upon. The relevance of plasma Aβ sampling is underscored by its frequent use in clinical trials as a biomarker to monitor the effect of anti-amyloid compounds on the disease course. For instance, in a phase 3 trial of the gamma-secretase inhibitor semagacestat, reductions in plasma Aβ42 and Aβ40 were reported on a scale of 5%-48% from baseline to week 76 or early termination in the treatment groups [ 17 ]. Trials of the anti-amyloid antibody solanezumab measured plasma amyloid slightly more frequently, once at baseline, week 12, and week 80, and found statistically significant increases at week 12 and week 80 for both amyloid peptide variants in the treatment groups [ 18 ]. For the FDA-approved lecanemab, plasma Aβ42/Aβ40 was measured at baseline, week 53, and week 79, and change from baseline to the end of the study for the various treatment groups ranged from approximately 20–90%, supporting the drugs’ potentially disease-modifying effect [ 20 ]. However, our studies find that natural weekly fluctuations in plasma amyloid levels can be substantial, sometimes exceeding 50%, calling into question the reliability of the infrequent testing intervals common in clinical trials. These natural variations suggest that without more frequent monitoring, it may be challenging to determine whether changes in amyloid levels are due to therapeutic intervention or natural variability. We document here that weekly variability can be as pronounced as variability measured between several months to 1.5 years. We thus recommend that findings from plasma amyloid measures be cautiously interpreted as the dynamics of temporal plasma amyloid fluctuations are not well understood and must be studied further. Plasma Aβ levels are reported as poorly correlated with CSF [ 34 ]. One major reason is that blood Aβ can originate from peripheral sources rather than reflecting only central nervous system pathology. APP is widely expressed outside the brain, and Aβ is produced in many peripheral tissues (skeletal muscle, pancreas, kidney, liver, vascular walls, etc.), with platelets releasing substantial Aβ into circulation [ 35 ]. Moreover, plasma Aβ is largely bound to carrier proteins and blood cells, and it is cleared mainly via the liver and kidneys [ 36 ]. In older, frail patients with dementia, these peripheral production and clearance pathways may be altered – for example, reduced renal function has been linked to higher plasma Aβ levels [ 36 ], further increasing the variability of plasma measurements. Consequently, plasma Aβ changes may only partially reflect brain amyloid status, underscoring the limitations of blood Aβ as a surrogate for CNS amyloid burden. To our knowledge, this study has the highest frequency of sample measures over the shortest period of time. Other longitudinal studies tend to measure plasma amyloid less frequently (6–12 months) and over significantly longer time periods (1–2 years). For example, one study investigated the temporal trajectories of plasma amyloid-beta and tau in subjects with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and AD compared to healthy control subjects. In that study, blood was drawn at baseline and once annually for 3 years [ 22 ]. They found that plasma Aβ markers change significantly within patients in a time-dependent manner over the course of AD stages, whereas tau markers differed mostly based on diagnostic classification. Our findings are in agreement as we document here much faster and more dramatic time-dependent changes in plasma Aβ measures than what many in the field may have expected. Another study examined the plasma Aβ42/40 ratio at baseline, 18 months, and 36 months. They found that the Aβ42/40 ratio decreases over time in mildly cognitively impaired individuals compared to cognitively unimpaired individuals, whereas the AD patients showed a decrease in the first 18 months and a near return to initial levels in month 36 [ 21 ]. Our findings are consistent with those of this study as it pertains to the AD group. Our data shows that in AD patients the plasma Aβ42/40 ratio may see substantial change in either direction on a week-to-week basis. Their data shows that over the course of 18 months, AD patients may have their plasma Aβ42/40 ratio increase or decrease by comparable amounts. Our data indicates that such a finding may easily have been captured with a longitudinal design that is much shorter in length, and calls into question how informative measurements of plasma amyloid taken months and years apart are if week-to-week measurements show differences of comparable magnitudes. It is becoming increasingly reported that in AD patients, the plasma Aβ42/40 ratio is lower compared to controls [ 37 ]. Our study does not include a non-AD comparison group, and thus we are unable to confirm or challenge these cross-sectional findings. However, given the extreme rate of change we observe in plasma Aβ42/40 ratios within AD patients, it may also be the case that short-term fluctuation of plasma amyloid-beta levels is itself an important distinguishing feature between AD and non-AD populations. This is an area for future research that will require micro-longitudinal comparisons to healthy controls. Though such comparisons are not included in the present study, a recent investigation using a similar micro-longitudinal design examined plasma amyloid biomarkers in a cohort of 20 non-AD individuals aged 40–60 from various European countries, collecting samples weekly for 10 weeks [ 25 ]. That study found minimal within-subject variation in Aβ42 and Aβ40 levels (less than 10%), and even less variability in Aβ42/40 ratios (less than 5%), suggesting a relatively stable plasma amyloid profile in non-AD individuals. Notably, however, their study used the Simoa (Quanterix) platform, which is currently considered the more appropriate and recommended method for estimating amyloid-beta levels in blood. This difference in analytical methodology might partially explain the discrepancy between their findings and ours. Nonetheless, our data from AD patients exhibit substantial intra-individual fluctuations in plasma amyloid measures, in stark contrast to the stability reported in non-AD participants. While a direct comparison cannot be made due to differences in cohort composition, ethnicity, and methodology, these contrasting findings highlight the potential utility of dynamic changes in plasma Aβ42/40 as a disease-specific signature. A study explicitly designed to compare short-term variability in plasma amyloid between AD and non-AD individuals using standardized methodology will be a valuable next step. Current preliminary evidence suggests that such variability may be a clinically meaningful biomarker of AD pathophysiology. Our study also explored demographic influences on amyloid measurements, revealing intriguing differences by race and sex. Our findings show that there is a significant interaction effect between race and sex in the measurement of Aβ42 and Aβ40 individually, but not the ratio of the two peptide variants. In all 3 amyloid measures, sex has significant main effects, but race does not. African American males exhibited the highest average concentration of both Aβ42 and Aβ40, suggesting the data from this group is driving the interaction effects. Males of both races have higher average Aβ42 and Aβ40 as well as higher ratios than females of the same race. Females of both races do not significantly differ from each other on any measure. We hypothesized that sex difference may be a product of age. Indeed, we found a significant association between plasma amyloid and age. However, we found aging to produce significantly different impact on plasma amyloid in males and females. Furthermore, we find there was no significant differences in week-to-week Aβ42/Aβ40 variability between race or sex nor any interaction effects. Thus, whereas sex and race produce differences in direct amyloid measures, they do not produce differences in week-to-week variability of Aβ42/Aβ40. Previous work has reported that African Americans had lower Aβ40 and higher Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio measurements relative to Caucasians [ 38 ]. This is in contrast to the findings of this study, which finds African American males have higher levels of both Aβ42 and Aβ40 than Caucasian males, but no significant differences in the ratio. The previous study that finds racial differences in the direct measurement of plasma amyloid used a substantially higher sample size than ours but a much lower ratio of African Americans to Caucasians (324 African Americans, 1,547 Caucasians), so it is possible that our differing results are an artifact of statistical power and study design. In line with best practices for BV studies, we used a BIVAC-consistent CV-ANOVA framework with multilevel outlier handling and steady-state verification. Consistent with BIVAC principles emphasizing careful control of analytical and preanalytical factors, we standardized sampling and processing, and estimated analytical and within-person components using recommended approaches. Consistent with established BV methodology, our within-person analyses revealed only minimal outliers, indicating that the repeated measures within individuals were stable and reliable across the five-week sampling period. This supports the technical robustness of our dataset and suggests that the variability we observe primarily reflects true biological shifts rather than measurement noise. We also followed BIVAC guidance to test for outliers at the subject level and to report the number of excluded observations; two within-person outlier measurements were removed, and we transparently retained all participants in the between-person analyses. We deliberately did not exclude participants flagged as between-person outliers. Our rationale is empirical and ethical. Empirically, the participants flagged as extreme values were disproportionately Black men in our racially diverse, clinic-based AD sample. Treating these observations as removable anomalies risks discarding signal that may reflect meaningful population heterogeneity in plasma Aβ biology. Ethically, routine removal of subgroup-specific extremes can encode algorithmic bias, systematically erasing underrepresented groups from parameter estimation, inflating apparent homogeneity, and propagating thresholds and reference ranges that underperform in those groups. Notably, the BV reference study for AD biomarkers we cite [ 25 ] was conducted in 20 healthy, 40 to 60 year old White participants sampled weekly for 10 weeks using highly standardized procedures. Those data are invaluable for methodologic exemplars and for healthy reference BV; however, their demographic and clinical scope differs materially from our older, AD, racially diverse cohort measured by ELISA. Directly porting between-subject outlier removal rules from such a homogeneous, healthy cohort to a heterogeneous, disease cohort may therefore suppress real, clinically relevant, between-group variability. In keeping with BIVAC’s core premise that BV estimates should be reliable and representative of the population and setting to which they are applied, our approach prioritizes population-appropriate estimation and transparent reporting over post hoc exclusion. We contend that BV parameters (CVI, CVG, RCV) and decision limits should be estimated and transparently reported with attention to race and sex, and that subgroup-aware models, rather than post hoc exclusion, are essential to prevent bias and to advance equitable biomarker science. A key strength of this study lies in its design, which specifically incorporates stratified sampling by race to test for racial differences. The naturally high rate of variability in plasma amyloid measurements of AD patients does have implications for the design of future intervention studies. First, high variability may necessitate more complex trial designs, such as crossover designs, where each participant can serve as their own control reducing between-subject variability. Additionally, larger sample sizes may be needed to detect true changes related to the intervention, rather than inherent natural variability. The observed variability in this study can inform more precise power analyses for future studies. In conclusion, the pronounced weekly fluctuations in plasma amyloid levels documented in our study underscore the potential benefits of frequent monitoring in AD research. Such an approach can improve disease monitoring of dementia patients in various clinical and research contexts. Future studies should consider employing frequent sampling schedules to explore these dynamics further, particularly across different demographic and diagnostic groups and in conjunction with other relevant biomarkers like tau and neurofilament light with larger sample sizes than what is presented here. These efforts will be crucial for improving our ability to monitor disease progression and the many clinical and research applications that are contingent upon being able to track the disease. Limitations A limitation of this study is the use of ELISA as the method for quantification of the Aβ40 and Aβ42. ELISA is typically not used for plasma amyloid measurement in Alzheimer’s disease studies, so its use here may limit the interpretability of our findings. An additional limitation is the reliance on ICD codes for inclusion criteria, as we recognize that there is variation between providers and clinics in diagnosing, and that inclusion of PET and CSF markers substantially improves diagnostic accuracy. As this study was impacted by COVID-19, with recruitment beginning in 2020, stricter inclusion criteria was not feasible due to the decrease in in-person visits.
