Elucidating the phase 1 trial experience among study participants following completion of the INTERCEPT-AD study of sabirnetug (ACU193) for early Alzheimer’s disease: a qualitative interview study

Background Recruitment and retention remain persistent challenges in Alzheimer’s disease (AD) clinical trials, particularly as studies increasingly focus on earlier disease stages and require longer participation and more invasive procedures. Understanding how trial enrollment and participation are experienced is critical to improving acceptability and sustaining engagement. Qualitative interviews conducted alongside clinical trials offer an opportunity to capture participant and study partner perspectives on the trial experience. In INTERCEPT-AD, a phase 1 trial evaluating safety and tolerability of the Aβ oligomer-selective monoclonal antibody sabirnetug (ACU193) among participants with mild cognitive impairment or mild dementia due to Alzheimer’s disease (AD), semi-structured qualitative interviews concerning trial experience were conducted with a subset of participants and their study partners. Methods Participant/study partner dyads completed qualitative interviews following the final study visit. A semi-structured interview guide elicited descriptions regarding motivations for participating, enrollment decision-making, and positive and negative aspects of participation. Principles of applied qualitative thematic analysis guided the qualitative analyses. Exploratory analyses examined how factors differ by participant gender. Results Twenty-eight participants (64.2% female) and their study partners were interviewed, representing 43% of the trial population ( n = 65; 53.8% female). Participants and study partner dyads described varied pathways to trial awareness and enrollment decision-making, including both independent and family-involved decisions. Motivations for participation reflected anticipated personal benefit as well as altruistic goals. While interactions with study staff were viewed positively, dyads reported meaningful burdens related to travel, time commitment, and study procedures, with cognitive testing more frequently described as challenging than invasive procedures. A recurring theme was the desire for clearer communication and greater access to study-related information, including test results and treatment assignment. Exploratory analyses suggested that perceived burden and enrollment decision-making may differ by participant gender. Conclusions Study findings suggest opportunities to enhance the AD trial experience by addressing trial-related burdens and logistical aspects of participation. Exploratory gender analyses yielded additional insight into the patient trial experience but should be further examined along with race/ethnicity and study partner characteristics to enhance clinical study design and execution. Background Participant recruitment and retention pose significant challenges to the successful completion of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) clinical trials [ 1 ]. Recent therapeutic advances have led to an increase in the quantity of AD trials for disease modifying therapies (DMTs), necessitating both a greater number of participants overall and greater time commitment by each participant [ 2 ]. Moreover, more recent trials have shifted the focus to early AD. In January 2024, there were 164 registered AD trials, with DMTs (biologics and small molecules) accounting for the majority (75%) of all investigational treatments [ 3 ]. Among these participant diagnoses were mild, moderate, or severe AD dementia (46% of trials), early AD (30%), and mild cognitive impairment (MCI) with or without confirmation of AD pathology (26%). Phase 3 trials of DMTs require the largest number of participants, with a total of 28,671 individuals needed to populate currently registered trials. Additionally, trial length, particularly of those investigating biologic DMTs, poses a potential challenge to participant retention. Mean treatment exposure periods for biologic DMTs were 1.5 years for non-prevention trials, and 3.8 years for prevention trials. Trials for cognitive enhancers (symptomatic treatments) had a mean duration of 4.5 months. Notably, a review of completed AD trials found that for trials of mild-to-moderate AD, every 6-month increase in duration was associated with a 34% decrease in the odds of participants completing the study; for mild cognitive impairment (MCI) trials, odds of participants completing the study were reduced by 20% [ 4 ]. The number of study procedures has also increased with therapeutic advancement; trials for DMTs commonly require imaging studies and/or lumbar punctures (LPs), procedures that participants may perceive as invasive. Understanding participant experiences of these demands is increasingly important to improving trial acceptability and supporting recruitment and retention, particularly in early-phase AD studies. Feedback from participants in early-phase clinical trials may be used to aid recruitment and retention in subsequent trials. Recent studies of the experiences of participants in AD trials found several common motivations for AD trial participation; notably, participants expressed altruistic motivations across all studies, as well as a desire to benefit themselves [ 1 , 5 – 8 ]. However, no recent reports contained findings specific to those who participated in early-phase clinical trials. Examination of findings stratified by participant gender or other variables is also limited. Gender-related differences in motivations for clinical trial participation and in perceptions of study burden could play a role in study enrollment. Women account for more AD cases than men; in the United States (USA), it is estimated that 66% of those with AD are women [ 9 ]. While women often account for more of the participants in AD trials than men, the percentage of women in AD trials is less than the percentage of women in the affected population (a difference of 4.56%, p &lt; 0.001) [ 10 ]. Understanding gender differences in the decision-making process surrounding enrollment in AD clinical trials and the perceived burden and benefits of clinical trial participation may provide insights into motivating enrollment and mitigating study-related burdens. We conducted semi-structured qualitative exit interviews among a subset of participants and their study partners as part of INTERCEPT-AD, a phase 1 randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study investigating the safety and tolerability of the monoclonal antibody sabirnetug (ACU193) in MCI or mild dementia due to AD [ 11 ]. In the present study, results regarding the decision-making process prior to trial enrollment and the subsequent trial experience are reported, both overall and according to participant gender. Methods Objectives The primary objective of this study was to characterize participant and study partner descriptions of their decision-making processes prior to trial enrollment and their perceptions of the subsequent trial experience. An exploratory objective was to compare these results by study participant gender. INTERCEPT-AD INTERCEPT-AD was a phase 1, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, single- and multiple-dose study investigating the safety, tolerability, and pharmacokinetics of intravenous (IV) sabirnetug in MCI or mild dementia due to AD (clinicaltrials.gov, NCT04931459 , registered June 18, 2021, https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04931459 ) [ 11 ]. The study was conducted at 15 study sites in the USA from June 23, 2021, to June 12, 2023. In the single ascending dose (SAD) portion of the study, 32 participants were randomized across four cohorts in a 6:2 ratio to receive a single IV infusion of sabirnetug (2, 10, 25, or 60 mg/kg) or placebo. The SAD portion included ten visits over approximately 140 days, with a required inpatient stay of at least 48 h following infusion on Day 1. In the multiple ascending dose (MAD) portion of the study, 33 participants were randomized across three cohorts in an 8:2 ratio to receive a total of three IV infusions of sabirnetug (10 mg/kg every 4 weeks [Q4W]; 60 mg/kg Q4W; or 25 mg/kg every 2 weeks [Q2W]) or placebo. In the MAD portion of the study, 6–7 visits occurred over 24–28 weeks, and no inpatient stay was required. Among other criteria, participants were required to be between 55 and 90 years of age and meet the National Institute on Aging–Alzheimer’s Association criteria for MCI or mild dementia due to AD, have a Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) score between 18 and 30 (inclusive), a Global Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR-GS) score of 0.5 or 1.0, and confirmed elevated brain amyloid by amyloid positron emission tomography (PET) scan. Notably, LPs were required of all participants. Additional details regarding the study design and results from INTERCEPT-AD have been published [ 11 ]. Sabirnetug is currently being evaluated in the ongoing phase 2 trial ALTITUDE-AD ( NCT06335173 ). Exit interview guide development A semi-structured interview guide was developed to explore the study topics of interest. Semi-structured interviews are common qualitative data collection methods used to obtain rich patient descriptions and typically include open-ended questions with accompanying probes to elicit a full range of experiences [ 12 ]. The interviews explored the decision-making process preceding trial enrollment and the overall trial experience. To obtain descriptions concerning the decision-making process preceding trial enrollment, questions focused on reasons for trial participation, who was involved in the decision to participate, and pre-trial concerns regarding trial participation and/or the study medication. Questions regarding trial experience examined the overall experience, study procedures, and visits. Although patient advisors were not involved in the formal development of the interview guide, the guide consisted of broad, open-ended questions designed to elicit participants’ experiences without imposing predefined assumptions. This approach allowed themes to emerge inductively from participant and study partner narratives. Prior to implementation, the semi-structured interview guide was piloted with two team members as mock interviewees to (1) gauge the length of the interview, (2) identify areas to reduce or expand probing depending on length, and (3) identify any need for restructuring of questions to streamline interview flow. Following the mock interviews, two questions were re-worded. The finalized interview guide was translated into Spanish via the forward–backward method to accommodate Spanish-speaking participants. The portions of the interview guide containing questions regarding enrollment decision-making and trial experience are available within the Supplementary Materials. Exit interview procedures The sample for the interviews was obtained via convenience sampling of the main clinical trial cohort. All participants who completed the INTERCEPT-AD study and their study partners were eligible to participate in the optional exit interviews if they signed the exit interview informed consent form (ICF). Participants who provided consent were assigned study IDs that were unique to the interview sub-study and separate from those used in the main trial. Participant gender was ascertained from the baseline characteristics obtained from the main INTERCEPT-AD study and does not distinguish between gender identity or sex assigned at birth. Dyads were identified as female or male based on the gender of the participant. The gender of the study partner was not collected. All interviews were performed by personnel with previous training and experience in qualitative research. Interviews were conducted via videoconference within 7 days after the end-of-study visit (Day 140–196, depending on SAD or MAD cohort). At the time of the interview, participants and their study partners verbally confirmed that they signed the ICF and provided permission to record the interview. All interviews took place at the residence of the study participant, and only the interviewer, participant, and study partner were present. Interviews were scheduled to last approximately 90 min, including initial warm-up questions to establish rapport. Next, the participant and study partner were interviewed together. Interviewers first directed questions toward the participant and reminded the study partner that they would have an opportunity to add information or different perspectives. Following this, the study partner was interviewed without the participant present and was asked to provide their perceptions of the participant’s trial experience. Each dyad received one electronic gift card of $100 as remuneration. Data analysis Interview recordings were professionally transcribed and imported into NVivo 14 for coding and analysis [ 13 ]. Interview transcripts were analyzed using principles of applied thematic analysis [ 14 – 16 ] and followed a hybrid deductive–inductive process, in which interview guide topics informed initial analytic domains, while patterns and themes were identified inductively within and across those domains [ 17 ]. Transcripts were coded iteratively by two team members; coding results were reviewed from every third to fifth transcript to identify discrepancies, ensure consistency across analysts, add new codes to capture new domains, review definitions, and revise the codebook structure as applicable. Subsequent analyses focused on identifying recurring patterns and themes in how dyads described their motivations and experiences. Results were first examined in the entire interview study cohort, after which results were compared between participant genders. For this, study participant/study partner dyads were defined as female or male according to the gender of the study participant as it was captured in the main INTERCEPT-AD study protocol. Study partner characteristics, including gender, were not obtained. Concept saturation, defined as the point during data collection in which no new relevant information is obtained [ 18 ], was based on concepts emerging for factors regarding trial participation and was assessed in the overall sample. Saturation was examined by dividing the interviews into seven groups with four interviews each. A saturation table was created and organized by concept codes to document each concept in successive interviews [ 14 ]. Finally, since the main analyses reflect interview contributions from the dyad as a unit (often with heterogeneous gender pairing), an exploratory analysis was conducted to evaluate the impact of the speaker (i.e., participant or study partner). Specifically, the main findings were compared to those when the concepts/themes were provided by the participant (i.e., the participant verbalized them). This enabled the assessment of whether results obtained using the dyad as an analytic unit were congruent with results derived primarily from participants only. For these analyses, results are presented in terms of the study participant. For the main analyses, results are presented in terms of the participant/study partner dyad. Results Sample characteristics Of the 65 participants randomized in INTERCEPT-AD, 45 were invited to participate in the exit interviews; the 20 remaining participants had completed their end-of-study visit prior to implementation of the interviews. A total of 28 participant and study partner dyads consented to participate in the exit interviews. Regarding participant demographics, mean age was 70.8 years (standard deviation (SD) 7.2) and ranged from 56 to 85 years (median = 71). Most participants were White (96.4%) and not Hispanic (96.4%); 18 participants were women (64.3%). One participant completed the interview in Spanish. Demographic characteristics were similar to the overall INTERCEPT-AD cohort in terms of age and race, although the percentage of female and Hispanic representation were higher and lower, respectively, among the interview study cohort (64.3% female and 3.6% Hispanic for the interview study cohort versus 53.8% and 15.4% for INTERCEPT-AD overall). Participants were also similar in terms of mean MMSE scores at study screening (23.8 versus 24.3 for the interview cohort and the overall INTERCEPT-AD cohort, respectively). A higher percentage of the interview cohort had CDR-GS scores of 1.0 at baseline (28.6%) versus the overall INTERCEPT-AD cohort (22.6%). A total of 16 and 12 participants were in the SAD and MAD portions of the study, respectively; most participants were from cohorts 3, 4, 6, and 7, followed by cohorts 5 and 2 (there were no participants from cohort 1). Within the interview study cohort, demographic characteristics were similar between genders, although female participants had a higher mean age (71.6 years, SD 7.5) than male participants (69.3 years, SD 6.8). Additionally, a higher percentage of women had CDR-GS scores of 1.0 at baseline (38.9%) than men (10.0%), whereas a higher percentage of men had scores of 0.5 (90.0% versus 61.1% for women). Although physicians were a common source of study awareness, they were rarely described as playing an active role in the decision to participate. Across narratives, decision-making reflected varying levels of autonomy. Several participants relied on family involvement to help evaluate study enrollment, whereas others described making the decision independently, emphasizing personal ownership over treatment choices. Motivations for and concerns prior to enrollment A central theme was the hope of personal benefit, including slowing disease progression or gaining access to new treatment options. Dyads also expressed a desire to help others living with AD, either by contributing to research or supporting future patients. Additional motivations included taking proactive steps early in the disease course and building personal understanding of AD through close monitoring and trial engagement. Dyads also noted apprehensions, most commonly centering on the potential side effects of treatment. Concerns related to study procedures (e.g., LPs, imaging, or blood draws), the time commitment, and the possibility of receiving placebo emerged less frequently but demonstrated the weighing of risks and benefits that preceded enrollment decisions. Participating in INTERCEPT-AD Dyads described both benefits and burdens associated with participation in the INTERCEPT-AD trial (Tables 2 and 3 ). Analysis identified two overarching themes: positive experiences related to study care and support, and logistical, procedural, and information-related challenges associated with trial participation. Positive aspects of trial participation A majority of dyads expressed appreciation for the quality of care, communication, and empathy demonstrated by study staff. Many described feeling supported throughout the study, citing responsiveness to questions, clear explanations of procedures, and a general sense of compassion and attentiveness. These interactions were noted as providing comfort and reassurance. Burdens and challenges of trial participation Dyads identified several challenges associated with participation. These burdens centered around four primary domains: travel and logistics, time commitment, study procedures, and limited access to study-related information. Travel distance and logistics were the most frequently cited challenges. Many dyads described long travel times to study sites or imaging facilities, often requiring overnight stays. Several questioned why procedures could not be performed closer to home, particularly when local facilities were available. Time demands and scheduling challenges also emerged as a significant source of burden. Dyads noted the lengthy inpatient stays, extended clinic visits with long waiting periods between procedures, and short-notice scheduling of visits, which complicated their daily responsibilities. Participants additionally expressed procedural burdens, particularly related to cognitive testing, blood draws, and LPs. Cognitive assessments were frequently described as stressful or frustrating and made cognitive difficulties more salient to participants. Blood draws and prolonged venous access were described as physically uncomfortable, while LPs elicited anxiety due to their invasive nature. Finally, dyads reported a desire for greater access to information, including results of imaging or cognitive assessments and clarification regarding treatment assignment following study completion. While many acknowledged the constraints of clinical trial blinding, the lack of feedback nonetheless contributed to frustration. Exploratory analyses: gender Exploratory analyses suggested several gender-related patterns in decision-making, motivations for participation, and perceived trial burdens (Table 2 ). Decision-making and motivations for enrollment Female dyads more often described making the decision to enroll independently, whereas male dyads more frequently reported seeking input or assistance from family members during the decision-making process. Differences also emerged in stated motivations for trial participation. Female dyads more commonly emphasized altruistic motivations, such as contributing to research that may benefit others, as well as a desire to gain more information about AD, compared with male dyads. Perceived burdens of trial participation Gender-based differences were also observed in how trial-related burdens were experienced. Male dyads more frequently described the length of study visits as burdensome, whereas female dyads more often highlighted challenges related to cognitive testing and blood draws. These differences suggest variation in which aspects of trial participation were perceived as most taxing. Information needs Male dyads more commonly expressed a desire for additional information regarding imaging results and treatment group assignment following study participation, while this concern was raised less frequently among female dyads. Overall, these exploratory findings suggest that gender may influence both how trial participation decisions are made and how specific aspects of clinical trial participation are experienced. Exploratory analyses: results by speaker Exploratory analyses examined how findings differed when analytic focus was restricted to concepts articulated by participants themselves, compared with analyses that incorporated input from the dyad level (Table 4 ). As expected, fewer concepts were identified when only participant-voiced content was considered; this attenuation was most pronounced for burdens experienced during trial participation. When analyses were limited to participant input, burdens included distance to clinics, cognitive testing, and blood draws. In contrast, inclusion of study partner perspectives yielded nine additional burden-related concepts, highlighting the complementary role study partners played in describing trial-related challenges. Notably, the desire for greater access to information , including imaging results and treatment assignment, was voiced predominantly by study partners rather than participants. Discussion Participants and their study partners reported a broad array of factors related to participation in the INTERCEPT-AD study including referral sources, decision-making resources and motivations, as well as burdens experienced during the study. Overall, our findings are consistent with those of published studies of the motivations and experiences of AD trial participants. These previous studies were conducted in the USA and European Union (EU) and included participants who were cognitively normal or who had MCI or AD; methods to elicit participant information varied and included written surveys and interviews. Despite methodological and geographic differences, several themes were consistent across studies. Findings unanimously reported altruistic motivations for trial participation, as well as participation for self-benefit [ 5 – 8 , 19 , 20 ], and being motivated by the desire to learn more about AD [ 7 , 19 ]. Fewer studies reported perceived trial-related burdens or barriers to participation, although those expressed by study participants were largely congruent with findings from the present study. Notably, concepts related to inconvenience (i.e., distance to clinics and time commitment) were reported as both a barrier to and negative aspect of participation [ 8 , 19 ]. Regarding other factors, Ottenhof et al. [ 8 ] reported that lack of communication about study results, LPs, and cognitive assessments were ranked as the most negative among participants in the EU. Similarly, Gabel et al. [ 7 ] found that when trial participants were asked for suggestions for improving the study experience, the most common responses included the desire to receive feedback about their results from the trial assessments. Importantly, the study site staff was cited as a positive aspect of the trial experience who facilitated study retention across most studies [ 5 , 7 , 8 ]. Our finding that the majority of participants spontaneously voiced appreciation for the site staff emphasizes their importance in the overall trial experience. Cox et al. [ 19 ] is the only previous study which, like our study, focused on participants with MCI. They assessed the role of the study partner in decision-making around clinical trial enrollment among a subset of participant/study partner dyads who were part of a longitudinal cohort study. When asked about deciding to enroll in a hypothetical trial, most participants and study partners indicated that they would make the decision jointly; study participants who reported that they would make the decision independently had exclusively non-spousal study partners (4/25 participants). In the present study, 25% of the participant sample reported making the decision to enroll in INTERCEPT-AD independently, all of whom were women. Furthermore, Cox et al. [ 19 ] found that more study partners than study participants cited time commitment as a barrier to trial participation; this was more frequently a concern among non-spousal versus spousal study partners. In the present study, exploratory participant-based analyses similarly indicated that concerns around time commitment were primarily expressed by the study partner. Although it was not possible to ascertain participant/study partner relationships in the present study, our findings, taken together with those of Cox et al. [ 19 ], emphasize the importance of the study partner in AD clinical trial enrollment decision-making. Additionally, findings suggest that barriers to participation and retention may vary according to the relationship between participant and study partner and should be further explored to increase recruitment of non-spousal dyads. The primary limitation of the present study is reflective of the shortcomings regarding participant diversity and inclusion across the current AD clinical trial landscape. The underrepresentation of participants of ethnic and racial minorities in AD trials in relation to their real-world disease burden is well acknowledged, as are the widespread barriers to their enrollment in terms of both recruitment and study eligibility [ 21 , 22 ]. In INTERCEPT-AD, the degree of ethnic diversity present among screened individuals was greater than among those randomized; ethnically diverse participants had a higher screen fail rate (88%) than White/non-Hispanic participants (60%) and a higher rate of negative amyloid PET results (68% versus 52%) [ 23 ]. Among those interviewed, the lack of participants of ethnic and racial minority may result in limited generalizability of findings. Indeed, the experiences and motivations of AD trial participants of ethnic and racial minority have not been sufficiently characterized and may differ from currently published findings that are derived from predominately White/Caucasian samples [ 7 ]. Gender analyses reported in the present study are exploratory in nature, and these results may evolve with larger sample sizes to enable achievement of concept saturation. Participant gender was ascertained from the baseline characteristics obtained from the main INTERCEPT-AD study and does not distinguish between gender identity or sex assigned at birth. These variables are being collected in the phase 2 ALTITUDE-AD study and should be considered in future analyses. The type of relationship between the participant and the study partner should also be considered. Next, the present study was designed to capture experiences through participant–study partner dyads, recognizing the interdependent nature of decision-making and trial participation in early AD. Although interviews were conducted with both members of the dyad present, the study was not designed to formally characterize interviews as being primarily participant-led versus study partner-led. As such, we are unable to systematically assess how narrative dominance may have influenced the content of interviews. Notably, exploratory analyses comparing participant-voiced versus study partner-voiced concepts suggest that study partners more frequently articulated logistical and informational challenges, whereas participants more often described experiential and cognitive burdens. These findings highlight the complementary roles of participants and study partners and underscore the need for future research explicitly designed to examine how leadership within dyadic interviews may shape trial-related narratives and implications for recruiting individuals with early AD. Finally, results are reflective of AD trial participants who completed a phase 1 study and may differ from the perspective of those who discontinued prematurely, as well as those who participate in later phase studies or studies of longer duration. Conclusions Motivations and study burdens reported by participants in the INTERCEPT-AD trial were largely consistent with previous reports, despite the range of cognitive impairment among the participants studied. In this study, gender differences were found in trial experiences, perceived burden surrounding study procedures and logistics, decision-making processes prior to trial enrollment, and in motivations surrounding the decision to enroll in an early-phase AD clinical trial. Taken together, these findings highlight opportunities to refine early-phase AD trial design and conduct in ways that may better support participant engagement, recruitment, and retention.
