Objective diagnosis and treatment outcome prediction in major depressive disorder based on a machine learning framework with dried-serum NIRS digital biomarkers

Background Major depressive disorder (MDD) involves complex interactions across multiple physiological systems, necessitating a comprehensive and integrative perspective for objective diagnosis and personalized treatment. Although multi-omics technologies provide broad molecular insights, their high cost and complexity limit clinical translation. Methods We developed a cost-effective and practical machine learning framework using digital biomarkers from dried serum near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS) for MDD diagnosis and antidepressant treatment outcome prediction. A total of 126 MDD patients and 86 healthy controls were enrolled. Six feature selection methods were systematically compared via rigorous nested cross-validation, and the final models were constructed using partial least squares discriminant analysis, evaluated with decision curve analysis (DCA), and deployed as a clinical diagnostic and decision-support web tool. Results Dried serum NIRS provided sufficient biochemical information to support the development of accurate diagnostic and treatment outcome prediction models. Competitive adaptive reweighted sampling (CARS) combined with SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) emerged as the best-performing approach for identifying decisive wavelengths. The diagnostic model achieved an AUC of 0.91 (95% CI: 0.86–0.95, accuracy 0.85, sensitivity 0.87, specificity 0.83), and the treatment outcome prediction model reached an AUC of 0.90 (95% CI: 0.80–0.97, accuracy 0.84, sensitivity 0.83, specificity 0.84). DCA confirmed substantial net clinical benefit across wide probability ranges. SHAP analysis revealed that the diagnostic model was associated with multiple biomolecular components, especially proteins, whereas the treatment outcome prediction model was primarily influenced by proteins and carbohydrates. Conclusions To our knowledge, this study is the first to comprehensively characterize dried-serum NIRS and demonstrate its potential utility in psychiatry, paving a promising avenue for spectral digital biomarker-based precision diagnosis and personalized treatment. Introduction Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a serious and disabling mental illness affecting 4.4% of the global population [ 1 ]. Despite its substantial disease burden, the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms remain incompletely understood. Current diagnosis relies heavily on subjective symptom assessment and clinical scales, leading to considerable rates of missed diagnosis and misdiagnosis, particularly in regions with limited access to specialized psychiatric care [ 2 ]. Moreover, more than 70% of patients do not receive the most suitable antidepressant under the conventional trial-and-error treatment approach [ 3 ]. These challenges underscore the urgent need for rapid, objective, and accurate methods to support both the diagnosis and choice of antidepressant strategies. The pathogenesis of MDD has been explained by several mechanistic hypotheses, including those centered on neurotransmitter systems, hypothalamic‒pituitary‒adrenal axis dysfunction, immune‒inflammatory responses, and impaired neuroplasticity. However, no single hypothesis can fully account for the heterogeneous clinical presentation and pathophysiology of the disorder. Instead, recent research increasingly converges on the notion that MDD involves complex interactions across multiple physiological systems [ 4 ]. This recognition underscores the need for a novel, more holistic analytical approach—moving beyond the narrow search for individual biomarkers and towards a multisystem perspective, much like the comprehensive strategies spanning genomics, proteomics, and metabolomics. However, existing multi-omics technologies‌ (e.g., integrated multi-omics platforms) ‌as well as advanced molecular biomarker panels are often costly and technically complex, limiting their scalability as first-line diagnostic tools. Infrared (IR) spectroscopy, including mid-infrared spectroscopy (MIRS) and near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS), has emerged as a powerful, reagent-free, and nondestructive analytical technique that is well suited for holistic biochemical profiling [ 5 ]. By capturing the interaction between infrared light and molecular bonds, IR spectroscopy generates a unique “digital fingerprint” of a biological sample, reflecting the composite signal of proteins, lipids, carbohydrates, and nucleic acids—effectively providing multiomics-level information in a single measurement [ 6 , 7 ]. This capability enables the sensitive detection of subtle and systemic biochemical alterations associated with disease states. However, one well-recognized challenge in IR-based biofluid analysis is the strong absorption of water, which may mask subtle biochemical signals and hinder the identification of disease-related characteristics. Controlled drying of serum or plasma prior to spectral acquisition has been progressively adopted in IR studies to reduce water interference and facilitate downstream spectral analysis in IR-based biofluid investigations [ 8 – 10 ]. In recent years, the integration of spectroscopic data with machine learning has shown promise in the diagnosis of neuropsychiatric disorders such as Alzheimer’s disease [ 11 , 12 ], Parkinson’s disease [ 13 ], and schizophrenia [ 14 ]. A small number of pilot studies have also explored its use in MDD. For example, Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) and Raman spectroscopy have revealed MDD-associated alterations of phospholipid‒protein balance in serum and shifts of protein peak positions in tear fluid [ 15 – 17 ]. Despite these advances, the application of NIRS—which benefits from rich spectral digital information derived from nonfundamental molecular transitions [ 18 ]—remains unexplored in MDD research. In this study, we established an analytical framework that integrates dried serum NIRS with multivariate machine learning. This study was designed with three primary objectives: (1) to develop and validate a diagnostic model distinguishing MDD patients from healthy controls (HCs); (2) to construct a predictive model for antidepressant treatment outcomes; and (3) to systematically interpret both models via SHapley additive exPlanations (SHAP), thereby identifying the influential spectral digital features and potentially disease-associated biochemical signatures in serum. The resulting models were implemented within a unified online platform, illustrating the feasibility of translating analytical development into a clinically oriented workflow. This framework is primarily intended as an objective and rapid auxiliary tool to complement clinical assessment and assist in antidepressant treatment stratification in psychiatric or general hospital settings, and may also have potential utility in community-based or hospital-level preliminary screening programs targeting individuals at risk for MDD. In practice, serum collected during routine clinical evaluation can be processed under standardized conditions, measured via NIRS, and analyzed via the unified online platform to generate probabilistic decision-support outputs. Methods Subject recruitment and treatment A total of 212 participants aged between 18 and 65 years were enrolled in the study, comprising 126 MDD patients and 86 HCs. The diagnosis of MDD was made according to the criteria of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 4th Edition (DSM-IV), using the Chinese version of the Mini-International Neuropsychiatric Interview (MINI), and confirmed by two senior psychiatrists, following procedures established in previous studies [ 19 – 21 ]. The 17-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD-17) was administered to assess depressive symptom severity, and MDD patients underwent 12 weeks of escitalopram (ESC) monotherapy. The inclusion and exclusion criteria, as well as medication details, are provided in the Supplementary Materials. All the serum samples from the MDD patients and HCs were collected and stored in a freezer at -80 °C until use. NIRS measurements NIRS data were acquired using an Antaris™ II FT-NIR analyzer (Thermo Scientific™ Co., USA) in the range of 1000–2564 nm (3900–10000 cm − 1 ) with a resolution of 8 cm − 1 , averaged from 64 scans. For the water and liquid serum samples, the NIRS were measured via a quartz cuvette with an optical path of 1 mm as the sample cell. For the dried serum samples, approximately 5 µL of each serum sample was dripped onto a quartz flake, air-dried at room temperature for 15 min, and then measured in transmission mode. Background scans were performed prior to each sample acquisition to eliminate spectral contributions from environmental variations, including CO₂ and water vapor in the sample chamber. Reproducibility was evaluated using the coefficients of variation (CVs) of baseline-corrected peak intensities at selected characteristic wavelengths and inter-spectral correlation coefficients calculated between replicate spectra of the same sample following independent sample preparation. Detailed results are provided in Supplementary Tables S1 and S2 . Statistical analysis and machine learning framework Statistical analyses were conducted via SPSS (version 26.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Group differences in demographic and clinical variables were examined using two-sample t tests or chi-square tests, as appropriate. All procedures for spectral preprocessing, feature extraction, model construction, and visualization were implemented in Python 3.12 using scikit-learn 1.5.1, numpy 1.26.4, pandas 2.2.2, and matplotlib 3.9.0 libraries. SHAP values were computed using the SHAP library (version 0.46.0). A web application was developed with the Python-based Streamlit 1.41.0 framework. A fixed random seed (random_state = 100) was applied to ensure reproducibility of model training and data splitting. Detailed model hyperparameters are provided in Supplementary Table S3 . The dataset was divided into training and test sets at a 4:1 ratio using the Kennard–Stone algorithm to ensure representative spectral coverage. To prevent information leakage, only the training set was used for feature selection and model development. A strict nested cross-validation (NCV) strategy was adopted within the training set: five outer iterations were generated using stratified outer-loop resampling, and each outer loop used an inner stratified fivefold CV to tune hyperparameters and select the optimal model. To estimate 95% confidence intervals (CIs), 1000 bootstrap resamples were drawn from the aggregated outer-loop predictions, and the bootstrap mean and percentile-based CIs were reported. Principal component analysis (PCA) and partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) were employed to explore the spectral data structure and construct classification models. PCA, an unsupervised approach, was used to visualize sample distribution patterns and potential clustering among groups, whereas PLS-DA, a supervised classification technique particularly suitable for high-dimensional and multivariate datasets, was applied for diagnostic and treatment outcome prediction classification. Detailed algorithmic descriptions and mathematical formulations of PCA and PLS-DA are provided in the Supplementary Materials. Feature selection is critical for handling high-dimensional variables and improving predictive performance by reducing dimensionality and eliminating irrelevant or interfering variables. In this study, six multivariate feature selection techniques—regression coefficients (RC), variable importance in projection (VIP), uninformative variable elimination (UVE), competitive adaptive reweighted sampling (CARS), random forest (RF), and eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost)—were employed as performance-oriented variable screening and ranking strategies to identify informative spectral features. Notably, as CARS directly yields the optimal feature subset without prior ranking by importance, the mean SHAP values of the CARS-selected features were subsequently calculated as feature importance values and ranked to allow comparison with other selection methods. In the present study, this exploratory interpretability-oriented integration was termed CARS-SHAP for convenience. A brief overview of each method is presented in the Supplementary Materials. Once the importance values of all wavelengths (for RC, VIP, and UVE) or the selected wavelengths (for CARS-SHAP, RF, and XGBoost) were obtained, they were ranked in descending order. The ranked wavelengths were then progressively introduced into model training, and the subset yielding the highest predictive performance was identified as the optimal feature combination, consistent with previously reported progressive feature refinement or backward elimination strategies in spectroscopic modeling [ 22 , 23 ]. Model performance was comprehensively evaluated using multiple evaluation metrics, including accuracy, sensitivity (recall), specificity, precision, the F1 score, the Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC), Cohen’s kappa, the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, and the area under the curve (AUC). The classification threshold for both models was determined on the training set by maximizing Youden’s index. These metrics collectively provided a robust evaluation of both overall and class-specific model performance. Detailed definitions and calculation formulas for each metric are provided in the Supplementary Materials. To facilitate the clinical application of the model, the final predictive models trained with the optimal feature combination for diagnosis and treatment outcomes were integrated into a web application developed with the Python-based Streamlit framework. On the basis of these final models, SHAP was used to quantify each feature’s contribution to the model output and to provide a comprehensive understanding of how each feature contributes to model predictions. Results Clinical characteristics A total of 126 MDD patients (46 males and 80 females), along with 86 matched HCs (38 males and 48 females) from a large cohort, were included in this study [ 20 ]. The overall study workflow is illustrated in Fig. 1 . No significant differences in age, sex, or BMI were observed between the MDD patients and HCs (all p &gt; 0.05) (detailed information for all participants is provided in Table 1 (a)). Among the 126 enrolled MDD patients, 71 patients who were treated with ESC monotherapy had available follow-up data. After 12 weeks of treatment, their HAMD-17 scores were significantly reduced ( p &lt; 0.05). Among these patients, 41 (57.75%) achieved remission, defined as a HAMD-17 score ≤ 7, and were classified as remitters (R), whereas 30 (42.25%) remained in the non-remission state (HAMD-17 &gt; 7) and were classified as non-remitters (NR). The definition of remission applied here followed internationally recognized criteria. The baseline characteristics did not differ significantly between the R and NR groups, as shown in Table 1 (b). The typical NIRS of pure water, liquid serum, and dried serum samples are shown in Fig. 2 A. Generally, the whole spectral range from 1000 to 2564 nm can be roughly divided into 6 different regions according to the literature [ 24 – 26 ]. The first spectral region between 1000 and 1280 nm shows low-intensity signals mainly from the second overtone of C–H stretching [ 24 , 25 ], with dried serum exhibiting a weak broad band at 1187 nm that is absent in water and liquid serum [ 27 , 28 ]. The second region between 1280 and 1420 nm mainly corresponds to C–H combinations [ 24 ], and no obvious peak is found in this region for all three NIRS. The bands in the third region of 1420–1600 nm mainly reflect the first overtones of O–H and N–H stretching [ 24 , 25 ]. Water and liquid serum show only a single broad O–H band at 1450 nm, whereas dried serum reveals two partially overlapping bands at 1450 and 1500 nm. The fourth region, spanning 1600–1800 nm, is associated with the first overtone of C–H stretching [ 24 ]. Notably, overlapping wide absorptions of low intensity centered at 1704 and 1738 nm are observed only in dried serum samples. In the next region, ranging from 1800 to 2300 nm, multiple vibrational combinations occur, mainly O–H and N–H, with possible couplings such as O–H/C–O and C = O/O–H or N–H, making peak identification complex [ 18 , 24 , 26 , 29 , 30 ]. In water and liquid serum, high-intensity bands at 1897–1932 nm (saturated detector) and a peak at 2002 nm reflect O–H bending and stretching [ 23 , 31 ]. Dried serum exhibites four characteristic peaks: a strong O–H combination at 1942 nm [ 23 , 26 ], two narrow protein-related bands at 2054 and 2172 nm (C–N and N–H) [ 32 ], and a peak at 2295 nm due to O–H/C–O stretching or C–H combinations [ 33 , 34 ]. The last spectral region, covering 2300–2564 nm, mainly includes C–H stretching/deformation and C–H/C–C combinations [ 26 ]. Dried serum shows three peaks at 2347, 2472, and 2538 nm (C–H and C–N–C from amides) [ 24 , 26 , 35 ], whereas a band at 2455 nm appears in water and liquid serum, likely due to detector nonlinearity in some instruments [ 36 ]. Owing to the high water content (~ 90%-92% w/w) in serum, the NIRS of pure water and liquid serum samples are almost identical, with liquid serum containing only small amounts of dissolved proteins, lipids, carbohydrates, and trace components [ 37 ]. The strong water absorption in liquid serum saturates the detector, compromising spectral quality and masking weak solute features, thereby limiting the analytical ability of NIRS. In dried serum samples, removing water enhances solute-specific spectral features, improving the signal-to-noise ratio and enabling more accurate molecular analysis. Therefore, this study focused on the NIRS of dried serum. The average NIRS of dried serum samples from HCs, MDD, R, and NR patients are presented in Fig. 2 C. Obviously, the average NIRS of the four groups almost completely overlapped, suggesting that, on average, the chemical information of the serum samples in these groups was highly similar. The PCA scatter plots of spectral datasets for diagnosis (MDD vs. HCs, Fig. 2 D) and treatment outcome prediction (R vs. NR, Fig. 2 E) illustrate group-related variations in chemical components. For the diagnostic spectral dataset, the first two PCs accounted for approximately 99.71% of the total variation, with PC1 and PC2 explaining 98.85% and 0.86% of the variance, respectively. For the treatment outcome prediction spectral dataset, the first two PCs accounted for approximately 99.82% of the total variation, with PC1 and PC2 explaining 98.58% and 1.24%, respectively. Notably, neither of the spectral datasets showed a clear tendency toward separation, whether between the MDD patients and HCs or between the R patients and NR patients. Therefore, more effective modeling methods and feature selection approaches are needed to fully utilize spectral information from NIRS to distinguish between MDD patients and HCs, as well as to differentiate R from NR within the MDD group. The classification models of PLS-DA were first developed on the basis of the full set of wavelengths. The relationships between three key model evaluation metrics—the AUC for the training set (AUC_C), the cross-validated AUC (AUC_CV), and the AUC for the test set (AUC_P)—and the most critical model parameter, the LVs in PLS-DA, are shown in Figure S1 A and S1B. In general, as the number of LVs and the model complexity increased, the models exhibited a typical transition from underfitting to overfitting, with optimal performance at intermediate LV values—6 for the diagnostic models and 14 for the treatment outcome prediction models. At these optimal LV values, the diagnostic models distinguishing MDD patients from HCs (Figure S1 A) achieved AUC_C, AUC_CV, and AUC_P values of 0.94, 0.71, and 0.74, respectively, whereas the treatment outcome prediction models (Figure S1 B) yielded values of 0.98, 0.60, and 0.80, respectively. On the basis of these established preliminary models, outliers were assessed via Hotelling’s T 2 vs. Q residual plots [ 2 , 23 , 38 ]. For the diagnostic model shown in Figure S1 C, two samples exceeded the 95% CIs for both Hotelling’s T 2 and Q residual values [ 39 ], whereas for the treatment outcome prediction model (Figure S1 D), no samples simultaneously exceeded both CIs. These two outliers were therefore excluded from the corresponding training sets for subsequent modeling. Feature wavelength extraction Six feature selection strategies, including RC, VIP, UVE, CARS-SHAP, RF, and XGBoost, were applied to identify the most informative wavelengths from the full spectral range (1000–2564 nm). Figure S2 shows the feature importance and selected wavelength subsets obtained by different feature extraction methods for both the diagnostic and treatment outcome prediction models. For RC, VIP, UVE, and CARS-SHAP, the selected wavelengths were broadly distributed across the entire 1000–2564 nm range. In contrast, RF- and XGBoost-selected features were mainly concentrated in the shorter wavelength regions, typically restricted to regions Ⅰ-Ⅳ or Ⅰ-Ⅱ, and corresponded to a few characteristic peaks. Notably, the wavelength subsets obtained by VIP and CARS-SHAP were located mainly in regions with apparent infrared absorption, suggesting a physicochemically meaningful distribution. To minimize selection bias and ensure robust feature evaluation, all model training in wavelength selection procedures was conducted within a strict NCV framework, using the AUC as the primary performance metric. Figure S3 displays the performance of the PLS-DA models, which were trained using a series of feature subsets derived from each of the six feature selection techniques. Specifically, the mean AUC, denoted as AUC_P, and the mean accuracy, denoted as accuracy_P, were calculated across all the outer loops of the NCV. For the optimal feature subsets, model evaluation results were further estimated using 1,000 bootstrap resamples based on the aggregated outer-loop predictions. The resulting bootstrap mean AUCs, 95% CIs, and corresponding numbers of selected features are summarized in Fig. 3 A-D. For both the diagnostic (Fig. 3 A, C) and treatment outcome prediction (Fig. 3 B, D) models, the majority of feature-selected models outperformed those based on the full spectrum, despite the use of far fewer variables. The performance of the diagnostic models followed the order CARS-SHAP &gt; UVE &gt; RC &gt; RF &gt; XGBoost &gt; VIP &gt; None. Notably, CARS-SHAP achieved the highest predictive performance with only 59 features, illustrating that model performance was not strictly dependent on the number of selected wavelengths. In the treatment outcome models, a similar ranking was observed: CARS-SHAP &gt; UVE &gt; RC &gt; VIP &gt; RF &gt; XGBoost ≈ None. Here, again, the CARS-SHAP algorithm, which was constructed using only 33 features, achieved the highest AUC_P. Across both the diagnostic and treatment models, CARS-SHAP consistently outperformed the other approaches, indicating its superior ability to capture decisive spectral features. This advantage likely arises from its more evenly distributed wavelength selection across the full 1000–2564 nm range, enabling a more comprehensive representation of relevant spectral information. A detailed comparison of the bootstrap mean metrics, including the AUC, accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, precision, F1 score, MCC, and kappa coefficient, is presented in Fig. 3 E and F, with the corresponding 95% CIs provided in Tables S4 and S5 . For the PLS-DA diagnostic models (Fig. 3 E), CARS-SHAP achieved the highest classification performance, with an AUC of 0.91 (95% CI: 0.86–0.95), accuracy of 0.85 (95% CI: 0.79–0.90), sensitivity of 0.87 (95% CI: 0.80–0.93), specificity of 0.83 (95% CI: 0.73–0.91), precision of 0.88 (95% CI: 0.81–0.94), F1 score of 0.87 (95% CI: 0.82–0.92), MCC of 0.69 (95% CI: 0.57–0.80), and kappa of 0.69 (95% CI: 0.57–0.80). Similarly, among the treatment outcome prediction models (Fig. 3 F), CARS-SHAP also yielded the best overall performance, with an AUC of 0.90 (95% CI: 0.80–0.97), accuracy of 0.84 (95% CI: 0.75–0.93), sensitivity of 0.83 (95% CI: 0.67–0.96), specificity of 0.84 (95% CI: 0.71–0.97), precision of 0.80 (95% CI: 0.63–0.95), F1 score of 0.81 (95% CI: 0.68–0.92), MCC of 0.67 (95% CI: 0.47–0.86), and kappa of 0.67 (95% CI: 0.46–0.86). Collectively, these results demonstrate that CARS-SHAP is the most effective feature selection method, producing concise feature subsets with superior comprehensive predictive performance. Therefore, CARS-SHAP was chosen as the preferred feature selection approach for distinguishing MDD patients from HCs and for differentiating R from NR within the MDD group. Compared with the preliminary models built on the full spectral range, the two optimized models showed relatively improved diagnostic performance within the test set of the current dataset, suggesting enhanced discriminative ability in distinguishing MDD patients from HCs and NR from R. For diagnostic classification, the AUC increased from 0.74 (95% CI: 0.56–0.90) to 0.91 (95% CI: 0.84–0.96; Fig. 4 A), and for treatment outcome prediction, the AUC increased from 0.80 (95% CI: 0.52–1.0) to 0.85 (95% CI: 0.61–1.0; Fig. 4 D). In addition, the final diagnostic model yielded a sensitivity of 92.3% and a specificity of 72.2% (Fig. 4 B), whereas the final treatment outcome model achieved a sensitivity of 83.3% and a specificity of 77.8% (Fig. 4 E). All the above test set results fell within the 95% CIs of the performance metrics estimated via NCV and bootstrap resampling (Tables S4 and S5 ), indicating consistency between internal cross-validation estimates and test set performance within this cohort. In addition to evaluating predictive discrimination, we assessed the potential clinical utility of the final models for diagnosis and treatment outcome prediction using decision curve analysis (DCA), together with calibration curves, on the test set. DCA quantifies the net clinical benefit across a range of threshold probabilities by comparing model-guided strategies with default “treat all” or “treat none” approaches [ 40 , 41 ]. As shown in Fig. 4 C and F, the final diagnostic model provided net benefits across thresholds from 0.01 to 0.97, whereas the treatment outcome model yielded benefits between 0.01 and 0.74. Calibration analysis on the test set demonstrated acceptable agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes, with Brier scores of 0.131 for diagnostic classification and 0.161 for treatment outcome prediction (Figure S4 ). These results suggest that, within the current dataset, across a wide range of clinically relevant thresholds, both models for diagnostic and treatment outcome prediction may outperform the reference strategies, indicating their potential clinical applicability. To enhance generalizability and explore the feasibility of potential translation into clinical practice, the two final NIRS-based models for MDD diagnosis and antidepressant treatment outcome prediction were deployed as a web-based application (Fig. 4 G). Upon uploading the required spectral file, the application automatically extracts the relevant feature wavelengths, estimates the individual risk of MDD, and, if the subject is identified with MDD, further provides the predicted treatment outcome of antidepressants. The web application is accessible online at https://prediction-of-diagnosis-and-treatment-outcome.streamlit.app . This user-friendly system serves as a preliminary demonstration platform for integrating NIR-based diagnostic and prognostic assessments into clinical decision-making for MDD. The global importance of each wavelength was estimated by the mean absolute Shapley values, with higher values indicating greater contributions. The ranked importance of wavelengths and the corresponding cumulative ratios are presented in Fig. 5 . The input wavelengths covered the entire spectral range from 1000 to 2545 nm, and those with higher mean SHAP values were predominantly located in regions III, V, and VI (Fig. 5 A), which were generally not detectable in the NIRS of liquid serum. For the diagnostic model (Fig. 5 B), the top 28 of 59 wavelengths accounted for 86% of the total model interpretation, with a steeper decline in importance after the 29th feature. Molecular assignments (Table S6 ) indicated that these 28 wavelengths corresponded to proteins (15), carbohydrates (6), lipids (7), and nucleic acids (2), with proteins being the dominant contributors (15 of 28, including 7 of the top 10) [ 18 , 26 ]. The SHAP summary plot (Fig. 5 C) further revealed that higher absorbance at protein-related wavelengths (10 of 15) tended to negatively influence model predictions, whereas most carbohydrate-related wavelengths (4 of 6) exhibited positive effects. For the treatment outcome prediction model (Fig. 5 D), the top 17 of 33 wavelengths explained 95% of the total model interpretation. These wavelengths were assigned primarily to proteins (8), carbohydrates (8), and lipids (5) (Table S6 ). As shown in Fig. 5 E, protein-related wavelengths (5 of 8) had mostly negative effects on the prediction, whereas carbohydrate-related wavelengths (6 of 8) had positive effects. To our knowledge, this is the first study to comprehensively characterize dried-serum NIRS and investigate its potential applicability for diagnostic and predictive modeling in psychiatry. A key strength of our approach lies in its attempt to move toward an objective, data-driven tool for MDD diagnosis and antidepressant treatment outcome prediction, complementing existing clinician-rated scales and potentially reducing the burden associated with the conventional trial-and-error treatment process. The integrated CARS-SHAP strategy further enabled the identification of candidate spectral digital biomarkers, which may reflect holistic biochemical profiles associated with MDD diagnosis and antidepressant treatment outcome prediction. These findings suggest that dried-serum NIRS may represent a potentially useful platform for rapid, low-cost, and reproducible spectral digital biomarker discovery and application in psychiatric research. Blood-based biomarkers have long been investigated as potential diagnostic tools for psychiatric disorders. However, owing to the complex interactions among multiple physiological systems in MDD, single biomarkers may not capture the multifactorial nature of the disease. In this context, IR-based spectralomics provides a multi-omics integrative perspective by capturing spectral digital signatures that encode molecular structures and chemical compositions across proteins, lipids, carbohydrates, and nucleic acids. This approach has the potential to enable holistic biochemical profiling and provides a general characterization of the molecular alterations underlying complex diseases [ 43 ]. Currently, most blood-based NIRS measurements are performed using liquid serum or plasma, where strong water absorption bands obscure the subtle spectral features of biomolecular solutes, thereby limiting the analytical capability of NIRS. Our study acquired NIRS of dried serum with pronounced spectral signatures of solutes in serum, which revealed distinct spectral patterns that may reflect “digital fingerprint” information and suggest the potential utility of blood-based spectral biomarkers in MDD diagnostics. Feature selection is critical for multivariate spectral analysis, as it enhances both predictive performance and model interpretability by eliminating irrelevant or interfering variables. Given its suitability for small-sample and high-dimensional spectral datasets [ 38 , 44 ], PLS-DA was employed in this study to construct full-spectrum models, upon which six feature selection strategies were subsequently applied to identify decisive wavelengths. Among these strategies, CARS and SHAP were innovatively integrated, with the SHAP method employed to evaluate the importance of features selected by CARS, allowing further refinement of the feature set. In addition, a rigorous NCV was conducted during model training for each wavelength selection procedure to minimize selection bias. Our findings suggest that CARS-SHAP achieved comparatively better performance than other feature selection methods in both diagnostic and treatment outcome prediction models, generating the minimum number of features and the highest model performance. The superiority of the CARS-SHAP approach largely arises from the unique design of CARS, which emulates the Darwinian principle of “survival of the fittest” to adaptively eliminate uninformative variables through an iterative sampling process based on Monte Carlo sampling and PLS regression coefficients [ 45 ]. These findings are generally in line with previous reports in biological material analysis, where CARS has often been reported to exhibit relatively strong performance compared with other feature selection strategies [ 46 , 47 ]. The optimal spectral panels were rigorously validated through multiple strategies for both MDD diagnosis and antidepressant treatment outcome prediction. The models demonstrated relatively stable performance within the applied validation framework, as supported by the bootstrap mean AUCs and 95% CIs from the NCV, and showed consistent generalizability to the test set. In the validation set, the diagnostic and predictive models achieved mean AUCs of 0.91 (MDD vs. HC) and 0.90 (R vs. NR), whereas comparable AUCs of 0.91 and 0.85 were obtained in the test set, suggesting internal consistency of model performance. As the first study to diagnose MDD and predict antidepressant treatment outcomes via dried-serum NIRS–based digital biomarkers, our models achieved performance levels within the range reported for complex multi-omics approaches. For example, our previous metagenomic analyses achieved AUCs of 0.82 using carbohydrate-active enzyme features [ 19 ] and 0.98/0.90 in the discovery and validation sets with integrated virome–microbiome–metabolome markers for distinguishing MDD patients from HCs [ 48 ], whereas baseline sporulation gene signatures enabled treatment-remission prediction, with an AUC of 0.710 [ 20 ]. Beyond competitive accuracy, the IR-based workflow requires only ~ 5 µL of serum and can be completed within minutes via a compact, low-cost benchtop NIRS device, suggesting potential feasibility for larger-scale or point-of-care applications, pending further validation. In addition, DCA confirmed a substantial net clinical benefit across a broad range of threshold probabilities, and an interactive web-based application further supports seamless cascading diagnostic and predictive analyses through a user-friendly interface, serving as a preliminary demonstration of the possible practicality and scalability of spectral biomarker–based approaches in psychiatric research. The spectral features identified in this study provide preliminary insights into the biochemical alterations associated with MDD. The diagnostic model integrated all major biomolecular components, with key features associated primarily with proteins. More specifically, as shown in Table S3 , features assigned to N–H bonds, C = O and C = ONH groups from amides, and C–H aryl vibrations indicate the presence of proteins, with the C–H aryl signals potentially also representing aromatic amino acids. Alterations in proteins are frequently observed in MDD [ 49 , 50 ], and dysregulation of aromatic amino acid metabolism, including phenylalanine, tyrosine, and tryptophan, is consistent with our previous findings [ 20 , 51 ]. The wavelengths assigned to C–H vibrations from aliphatic hydrocarbons and the P–OH/O–H signals from phosphates may reflect potential alterations in lipid metabolism and nucleic acid turnover, both of which have been implicated in MDD. Dysregulated lipid metabolism can exacerbate depressive symptoms through neuroinflammatory pathways [ 52 , 53 ], whereas oxidative stress–induced RNA and DNA damage represents a potential pathophysiological mechanism contributing to MDD [ 54 ]. Compared with the diagnostic model, the treatment-outcome prediction model was driven mainly by proteins and carbohydrates. The O–H/C–O combination, C = O–O, and C–H vibrations correspond to carbohydrates such as polysaccharides, which have been shown to exert antidepressant effects and offer promising therapeutic options for MDD with minimal side effects and multiple molecular targets [ 55 ]. Our findings offer a tentative and integrative spectral perspective on the biomolecular components associated with MDD and highlight that these spectral biomarkers may play a significant role in the pathogenesis of depression and in mediating treatment response. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the NIRS technique captures highly overlapping absorbance signals, which can only be broadly assigned to specific biochemical classes. While the spectral band assignments in this study were referenced to extensive prior literature, minor deviations or misattributions cannot be entirely excluded. Second, the grouping of R and NR was based on internationally accepted clinical criteria, which inevitably carry some degree of subjectivity. Employing more stringent or extreme grouping thresholds might reveal additional biochemical distinctions associated with treatment outcomes. Third, although the dataset was partitioned into training and test sets using the Kennard–Stone algorithm to ensure spectral representativeness, this approach constitutes an internal validation strategy rather than a truly independent or external validation cohort. Additionally, the limited sample size and unequal group distribution between R and NR may have contributed to increased estimation variability, as evidenced by the relatively wide CI observed for the treatment outcome model. Although our models were rigorously validated using multiple complementary strategies, further studies with larger and multicenter cohorts, incorporation of external validation datasets, and more comprehensive control of potential confounders are warranted to reinforce the robustness and generalizability of these findings. Conclusions In conclusion, this study proposed and explored a novel and practical framework for the objective assessment of MDD and the exploratory prediction of antidepressant treatment outcomes using NIRS of dried serum samples. The biochemical information encoded in NIRS was shown to contain informative spectral patterns that supported the construction of accurate, interpretable, and potentially clinically applicable models. By combining the wavelength selection method with rigorous NCV, two panels of spectral biomarkers were identified and utilized to develop diagnostic and treatment outcome prediction models. These models were evaluated on the test set, assessed via DCA, and implemented in a prototype user-friendly online platform. The biochemical relevance of the selected wavelengths, combined with SHAP-based interpretation, suggests possible contributions of key biomolecules underlying psychiatric disorders. Overall, the present findings should be regarded as preliminary and hypothesis-generating, highlighting a promising but exploratory avenue toward the development of spectral biomarker-based biosensing systems for precision medicine.
