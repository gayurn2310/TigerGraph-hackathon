Early onset Alzheimer’s disease with V180I variant of prion protein gene and a family history of dementia: a case report

Background Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is the most common cause of dementia in older adults. The apolipoprotein E ( APOE ) ε4 allele represents the strongest genetic risk factor for late-onset AD, and individuals homozygous for APOE ε4 typically develop cognitive impairment at a mean age of 65 years. Here, we report a case of early-onset AD with an apparent autosomal dominant family history who was homozygous for the APOE ε4 allele and harbored a prion protein gene ( PRNP ) variant involving a valine-to-isoleucine substitution at codon 180 ( PRNP -V180I). Case presentation The patient developed memory impairment at 47 years of age, and presented to our hospital eight months after symptom onset. His older brother experienced memory decline beginning at 47 years of age, which progressed to dementia, and died at 55 years of age. The patient’s mother also developed memory impairment at 65 years of age, followed by a diagnosis of dementia, and his maternal grandmother reportedly had a history of marked forgetfulness. Neurological examination revealed cognitive impairment predominantly characterized by disorientation and deficits in recent memory, with a Mini-Mental State Examination score of 20/30. No neurological abnormalities were identified apart from the cognitive deficits. Brain magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated mild bilateral hippocampal atrophy, while diffusion-weighted imaging showed no abnormal signal changes. Cerebrospinal fluid analysis revealed normal cell counts, protein, and glucose levels. However, the amyloid-β protein (Aβ)42/Aβ40 ratio was reduced to 0.036 (normal: ≥0.067), and phosphorylated tau was elevated to 175 pg/mL (normal: 21.5–59.0 pg/mL). Based on these findings, a diagnosis of early-onset AD was established. Given the strong family history of dementia, whole-exome sequencing was performed, and revealed no pathogenic variants in the amyloid precursor protein , presenilin 1 , or presenilin 2 genes. The APOE genotype was ε4/ε4, and the PRNP -V180I variant was also identified. Conclusion We describe a case of early-onset AD with a family history of dementia in the context of APOE ε4 homozygosity and the PRNP -V180I variant. APOE ε4 homozygosity likely played a central role in the early disease onset and familial aggregation observed in this patient.

Background Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is the most prevalent cause of dementia in older adults, typically manifesting after the age of 65, although approximately 5.5% of cases present before this age threshold, constituting early-onset AD [ 1 ]. Among individuals with early-onset AD, families exhibiting autosomal dominant inheritance patterns have been documented, and pathogenic variants in the amyloid precursor protein ( APP ), presenilin 1 ( PSEN1 ), and presenilin 2 ( PSEN2 ) genes have been identified as causative factors. In addition, the apolipoprotein E ( APOE ) ε4 allele is recognized as the strongest genetic modifier of susceptibility to sporadic AD [ 2 ]. Prion diseases are uniformly fatal neurodegenerative disorders characterized by cerebral accumulation of misfolded prion protein (PrP Sc ), leading to rapidly progressive neurological decline, including dementia. These conditions share pathological parallels with AD, notably the aggregation of aberrant proteins, PrP Sc in prion diseases and amyloid-β protein (Aβ) and tau protein in AD, and include predominantly sporadic cases with a minority of hereditary forms. Although several studies have proposed a potential association between variants in the prion protein gene ( PRNP ) and AD pathogenesis [ 3 ], the relationship remains unresolved. Here, we describe a case of early-onset AD with an apparent autosomal dominant family history, presenting at 47 years of age, without detectable pathogenic variants in APP , PSEN1 , or PSEN2 . The patient was found to be homozygous for the APOE ε4 allele and carried a PRNP variant involving a valine-to-isoleucine substitution at codon 180 ( PRNP -V180I). Case presentation The patient began exhibiting frequent occupational errors at age 47, including missed appointments and episodes of disorientation while driving. He had been diagnosed with hypertension at age 42 but remained untreated. His memory impairment progressively worsened, resulting in recurrent mistakes at work, and he presented to our hospital approximately 8 months after symptom onset. His older brother developed memory impairment at age 47 (III-1), which subsequently progressed to dementia, and he died at the age of 55 (Fig. 1 ). The patient’s mother also experienced memory decline beginning at age 65 (II-4), followed by a diagnosis of dementia, and his maternal grandmother reportedly had a history of significant forgetfulness (I-4) (Fig. 1 ). Given the early-onset presentation in both the patient and his brother, along with a family history suggestive of autosomal dominant inheritance, we performed a comprehensive analysis of whole-exome sequencing (WES) data, targeting approximately 400 genes associated with adult-onset neurodegenerative disorders. To identify potentially pathogenic variants, we applied the following filtering criteria: Minor Allele Frequency &lt; 1% in public databases, non-synonymous amino acid substitutions, and Combined Annotation Dependent Depletion score ≥ 10. In our study, WES yielded 7.2 gigabases of data, achieving a mean depth of coverage of 55.9x, with 88.8% of the targeted exonic regions covered at least 20x. No pathogenic variants were identified in APP , PSEN1 , or PSEN2 . The APOE genotype was ε4/ε4. Additionally, the patient harbored the PRNP -V180I variant, accompanied by methionine homozygosity at codon 129 and glutamic acid homozygosity at codon 219 of PRNP . Aside from the PRNP -V180I variant and the APOE ε4/ε4 genotype, no other variants were identified that could be classified as pathogenic or likely pathogenic in the context of the patient’s clinical presentation. Discussion We describe a patient who developed cognitive impairment at age 47, which progressed to dementia within two years of onset, accompanied by a family history of dementia consistent with an autosomal dominant inheritance pattern. Neuroimaging findings and CSF biomarker profiles were indicative of AD, leading to a diagnosis of early-onset AD. Genetic testing identified no pathogenic variants in APP , PSEN1 , or PSEN2 , and the patient was found to be homozygous for the APOE ε4 allele. Notably, the patient also carried the PRNP -V180I variant. Genetic Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease (gCJD) associated with the PRNP -V180I variant (V180I-gCJD) is the most prevalent form of genetic prion disease in Japan, whereas this variant is exceedingly uncommon outside Japan and Korea [ 4 ]. In contrast to classical sporadic CJD, V180I-gCJD exhibits several distinguishing clinical characteristics, including an older age at onset, a comparatively slower progression of cognitive decline with fewer additional neurological manifestations, and diffuse edematous ribbon-like cortical hyperintensities on DWI of brain MRI [ 4 ]. In the present case, brain MRI performed 24 months after symptom onset demonstrated no cortical hyperintensity on DWI, suggesting that prion disease had not yet developed. APOE is the strongest genetic risk factor for late-onset AD, with the APOE ε4 allele conferring a markedly increased risk of developing the disorder [ 2 ]. Nearly all individuals homozygous for APOE ε4 exhibit Alzheimer-type neuropathological changes, and CSF biomarkers of amyloid and tau are consistently elevated compared with those in APOE ε3 homozygotes beginning around age 55; by age 65, almost all APOE ε4 homozygotes demonstrate abnormal CSF amyloid levels [ 5 ]. APOE genotype also influences the age at onset: individuals homozygous for ε4 typically develop cognitive impairment at an average age of 65.1 years, substantially earlier than heterozygotes or noncarriers [ 5 ]. In the present case, cognitive impairment began at age 47, representing an unusually early onset even for an APOE ε4 homozygous individual. Although APOE ε4 is a potent risk factor for AD, the phenotypic expression among ε4 homozygotes is highly heterogeneous, ranging from early-onset AD to lifelong asymptomatic status [ 2 ], indicating the influence of additional genetic and environmental modifiers. A recent study comparing polygenic risk scores (PRS) in APOE ε4 homozygotes who developed AD before age 65 with those who remained cognitively intact beyond age 75 demonstrated significantly higher PRS values among early-onset cases [ 6 ]. Another study found that among APOE ε4 carriers with subjective cognitive decline, individuals with higher PRS values were more likely to progress to AD dementia [ 7 ]. Collectively, these observations suggest that genetic modifiers substantially influence APOE ε4–associated AD risk [ 2 ]. To date, only a single case of early-onset AD associated with the PRNP -V180I variant has been reported [ 8 ]. That patient had no family history of dementia, developed cognitive impairment in the late 50s, and exhibited no pathogenic variants in APP , PSEN1 , or PSEN2 ; however, the APOE genotype was not documented [ 8 ]. Additionally, although several cases of V180I-gCJD with concomitant Alzheimer-type pathology have been described [ 9 – 12 ], all involved elderly individuals, raising the possibility that the Alzheimer-type pathology was incidental rather than mechanistically related. In contrast, the present patient had a family history strongly suggestive of autosomal dominant inheritance, with both the patient and his older brother developing cognitive impairment at age 47, indicating a substantial hereditary contribution beyond APOE ε4. In AD, Aβ oligomers have been reported to bind to cellular PrP, inducing neurotoxicity [ 13 , 14 ]. Moreover, several studies have suggested that the PRNP may be associated with AD onset [ 15 , 16 ]. In the present case, PRNP -V180I is likely an incidental finding, but the possibility that PRNP -V180I contributes to AD pathogenesis cannot be ruled out. Although APOE ε4 homozygosity likely played a major role in the early-onset presentation and familial aggregation in the present patient, the PRNP -V180I variant may also have influenced disease development. V180I-gCJD represents the most common form of gCJD in East Asia populations, yet it is rarely reported in Western countries. Further investigations are warranted to elucidate the interactions among the PRNP -V180I variant, APOE genotype, and the risk and timing of AD onset in East Asian patients. Conclusions We describe a case of early-onset AD with a family history of dementia in the context of APOE ε4 homozygosity and the PRNP -V180I variant. Although APOE ε4 homozygosity likely played a central role in the early disease onset and familial aggregation observed in this patient, the PRNP -V180I variant may have additionally contributed to disease pathogenesis. This case demonstrates that even when the PRNP -V180I variant is detected by genetic testing in patients with cognitive impairment, neurologists should not conclude prion disease. Instead, they must carefully evaluate the clinical presentation, family history, and biomarker evidence before making a diagnosis.
