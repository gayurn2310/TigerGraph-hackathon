Blood Levels of Homocysteine Predict Cardiovascular Events, but Not Alzheimer Disease Dementia, during a 4.7-year Follow-up in Patients with Vascular Risk Factors

Objective The homocysteine levels are risk factors for vascular events and dementia. However, whether their predictive validity is similar remains unknown. We compared the utility of the homocysteine levels for predicting vascular events and Alzheimer's disease (AD) and dementia in the same population. Methods This study was a Japanese hospital-based cohort study. A total of 426 patients with vascular risk factors were followed for a median of 4.65 years. Based on the homocysteine levels, patients were divided into three groups: 1st tertile (≤8.2 nmol/L, n=141), 2nd tertile (8.3-10.9 nmol/L, n=141), and 3rd tertile (≥11.0 nmol/L, n=144). They were also divided with the cut-off value of 15.0 nmol/L into high (n=55) and low (n=371) groups. The outcomes were stroke, major cardiovascular events (MACE), and AD dementia. Results During follow-up, stroke, MACE, and AD dementia occurred in 23, 31, and 15 patients, respectively. Patients in the 3rd tertile and high groups were more likely to have incident stroke and MACE than those in the other tertiles or low group. Patients with high homocysteine levels had a higher risk of stroke (hazard ratio 3.59; 95% confidence interval 1.09-11.83) and MACE (hazard ratio 5.19; 95% confidence interval 1.90-14.15) than those with low homocysteine levels after adjustment for confounding factors. However, no association was observed between the homocysteine levels and AD. Conclusion High serum homocysteine levels, especially more than 15.0 nmol/L, were significantly associated with vascular events but not with AD dementia in the same population during a 4.65-year follow-up period.

Introduction Under healthy conditions, the serum homocysteine levels are determined by dietary intake, vitamin status, and methylenetetrahydrofolate reductase (MTHFR) polymorphisms, and induce oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, and inflammation, potentially leading to atherosclerosis and neuroinflammation ( 1 ). In cohort studies, high serum homocysteine levels have been shown to increase the risk of stroke ( 2 ), myocardial infarction ( 2 ), dementia ( 3 ), Alzheimer's disease (AD) dementia ( 3 - 6 ), and vascular dementia ( 6 , 7 ). Serum homocysteine levels are associated with several surrogate markers of stroke and dementia, including white matter hyperintensities (WMHs) ( 7 , 8 ), brain atrophy ( 9 ), cognitive decline ( 8 ), and carotid plaque severity and vulnerability ( 10 ), thus supporting the role of homocysteine in the development of vascular disease and dementia. However, no study has so far investigated which events (vascular or dementia) are more closely associated with serum homocysteine levels. This study aimed to compare the utility of serum homocysteine levels for predicting vascular events and AD dementia in the same individuals. Materials and Methods Study design and patients This was a post hoc investigation of the Tokyo Women's Medical University Cerebral Vessel Disease (TWMU CVD) registry (Registration URL: https://www.umin.ac.jp/ctr/index.htm ; UMIN000026671), which was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Tokyo Women's Medical University (approval number 3,621) ( 11 ). Informed consent was obtained from all participants. Briefly, patients aged ≥40 years who presented with cerebral vessel disease on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and cerebrovascular risk factors ≥1 were included in the study. The exclusion criteria for the registry were any type of aphasia, evidence of dementia, dependence on activities of daily living, and vascular events within 1 month. The protocol conformed to the ethical guidelines of the 1975 Declaration of Helsinki, which are consistent with the Ethical Guidelines for Epidemiological Research by the Japanese Government and the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines. Between October 2015 and July 2019, 1,011 outpatients were enrolled in the TWMU CVD study. To examine the utility of the homocysteine levels for predicting stroke, major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), and AD dementia in independent outpatients, patients with a modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score ≥2 (n=53), a Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) score ＜24 (n=58) due to suspected previous cognitive impairment, and missing homocysteine measurement data (n=433) were excluded. After excluding these patients, 426 with complete baseline data were included in the final analyses ( Fig. 1 ). Figure 1. Patient recruitment flowchart in this study. TWMU CVD: Tokyo Women’s Medical University Cerebral Vessel Disease, CDR: Clinical Dementia Rating, MACE: major cardiovascular event, MMSE: Mini-Mental State Examination, mRS: modified Rankin Scale, AD: Alzheimer’s disease MRI protocol and assessment The participants underwent a brain MRI scan within one year before entry into the registry using a 1.5-Tesla (T) MRI scanner (Ingenia Ambition 1.5T, Philips Healthcare, Best, Netherlands; Magnetom Avanto Fit 1.5T, Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany). MRI assessments were performed as previously described ( 11 ). In brief, WMH lesions were considered significant if periventricular hyperintensity Fazekas grade 3 (extending into the deep white matter) and/or deep WMH Fazekas 2-3 (confluent or early confluent) were present ( 12 ). Medial temporal atrophy (MTA) was rated from 0 (absent) to 3 (severe) according to previous guidelines ( 11 ). The intracranial arteries were examined using time-of-flight magnetic resonance angiography (MRA), and significant intracranial artery stenosis was defined as stenosis or occlusion ≥50%. Carotid ultrasound The mean intima-media thickness (IMT) was calculated by averaging the thicknesses at 12 sites: the near and far walls of the right and left distal common carotid arteries, bifurcation, and internal carotid artery ( 13 ). Carotid stenosis was defined as a ≥50% reduction in the diameter of the carotid artery. Risk factors and serum homocysteine measurement Hypertension was defined as a blood pressure ≥140/90 mmHg or the use of antihypertensive medications. Diabetes mellitus was defined as a fasting plasma glucose level ≥126 mg/dL, glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) level ≥6.5%, or the administration of antidiabetic therapies. Dyslipidemia was defined as low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) level ≥140 mg/dL, total cholesterol level ≥220 mg/dL, triglyceride level ≥150 mg/dL, or administration of cholesterol-lowering therapies. Smoking status was evaluated based on current smoking habits. Baseline kidney function was determined based on the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR). Chronic kidney disease (CKD) was defined as an eGFR of ＜60 mL/min/1.73 m 2 . Large artery disease (LAD) was defined as intracranial artery stenosis on MRA and/or carotid stenosis on ultrasonography. Peripheral blood samples were collected via a venipuncture after overnight fasting. The serum homocysteine levels at enrollment were measured using high-performance liquid chromatography with fluorometric detection (SRL, Tokyo, Japan). Follow-up and outcomes Patients were followed up every 3 months in the outpatient clinic until March 2023. If the patient could not be followed up, then a relative or caregiver was interviewed via telephone. We could not reach the 25 patients who stopped the follow-up visits by telephone. The patients were therefore censored at the last visit. The outcomes included stroke, MACE, and AD dementia. MACE included nonfatal stroke, nonfatal acute coronary syndrome, major peripheral artery disease, and vascular death, as described previously ( 11 ). Stroke was clinically defined as a focal neurological deficit confirmed by MRI or computed tomography. Acute coronary syndrome was diagnosed in accordance with the standard criteria (compatible clinical history with changes in electrocardiogram or cardiac enzyme concentrations). Vascular death was defined as fatal acute coronary syndrome, fatal stroke, or other cardiovascular-related deaths. AD dementia was diagnosed according to the standardized criteria ( 14 ). Statistical analyses All analyses were performed using the JMP version 17 Pro (SAS Institute, Cary, USA). Quantitative variables are expressed as the mean [standard deviation (SD)] or median (interquartile range), whereas categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages. Comparisons between the two groups were performed using ANOVA or the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for quantitative variables and the chi-square test for qualitative variables. Patients were divided into three groups according to their serum homocysteine level tertiles: 1st tertile, ≤8.2 nmol/mL (n=141); 2nd tertile, 8.3-10.9 nmol/mL (n=141); and 3rd tertile, ≥11.0 nmol/mL (n=144). They were also divided at the cut-off value of 15.0 nmol/L because previous studies showed that those with levels ≥15.0 nmol/L had a higher risk of vascular events ( 1 ). Event rates were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method, and intergroup differences were assessed using the log-rank test. Cox proportional hazard regression models were used to evaluate the association between groups and the risk of events by calculating hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). In a univariate analysis, all variables with p＜0.05, along with known related factors (i.e., age, sex, and years of education) were included for multivariable adjustments. For all analyses, differences were considered statistically significant at p＜0.05. Results The baseline characteristics of the study participants are presented in Table 1 . AD: Alzheimer disease, IMT: intima-media complex thickness, LAD: large artery disease, MACE: major adverse cardiovascular events, MTA: medial temporal atrophy, WMH: white matter hyperintensity Discussion This study showed that serum homocysteine levels could predict stroke and cardiovascular events, but not AD dementia, five years later. Although high levels of serum homocysteine have been shown to be associated with vascular lesions in the brain and carotid artery and cognitive function, the findings of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) using homocysteine-lowering therapy remain controversial. Two RCTs using patients at high vascular risk did not show the benefit of homocysteine-lowering treatment, that is administration of folic acid and vitamin B6 and B12, in MACE ( 15 , 16 ). For patients with recent ischemic stroke, two RCTs did not show a clear benefit of homocysteine-lowering treatment for recurrent stroke ( 17 , 18 ). However, in the VITATOPS trial, homocysteine-lowering treatment reduced the risk of recurrent stroke in patients with lacunar stroke and normal kidney function ( 18 ). The authors proposed that the use of cyanocobalamin as vitamin B12 and the enrollment of patients with kidney dysfunction may explain the lack of any clear benefit of homocysteine-lowering treatment in their study ( 19 ). In a meta-analysis of the Cochrane Library, they concluded that homocysteine-lowering therapy had no effect on the prevention of myocardial infarction but it seemed to show a significant protective effect on the development of stroke ( 20 ). In the present study, we did not find any association between the serum homocysteine levels and incident AD dementia, although previous cohort studies have shown positive results between homocysteine and all-cause dementia. Differences in the follow-up period may explain this finding. The follow-up period in this study was approximately 4.6 years, which was much shorter than that in studies showing a positive association, in which the mean follow-up period ranged from 8 years to 10.2 years ( 4 - 6 ). In contrast, other studies have shown either a weak association or no association between homocysteine and AD dementia with 3-7 years of follow-up ( 7 , 21 ). Previous studies have shown that differences in dementia risk in the Kaplan-Meier curve between high and low homocysteine groups began to appear 4-5 years after enrollment ( 4 , 7 ). The involvement of homocysteine in neurodegeneration has been shown in brain autopsy samples ( 22 ), but it may take a long time, as seen in amyloid beta deposition in AD dementia. Most RCTs using homocysteine-lowering therapy employed 2-3 years of intervention and did not show a protective effect on cognition in healthy individuals ( 23 ) or patients with AD dementia ( 24 ) and cerebral infarction ( 25 ). However, lowering homocysteine with vitamin B for two years has been shown to reduce the progression of brain atrophy, which might later influence the cognitive function ( 26 ). In a cross-sectional study, the serum homocysteine levels were reported to be higher in all dementia subtypes, including AD and vascular dementia, than in the controls ( 27 ). Therefore, our results do not deny the involvement of homocysteine in AD dementia, and its investigation therefore requires a longer follow-up period, such as 10 years. This study was associated with several limitations. First, the serum homocysteine levels were measured only once at enrollment; therefore, the importance of changes in the level of incident events could not be examined. As this was a single-hospital-based study conducted in Japan, the results cannot be generalized to healthy individuals. The number of events and follow-up periods were small and too short to investigate AD dementia. Lastly, information about dietary intake of vitamin B6, B12, and folic acid, which could influence serum homocysteine levels, was lacking. In conclusion, high serum homocysteine levels, especially ≥15.0 nmol/L, increased the risk of incident stroke and vascular events but not AD dementia during a 4.65-year follow-up of patients with vascular risk. Our results suggest that homocysteine is more closely associated with atherosclerosis than with AD dementia. The authors state that they have no Conflict of Interest (COI).
