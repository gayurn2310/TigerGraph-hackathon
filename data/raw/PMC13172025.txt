Spatiotemporal brain transcriptomics reveal risk gene hot-spots in major neuropsychiatric disorders

Brain development is guided by dynamic gene expression programs that vary across brain regions and developmental stages. Although numerous risk genes for neuropsychiatric disorders have been identified, the spatiotemporal contexts in which they act remain unclear. Here we show the spatiotemporal expression patterns of genome-wide risk gene sets across 15 neuropsychiatric traits, including autism, attention deficit hyperactive disorder, obsessive compulsive disorder, major depression, bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, epilepsy, Alzheimer’s disease and Parkinson’s disease, using bulk and single-cell transcriptomic data from the human brain. We identify trait-specific spatiotemporal enrichment patterns, allowing a classification of disorders into prenatally and postnatally enriched groups that align with known ages of disease onset. Integration with brain imaging datasets and gene co-expression network analysis further identifies synaptic development and function, as well as RNA processing during early brain development, in neuropsychiatric risk. These findings provide a systems-level framework linking genetic risk to neurodevelopmental and neuroanatomical contexts.

Introduction Neuropsychiatric disorders frequently manifest during specific developmental windows, suggesting a crucial role for the timing of gene expression in the pathogenesis of these conditions 1 , 2 . Advances in genomics have facilitated the identification of extensive genome-wide risk gene sets associated with various psychiatric and neurologic disorders 3 – 10 . These risk genes, however, often exert minor to median effects individually, contributing to disease mechanisms in an additive or interactive manner 11 . The complexity of these disorders underscores the importance of understanding how the spatiotemporal dynamics of risk gene sets’ expression contribute to disease mechanisms and clinical characteristics. A comprehensive analysis of the spatiotemporal dynamics of the transcriptome could reveal the anatomical and developmental patterns underlying brain maturation and regional specification, providing deeper insights into their intrinsic molecular mechanisms, and indicating potential “hot-spots” for neuropsychiatric risks operates 1 , 12 . Previous studies have highlighted the correlation between the temporal expression patterns of risk genes and the age of disease onset for polygenic neuropsychiatric disorders 13 . However, the extensive spatiotemporal pattern of risk gene expression—when, where, and which cell types in the brain these genes are co-expressed during development—and its role in distinguishing between different neuropsychiatric disorders remain less explored. This study aims to bridge this knowledge gap by investigating the spatiotemporal expression patterns of disorder-associated risk gene sets derived from data-driven, hypothesis-free genome-wide studies across a broad range of neuropsychiatric traits, including intelligence quotient (IQ), autism spectrum disorders (ASD), attention deficit hyperactive disorder (ADHD), Tourette’s Syndrome (TS), obsessive compulsive disorder (OCD), anorexia nervosa (ANO), Neuroticism, Panic disorder, major depressive disorder (MDD), bipolar disorder (BIP), schizophrenia (SZ), Epilepsy, Alzheimer’s disease (AD), and Parkinson’s disease (PD). We hypothesize that these spatiotemporal expression patterns not only relate to key clinical characteristics, such as developmental timing and symptom profiles, but also provide a framework for differentiating neuropsychiatric disorders at the molecular level. By quantifying the risk gene set expression enrichment that was defined as the overrepresentation of disorder-associated risk genes relative to background gene expression in distinct brain regions, developmental stages, and cell types, we seek to identify neuronal and developmental “hot-spots” that may reflect periods and locations of heightened vulnerability to these disorders. To address these questions, we systematically investigated the spatiotemporal expression patterns of risk gene sets across major neuropsychiatric traits. By integrating genome-wide genetic studies with bulk and single-cell transcriptomic datasets from the developing and adult human brain, we examined how risk genes are distributed across brain regions, developmental stages, and cell types. We further linked these expression patterns to disorder-related brain structural alterations using large-scale neuroimaging data and identified spatiotemporal co-expression gene modules associated with specific functional pathways and neuropsychiatric risk. Together, these analyses reveal shared and disorder-specific spatiotemporal signatures of genetic risk and provide insights into the neurodevelopmental mechanisms underlying major neuropsychiatric disorders. Results Research schema The study employed a multi-layered bioinformatic approach to explore the neurodevelopmental mechanisms and putative therapeutic targets of polygenic neuropsychiatric disorders (Fig. 1 ). Landmark genome-wide association studies (GWAS), exome sequencing (ES), and similar hypothesis-free cardinal risk gene databases were used to identify polygenic risk genes associated with the neuropsychiatric traits (Supplementary Table 1 ). RNA-seq datasets from the BrainSpan developmental human brain project, Allen Human Brain Atlas (AHBA) microarray data, and single-cell RNA-seq data were integrated into the analysis pipeline. Gene-set expression enrichment analysis across spatiotemporal transcriptomes identified key patterns of risk gene expression for different neuropsychiatric disorders. The spatiotemporal expression patterns of risk genes linked these genes to disease-relevant brain regions, neural circuits, ages of onset, and diagnostic categories. To further validate these associations, we leveraged summary statistics from the Enhancing Neuro Imaging Genetics through Meta Analysis (ENIGMA) consortium for major neuropsychiatric disorders, which supported the correlations between risk gene set expression enrichment and disorder-related structural brain alterations. Finally, the weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA)-based regression model revealed when, where, and in which cell types the co-expressed gene modules were upregulated and enriched with neuropsychiatric risk genes. In parallel, the module gene-enriched Gene Ontology (GO) terms suggest putatively involved pathogenic molecular mechanisms, culminating in the identification of potential spatiotemporal-specific molecular mechanisms, biomarkers, and therapeutic targets. Putatively early and late expression enrichment control gene sets showed the expected spatiotemporal enrichment patterns To validate the feasibility of using the spatiotemporal expression patterns of risk gene sets to infer key pathogenic “hot-spots”—and thereby estimate the peak age of onset and the vulnerable brain regions for various neuropsychiatric disorders—we employed both positive and negative controls. As positive controls, we selected risk gene sets expected to show stage-specific enrichment, such as expression Quantitative Trait Locus associated genes identified from fetal or adult brain tissues. As negative controls, we used randomly generated gene sets with varying sample sizes. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 1a , gene set expression enrichment analysis revealed that risk genes associated with IQ, as well as those with anticipated enrichment in fetal brain tissue, exhibited prominent expression enrichment during prenatal development. In contrast, risk gene sets derived from the PsychENCODE Phase 1 integrative analysis—largely based on adult brain transcriptomes—displayed peak enrichment during adulthood (Supplementary Data 1 ). By comparison, randomly generated gene sets showed no consistent spatial or temporal pattern of expression enrichment. These control analyses collectively support the validity of our approach in capturing disease-relevant spatiotemporal enrichment signatures from gene expression data. Developmental expression enrichment patterns of risk gene sets align with the peak age of onset across major neuropsychiatric disorders To explore the relationship between the developmental dynamics of risk gene set expression enrichment and the corresponding peak ages of onset across the 15 traits, we ordered all BrainSpan samples from early to late developmental stages and arranged the 15 traits according to their peak ages of onset, from early to late. As shown in Fig. 2a , the genome-wide unbiased risk gene sets exhibited progressively delayed expression enrichment during brain development—from early prenatal stages to middle adulthood—in association with increasing peak ages of disease onset(Supplementary Data 2 ) 14 . Namely, traits exhibiting earlier enrichment of risk gene expression tended to have younger peak ages of onset (Fig. 2d ). This trend suggests an additive or interactive polygenic contribution to the pathogenesis of these disorders and traits. Consistent with previous studies 13 , most of the early onset psychiatric disorders—including ASD, ADHD, TS, and neuroticism—demonstrated relatively early developmental enrichment of risk gene expression. In contrast, late onset neurological disorders such as AD, PD, and epilepsy showed obviously postponed enrichment patterns (Fig. 2a ). As expected, hierarchical clustering revealed that risk gene sets derived from different datasets or mapping strategies for the same disorder generally clustered closely (Supplementary Fig. 1b ). This analysis also revealed a clear separation between risk gene sets predominantly expressed prenatally versus those expressed postnatally in development (Supplementary Fig. 1b, c ). Notably, the prenatal cluster was enriched for psychiatric disorders and neurodevelopmental-related traits (e.g., IQ, neuroticism), whereas the postnatal cluster was composed of neurological disorders and psychiatric conditions with bimodal onset ages or neurodegenerative features (e.g., OCD, schizophrenia, and MDD) 15 , 16 . Interestingly, the schizophrenia risk gene sets derived from exome sequencing (SZes) and GWAS (SZgw) displayed divergent developmental trajectories (Fig. 2a ). Specifically, SZgw showed predominant postnatal enrichment, while SZes showed relative enrichment in prenatal to early infancy stages (before 1 year of age), consistent with evidence that rare exonic variants in SZ are more strongly associated with early neurodevelopmental disruptions and an earlier age at onset 17 , 18 . We then merged the risk gene sets identified by different studies and gene-mapping approaches for each trait (with the exception of SZ, which was separated into SZgw and SZes due to pronounced disease heterogeneity) to generate 15 integrated risk gene set enrichment profiles for head-to-head comparisons. Overall, the 15 merged gene sets yielded clustering patterns comparable to those observed using individual gene sets (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Data 3 ). This observation was further supported by highly consistent clustering of additional risk gene sets for five traits (ADHD-, BIP-, OCD-, AD-, and PD-Current), incorporating the recently published genome-wide study results (Supplementary Fig. 2 ). Spatial enrichment patterns of neuropsychiatric risk gene sets implicate disease-relevant brain regions and circuits To enable straightforward intra-disorder comparisons of spatial enrichment patterns across major brain structures, we arranged all BrainSpan samples from anterior to posterior and from cortical to subcortical regions, as shown in Fig. 3 . Across all 15 traits, risk gene set enrichment patterns exhibited greater cross-trait similarity in cortical samples than in subcortical samples, as supported by correlation-based analyses in Fig. 3b . Consistent with the above spatial comparisons, region-wise quantitative analyses using the frontal cortex as the reference revealed disorder-specific enrichment patterns across major brain structures (Supplementary Fig. 3 and Supplementary Data 4 ). Notably, ADHD risk genes exhibited significantly higher expression enrichment in the parietal and temporal cortex, thalamus, and striatum compared with the frontal cortex. Similarly, risk genes for panic disorder showed significantly elevated enrichment in the ganglionic eminence (GE), striatum, and amygdala. These patterns align with the well-established roles of the thalamus in attention processes, and of the amygdala, striatum, and GABAergic interneurons in emotion-regulation and arousal 19 – 21 . These results support prevailing neurobiological theories implicating thalamic dysfunction in ADHD, as well as amygdala and GABAergic involvement in panic disorder 21 , 22 . Moreover, the striatum, increasingly recognized as a key node in anxiety-related circuits 23 , 24 , potentially implicates a common disruption in valence processing mechanisms across ADHD and panic disorder 25 , 26 . For OCD, risk gene sets were significantly overrepresented in the striatum, thalamus, and cerebellum–consistent with the cortico-striato-thalamo-cortical (CSTC) circuitry model 27 . Recent neuroimaging studies have highlighted abnormal microstructural and functional connectivity between the cerebellum and CSTC circuits in OCD 28 , further corroborating our findings. Additional enrichment “hot-spots” were observed in the amygdala for neuroticism, panic disorder, and MDD, a key region for emotion processing that has been consistently implicated in the pathophysiology of these disorders 29 , 30 . In contrast, risk gene sets for IQ, ASD, TS, and SZ did not show prominent subcortical expression enrichment but instead exhibited relative depletion in the thalamus, striatum, and cerebellum in some of these disorders, suggesting a predominantly cortical origin of pathogenesis of these disorders. Spatiotemporal enrichment patterns of risk gene sets differentiate neuropsychiatric disorder subgroups Considering the spatiotemporal enrichment patterns of disease risk gene expression as an intermediate phenotype, it is possible to stratify neuropsychiatric disorders based on shared developmental and anatomical features, thereby facilitating cross-disorder comparisons and integrative analyses. To separately characterize the spatial and temporal relationships among major neuropsychiatric disorders, we performed principal component analysis (PCA) on the Z -scored rank biserial correlation coefficients representing enrichment of risk gene expression across all BrainSpan samples. This analysis incorporated all 15 traits and enabled the identification of orthogonal components capturing shared temporal and spatial variation in risk gene expression (Fig. 4 , Supplementary Fig. 4 , and Supplementary Data 5 ). The temporal dimensions represented by PC1 and PC2 together effectively segregated the 15 traits into prenatal and postnatal clusters (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig. 4a ). PC1 primarily reflected a global prenatal-to-postnatal contrast in risk gene enrichment (Fig. 4c ), whereas PC2 captured rapid perinatal dynamics characterized by a transition from relatively low expression during late prenatal stages to increased expression during late infancy (Fig. 4d ). Disorders such as epilepsy, PD, MDD, and SZgw showed pronounced loadings along PC2, consistent with their marked perinatal expression shifts observed in Fig. 2a–c . In contrast, the spatial component PC4 was strongly associated with cerebellar enrichment (Fig. 4e ; Supplementary Fig. 4b ). Using the frontal cortex as a reference, risk genes for OCD, AD, and PD exhibited relatively higher enrichment in the cerebellum, whereas ADHD, IQ, panic disorder, SZgw, and epilepsy showed comparatively lower cerebellar enrichment, suggesting that cerebellar involvement is confined to a subset of neuropsychiatric and neurodegenerative disorders. Additional PCA on the 15 original risk gene sets, together with five updated risk gene datasets incorporating recently published GWAS results, validated the overall spatiotemporal clustering patterns of the 15 traits (Supplementary Fig. 2b–d ). However, clustering results for disorders with relatively small risk gene sets, such as OCD and ADHD, may be influenced by changes in gene composition. Taken together, the temporal and spatial PCA dimensions delineate biologically meaningful subgroups of neuropsychiatric disorders based on shared spatiotemporal expression patterns of their respective risk gene sets. Spatiotemporal enrichment patterns, but not the shared risk genes, drive the classification of the traits To exclude the possibility that trait clustering was driven by shared risk genes across datasets, we quantified gene set overlap among the 15 traits using a pairwise Jaccard similarity test. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 5a–c , traits within the same cluster, such as SZes, ASD, ANO, TS, and ADHD in the “Prenatal” cluster, exhibited minimal gene overlap. Overall, pairwise Jaccard similarities among the 15 traits were low, with the highest ( J = 0.15) observed between MDD and neuroticism (Supplementary Fig. 5c, d and Supplementary Data 6 ). Other traits within the same spatiotemporal cluster, including SZgw, AD, PD, and epilepsy in the “Postnatal” group, showed generally lower similarities with each other than with traits from the “Prenatal” cluster, reinforcing the notion that clustering reflects convergent expression dynamics rather than shared genetic architecture. Assessing the biological relevance of risk gene expression patterns using ENIGMA brain morphometry To further assess the biological relevance of the spatiotemporal expression enrichment patterns of risk gene sets, we examined their associations with meta-analyzed structural brain alterations from the ENIGMA consortium. A total of 231 anatomically defined brain regions were extracted from six adult human donors in the AHBA microarray dataset. Of these, 37 regions overlapped with the ENIGMA summary data and were retained for visualization and correlation analyses. The enrichment patterns of risk gene expression for eight available ENIGMA neuropsychiatric disorders were projected onto a FreeSurfer brain template (Fig. 5a–d, i–l and Supplementary Data 7 ). Notably, risk genes for ASD, ADHD, and BIP showed widespread expression enrichment across the neocortex. In comparison, SZ and MDD risk genes exhibited relatively stronger enrichment in the occipital cortex—particularly in the lingual and cuneus regions. Furthermore, subcortical structures showed marked disorder-specific patterns. For instance, the striatum—including the putamen, caudate nucleus, and nucleus accumbens—was preferentially enriched with OCD risk genes, whereas the hippocampus showed enrichment for risk genes associated with ASD, BIP, SZes, and epilepsy. Together, these results indicate that the relationship between spatial “hot-spots” of risk gene expression and macroscopic brain structural alterations is disorder-specific, with some conditions showing convergent negative associations suggestive of cumulative structural vulnerability, whereas others exhibit positive or null associations, potentially reflecting more functionally mediated effects. Importantly, enrichment patterns derived from adult brain tissue in the AHBA differed from those observed across multiple developmental stages in the BrainSpan dataset. This discrepancy underscores the value of spatiotemporal analyses that integrate both developmental timing and regional specificity, as cross-sectional adult data alone may overlook risk genes that exert pathogenic effects primarily during specific developmental windows. Such temporal specificity may also partially explain why significant associations between regional risk gene enrichment and brain volume alterations were not observed for certain disorders. WGCNA-derived co-expression modules and their functional implications in neuropsychiatric disorders We applied WGCNA to the BrainSpan transcriptomic dataset to identify modules of co-expressed genes across developmental stages and brain regions. A total of 41 co-expression modules were initially identified (Supplementary Fig. 6 and Supplementary Data 8 ), of which 32 modules exhibited significant spatial or temporal expression enrichment (defined as at least one dummy variable with a B value &gt; 0.05 in the linear regression models) and were retained for downstream analysis (Supplementary Data 9 ). Notably, modules with distinct spatiotemporal expression profiles exhibited different enrichments in different neuropsychiatric disorders (Supplementary Fig. 7 and Supplementary Data 10 ). After false discovery rate (FDR) correction across the 41 modules for each of the disorders, a total of six modules show significant enrichment in eight traits. Given the substantial variation in module size (ranging from 56 to 3030 as shown in Supplementary Fig. 7c ), larger modules tended to achieve stronger statistical significance, whereas smaller modules occasionally exhibited higher odds ratios (ORs) but did not always reach significance. Therefore, enrichment results were interpreted by jointly considering effect sizes (ORs) and statistical significance, rather than relying solely on P values. Importantly, allowing smaller module sizes in WGCNA may enhance the detection of functionally specific and biologically coherent gene sets, providing complementary resolution for identifying molecular pathways potentially relevant to neuropsychiatric disease risk. A direct comparison of modules obtained with a minimum module size of 40 versus 120 revealed that most modules formed corresponding pairs with highly similar spatiotemporal expression patterns and risk gene enrichment, indicating that the core co-expression structures are largely preserved across parameter settings (Supplementary Figs. 7 – 9 ). Nevertheless, the choice of minimum module size did influence disease risk gene enrichment for specific modules. For example, the module originally identified as ME brown and associated with AD risk at a minimum module size of 40 corresponded, based on gene functional annotations and spatiotemporal enrichment patterns, to the ME yellow module when the minimum module size was increased to 120 (Supplementary Data 11 ). However, under this parameter setting, ME yellow was no longer significantly enriched for AD risk genes but instead showed a stronger association with OCD risk genes. Interestingly, although certain modules (e.g., royalblue and plump1) exhibited pronounced hippocampal or thalamic enrichment, they did not show significant enrichment for risk-associated genes across the 15 neuropsychiatric traits examined, suggesting that these modules may represent molecular networks underlying region-specific physiological or homeostatic functions rather than mechanisms directly involved in neuropsychiatric disease. Furthermore, the reference level (thalamus versus neocortex) for the spatial-dummy variables didn’t impact the spatiotemporal dynamics observed (Supplementary Fig. 10 ). These observations further support the robustness of our findings and suggest that the identified modules reflect genuine biological organization rather than sensitivity to the choice of module size. To further characterize the cellular context of these spatiotemporal modules, we examined the expression enrichment of module genes across major brain cell types from different developmental stages in the linear regression model (Supplementary Fig. 11 and Supplementary Data 12 ). This analysis revealed distinct cell-type-specific expression enrichment patterns among modules, reflecting differential deviations in standardized expression levels relative to the reference. Furthermore, the divergent cell-type enrichment patterns observed between the glia-enriched and adult-neuron-enriched modules (defined by the cell type exhibiting the largest regression coefficient among all cell types for each module) were independently replicated using an additional single-cell transcriptome dataset derived from the adult human brain (Supplementary Fig. 12 and Supplementary Data 13 ), further supporting the robustness of these findings. To further prioritize biologically relevant modules, we focused on 16 that exhibited the strongest enrichment for disorder-associated risk genes by selecting the top three modules per trait based on P values (Fig. 6c ). These modules were then integrated with single-cell transcriptomic enrichment profile to construct a composite map linking the spatial, temporal, and cellular dimensions of disease vulnerability (Fig. 6a–c ). Enriched GO terms for M7-9 include “Ach receptor”, “peptide receptor”, and “synaptic transmission” (Supplementary Fig. 13 ), in line with the thalamus functions and pathophysiology of PD. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 16 , the ADHD-associated gene FOXP2 (from M8) exhibited the strongest expression in the thalamus, consistent with its known role in sensorimotor and cognitive integration 20 . Furthermore, the M5 module gene PLP1 , which is overexpressed in both the thalamus and oligodendrocytes, exhibits prominent expression in the white matter of the neonatal marmoset brain and is markedly upregulated at 6 months of age, suggesting its potential involvement in the pathogenesis of ADHD. Additional examples include neuroticism-associated genes PAX6 and KDM3A (both from M12), which exhibited enriched expression in the GE, hippocampus, amygdala, and cerebellum (Supplementary Fig. 17 ). Likewise, cortex-enriched M14 genes- SYT6 (OCD-associated) and SATB1 (ASD-associated), were preferentially expressed in the cerebral cortex (Supplementary Fig. 18 ). These high-resolution, whole-brain ISH data from the neonatal marmoset provide independent validation of the disorder-related spatiotemporal “hot-spots” identified through WGCNA and regression analysis. Moreover, the distinct spatial expression profiles of these module/risk genes at cellular resolution provide a comprehensive overview of brain structures implicated in disease pathogenesis, including regions that may have been overlooked in prior studies. Discussion The findings of this study reveal significant insights into the spatiotemporal dynamics of risk gene expression and their implications for understanding neuropsychiatric disorders. Our analysis demonstrates that the spatiotemporal expression patterns of genome-wide risk gene sets are not only reflective of the temporal onset of these disorders but also serve as distinguishing features and pathogenesis extrapolations for different groups of neuropsychiatric conditions. The spatiotemporal expression enrichment patterns observed across neuropsychiatric disorder risk genes underscore the importance of developmental timing and regional specificity in disease pathogenesis. Consistent with prior studies, our results confirm that traits with earlier peak onset ages, such as IQ, ADHD, and ASD, show prenatal expression enrichment of risk genes 13 . In contrast, late-onset disorders, such as epilepsy, PD, and AD, display enriched gene expression during postnatal periods. The differentiation between prenatally and postnatally enriched disorders based on spatiotemporal gene expression supports the hypothesis that neurodevelopmental and neurodegenerative disorders have distinct transcriptional dynamics. Notably, the risk gene set spatiotemporal expression enrichment dynamics for certain disorders, such as MDD and SZ, exhibit dual peaks, corresponding to distinct phases of heightened susceptibility. Furthermore, prolonged OCD risk gene expression enrichment from prenatal to adulthood aligns with clinical observations of bimodal onset peaks for this disorder, suggesting that different pathogenic pathways may contribute to the disorder at different developmental stages 39 . Importantly, we also observed a convergent increase in risk gene expression during late infancy across both prenatally and postnatally enriched disorder groups. This period is characterized by elevated expression of genes from several co-expression modules (ME blue, ME darkorange, and ME skyblue3) that are enriched (even if not significant due to small sample sizes) for risk genes from multiple disorders. These modules are functionally associated with synaptic organization, signaling, and regulatory processes, indicating widespread engagement of neurodevelopmental pathways during this stage. Late infancy, therefore, appears to represent a transitional and highly plastic developmental window, during which diverse disease-associated genes and pathways are concurrently active across multiple cell types. Such convergence may help explain why genetic risk factors for otherwise distinct neuropsychiatric disorders show overlapping expression patterns during this period, potentially contributing to shared vulnerability and pleiotropic effects. Together, these findings highlight the importance of integrating temporal dynamics into neuropsychiatric disorder models, as focusing on dynamic windows of gene expression may reveal critical periods for risk convergence, early detection, and targeted intervention. Our study revealed disorder-specific yet spatially distributed “hot-spots” of risk gene expression, aligning with known neurobiological mechanisms while also reflecting the polygenic and network-level nature of neuropsychiatric disorders. For example, ADHD risk genes showed prominent increased expression enrichment in the thalamus and striatum, regions implicated in attention and sensorimotor integration 20 , 40 . Similarly, OCD risk genes were preferentially enriched in the CSTC circuits, consistent with established models of OCD pathophysiology 41 . The amygdala emerged as a key site for risk gene expression in emotion-related disorders such as neuroticism, MDD, and panic disorder, supporting its role in emotional processing abnormalities 30 . Interestingly, the different cortical subregions showed consistent risk gene expression patterns in most of the 15 traits, probably indicating widespread pathologies and cross-modality deficits in those disorders, with an exception for ADHD, which showed higher risk gene expression in the temporal and parietal cortex, in accordance with brain imaging studies which found abnormalities in the striatum, frontal, temporal, and parietal regions in children with ADHD 42 , 43 . These findings suggest that the spatial specificity of gene expression may offer critical insights into the underlying neural circuits and their roles in the pathogenesis of specific disorders. The observation that different risk gene sets for the same disorder generally clustered closely, and this spatiotemporal similarity could not be explained by shared risk genes, supports the robustness of this spatiotemporal expression enrichment pattern-based disorder differentiation and comparison. Moreover, the clustering of risk gene sets for the same disorder, such as schizophrenia, into both prenatal and postnatal enriched categories suggests the presence of complex, multi-phase pathogenic mechanisms. The validation of our findings using ENIGMA MRI datasets further underscores the translational potential of spatiotemporal gene expression analysis. Correlations between risk gene expression patterns and structural brain changes suggest that these “hot-spots” could serve as biomarkers for disease detection, monitoring, and therapeutic targeting. For instance, the negative correlation between MRI structural changes and risk gene expression in ASD, BIP, and schizophrenia highlights the potential for targeted interventions aimed at modulating these circuits during critical developmental periods. Single-cell transcriptomic analyses further revealed pronounced cell-type–specific enrichment patterns across neuropsychiatric traits, highlighting distinct cellular substrates underlying disease vulnerability. AD risk genes were preferentially enriched in glial populations, consistent with the central role of neuroinflammation and glial dysfunction in AD pathology. Panic disorder risk genes showed selective enrichment in astrocytes and inhibitory neurons, in line with their association with a GE–upregulated module. In addition, PD risk genes were preferentially enriched in thalamus-related modules, supporting longstanding evidence linking thalamic abnormalities to PD pathophysiology 44 . Gene co-expression network analyses further indicate that disruption of distinct biological processes at specific developmental windows may contribute to disorder risk. Enrichment of cell cycle-related pathways in the GE during early fetal development suggests that early neurodevelopmental dysregulation may play a role in neuroticism. In contrast, a perinatally overexpressed module enriched for AD risk genes was characterized by complement cascades and ECM–receptor interaction pathways, both implicated in AD 45 , 46 . The prominent perinatal activation of immune-related functions raises the possibility that early-life immune maturation and microglial activation may predispose the brain to exaggerated inflammatory responses and reduced cognitive reserve later in life 47 . Several pleiotropic modules enriched for risk genes across multiple neuropsychiatric disorders converged on excitatory neurons, particularly during early-mid prenatal and adulthood. These modules were functionally associated with synaptic processes, forebrain development, and transcriptional regulation. Accumulating genetic and functional evidence indicates that synaptic dysfunction—particularly within excitatory neuron circuits—is a convergent mechanism in major neuropsychiatric disorders, including IQ, ASD, BIP, MDD, SZ, and epilepsy 48 – 50 . Finally, distinct spatiotemporal expression profiles of the risk gene sets and the enriched biological pathways observed in this study may inform precision medicine approaches. Therapeutic strategies could focus on modulating gene expression or neural activity in specific regions during vulnerable developmental windows, potentially mitigating disease progression. Several limitations should be acknowledged. Some risk gene sets—such as those for ADHD, OCD, and panic disorder—are relatively small in size, which may limit statistical power. In addition, the identification of co-expression modules is influenced to some extent by parameter choices in WGCNA, particularly the minimum module size, which may affect module composition and downstream enrichment results. Finally, in the risk gene set enrichment analyses, all disease susceptibility genes were assigned equal weights. As a result, this approach does not emphasize the potentially disproportionate contributions of high-effect risk genes, nor does it capture gene- or isoform-specific effects arising from alternative splicing that may differentially influence disease risk. Future studies leveraging more refined analytical frameworks and higher-resolution datasets will be required to address these limitations. In conclusion, our findings demonstrate that the spatiotemporal co-expression of risk gene sets not only correlates with the onset of neuropsychiatric disorders but also provides critical insights into their differentiation and pathogenesis. These results highlight the potential for leveraging spatiotemporal gene expression patterns as biomarkers and therapeutic targets, offering additional avenues for advancing the diagnosis and treatment of major psychiatric disorders. Methods Risk gene sets expression enrichment analysis in the brain spatiotemporal transcriptome Curation of genome-wide risk genes for the 15 traits To avoid bias in the gene-sets spatiotemporal expression enrichment analysis in this study, only risk genes derived from data-driven, hypothesis-free genome-wide association/sequencing studies or similar databases were included. The up-to-date published GWAS of IQ 51 , 10 psychiatric disorders, including ASD 52 , ADHD 53 , TS 8 , OCD 54 , ANO 9 , neuroticism 55 , panic disorder 6 , MDD 3 , 56 , BIP 5 , 57 and schizophrenia (SZgw) 4 , 58 , 3 neurological disorders, including AD 10 , 59 , PD 60 and epilepsy 61 , as well as exome sequencing studies of ASD 7 and schizophrenia (SZes) 62 were carefully investigated to curate unbiased genome-wide risk gene sets for each trait (Supplementary Table 1 ). For some of those studies, multiple risk gene sets with different gene mapping strategies or criteria were selected for intra-trait comparison of their spatiotemporal expression patterns. While for overall cross-trait comparisons, the risk gene sets of different mapping strategies for the same trait were merged to form a pan risk gene set for the 15 traits. Hence, we can have head-to-head comparisons of their risk gene spatiotemporal expression patterns. Preparation of the developmental human brain transcriptome data Complete RNA sequencing datasets were obtained from the Allen Institute. The dataset encompasses 524 transcriptomic profiles from 26 anatomically defined brain regions across 42 human postmortem donors 12 , 63 . These donors included both males and females, spanning developmental stages from 8 post-conception weeks (PCW) to middle adulthood (approximately 40 years old) and represented diverse ethnic backgrounds. Detailed information regarding sample collection, sequencing protocols, and quality control procedures is available in the technical white paper 64 . To remove redundancy in the expression matrix, genes with duplicated names were deduplicated by averaging their RPKM values, resulting in a final dataset containing 47,808 unique genes for downstream analysis. Preparation of the adult human brain transcriptome data The AHBA provides a high-resolution transcriptional profile of the adult human brain 65 , 66 . The dataset encompasses samples from six neurotypical adult donors, ranging in age from 24 to 57 years. Transcriptome profiling was obtained from 3702 spatially-resolved samples that cover 231 anatomically defined brain regions. Data preprocessing and normalization were conducted by the Allen Institute using a multistep pipeline to correct for array-specific biases, batch effects, and dissection-related variability. For downstream analysis, the maximum expression value across multiple probes was selected for each of the 20,737 unique RefSeq genes per sample. To generate an averaged transcriptomic map across individuals, all 3702 sample-level expression profiles were pooled, and mean expression values for each gene were calculated across replicated brain regions using R (v4.3.0). Expression enrichment of risk gene sets within each brain region was then assessed, and regions were manually matched to ENIGMA-defined anatomical areas to enable cross-model correlation analyses. Statistics and reproducibility The Mann–Whitney U test, a non-parametric statistical method commonly employed in gene set enrichment analysis, was used to evaluate the expression enrichment of risk gene sets across the transcriptomes. Briefly, RPKM values from the deduplicated expression matrix were ranked within each sample, with higher ranks indicating relative expression enrichment. For each transcriptome, genes from the risk gene set were mapped to the ranked list and treated as the risk group, while the remaining genes served as the non-risk control group. To quantify the degree of enrichment, the Rank-Biserial correlation coefficient ( r ), a non-parametric effect size metric ranging from −1 to 1, was calculated. This coefficient represents the probability that a randomly selected gene from the risk group has a higher expression rank than a randomly selected gene from the control group. One-sided P values for each r were calculated and corrected by FDR across the samples for each gene set. To compare the spatiotemporal enrichment patterns of risk gene expression across different traits, sample-wise r values for each risk gene set were Z -score normalized. The normalized values were subsequently clustered using the R package “ComplexHeatmap” (v2.17.0) to visualize shared or distinct expression “hot-spots” across brain regions and developmental stages. The risk gene sets of different mapping strategies for the same trait were then merged to generate a unified (pan) risk gene set for the 15 traits, in order to validate the clustering patterns observed using individual gene sets. Furthermore, updated risk gene sets for 5 of the 15 traits, curated from the most recent publications, were used to replicate the analysis. All transcriptomic datasets used in this study were processed and analyzed independently, and no cross-dataset merging or joint normalization was performed. Instead, a consistent gene set enrichment framework was applied separately to each dataset, and results were compared across datasets for validation. The enrichment of different co-expression modules in the developmental single cell transcriptomes was partly replicated in the adult single cell datasets. The ENIGMA structure changes and correlation with disorder risk gene enrichment Structural MRI alterations associated with major neuropsychiatric disorders were obtained from the ENIGMA Toolbox summary statistics ( https://enigma-toolbox.readthedocs.io/en/latest/pages/04.loadsumstats/index.html ). To enable cross-modal integration, anatomical correspondences between ENIGMA-defined brain regions and AHBA microarray samples were manually established based on the AHBA anatomical reference and prior published mappings. Structural measures from the left and right hemispheres in the ENIGMA dataset were averaged to generate a unified value for each region. The Lateral Ventricles were excluded due to their non-parenchymal and non-functional characteristics. A total of 37 brain regions with clear cross-atlas correspondence were retained for correlation analyses. To quantify the relationship between risk gene set expression enrichment ( r ) and structural alterations (d_icv), Spearman correlation analysis was performed across the 37 regions. For spatial visualization, r values across the matched brain regions were projected onto the FreeSurfer average surface (fsaverage5) brain template using Python packages enigmatoolbox.plotting, and matplotlib (Python 3.12). Weighted gene co-expression network (WGCNA) analysis Gene co-expression modules were constructed from the BrainSpan developmental transcriptomes using the R package WGCNA (v1.72-1). Genes with low expression (total RPKM across all samples &lt;10) were removed. Outlier samples were identified and excluded by hierarchical clustering based on inter-sample expression distances, using a cut height of 100,000 on the sample dendrogram. For the remaining 26,598 genes in 521 qualified samples. Pairwise Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated across all samples. A soft-threshold power of 7 was used to achieve approximate scale-free topology ( R 2 &gt; 0.85). A signed weighted gene co-expression network was constructed using the blockwiseModules function. The network dendrogram was created using average linkage hierarchical clustering of the topological overlap dissimilarity matrix (1-TOM). Gene modules were detected using the Dynamic Tree Cut algorithm with a minimum module size of 40 or 120, and each module was summarized by its module eigengene (ME), defined as the first principal component of its expression matrix. Spatiotemporal expression modeling of WGCNA modules To characterize spatial and temporal expression patterns of co-expression modules, linear regression models were fitted with ME expression as the dependent variable. Brain regions and developmental stages were encoded as categorical predictors using dummy-variable coding, resulting in 11 spatial and 11 temporal indicators. One category from each variable group was designated as the reference level and absorbed into the model intercept. The thalamus and young adulthood were selected as the reference levels for spatial and temporal variables, respectively, as young adulthood represents a relatively mature developmental stage with stable transcriptomic profiles in the BrainSpan dataset, and the thalamus serves as a centrally connected subcortical structure with comparatively balanced expression across modules. Importantly, the choice of reference category does not affect relative differences among regression coefficients or the identification of spatiotemporal expression patterns, but only determines the interpretation of model parameters. Predicted ME expression levels for each spatial or temporal category were obtained by summing the corresponding regression coefficients with the intercept, enabling intuitive visualization of relative spatiotemporal expression patterns. Cell-type-specific expression patterns of module genes Cell-type-specific expression patterns of WGCNA modules were assessed using an analogous linear regression framework applied to single-cell transcriptomic data. Both developmental and adult human brain single-cell transcriptomic datasets were obtained from the PsychENCODE Consortium portal ( http://resource.psychencode.org ), including developmental data merged from PEC (Developmental), Darmanis et al. 67 , and Lake et al. 68 , and adult data merged from PEC (Adult) and Lake et al. 69 . All single-cell and single-nucleus transcriptomic datasets were generated and processed by the PsychENCODE Consortium using standardized Phase II processing pipelines prior to public release. These pipelines include raw read processing, alignment, quality control, and generation of gene-level expression matrices. The uniformly processed datasets ensure internal consistency within each resource. The developmental dataset comprises TPM values for 15,086 genes across 4249 single cells encompassing 35 annotated cell types, derived from both fetal (5–20 PCW) and adult neocortical brain tissues. For downstream analysis, expression values for all genes were first Z -score normalized across cells. Genes belonging to each WGCNA co-expression module were then identified, and within each cell, mean expression values were calculated separately for genes assigned to each module, yielding a cell-by-module expression matrix for regression analyses. The adult dataset comprises UMI counts for 17,176 genes across 27,412 individual brain cells. For the adult dataset, raw UMI counts were transformed to counts per million (CPM) using the R package edgeR (v3.43.8), followed by a log2( x + 1) transformation and Z -score normalization across cells. Module-wise mean expression levels were computed within each cell using the same procedure as for the developmental dataset. For regression modeling, cell types were encoded as dummy variables. Adult “neurons” and “Ex1” were used as the reference category for the developmental and adult datasets, respectively. Because expression data were Z -score normalized prior to model fitting, regression coefficients ( B values) represent standardized differences in mean module expression between each cell type and the reference category, facilitating direct comparison across modules and cellular populations. For each cell type, P values corresponding to regression coefficients across all WGCNA modules were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg FDR procedure. Risk gene enrichment in module genes Enrichment of risk-associated gene sets for the 15 neuropsychiatric traits within each WGCNA module was assessed using Fisher’s exact test. To control for multiple testing while preserving disorder-specific inference, FDR correction was applied separately within each disorder across modules, adjusting P values derived from Fisher’s exact tests. Function analysis of WGCNA module genes Module genes identified through WGCNA were subjected to functional enrichment analysis using the KEGG and GO databases. Analyses were performed with the R package clusterProfiler (v4.9.3), using a q value cutoff of 0.05. The minimum and maximum gene set sizes were set to 3 and 500, respectively. The top three GO terms in each category—biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF)—were visualized using bar plots. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed for genes in each of the 16 co-expression modules associated with neuropsychiatric disorders. Enrichment was conducted using the compareCluster function from the clusterProfiler R package, with a significance threshold of q &lt; 0.05. To further explore the relationships among enriched GO terms in the Biological Process category, REVIGO was used to generate semantic similarity-based visualizations. ISH data for exemplar module/risk genes in the marmoset brain The ISH data for representative module/risk genes in the neonatal and 6-month-old marmoset brain were obtained from our Marmoset Gene Atlas ( https://gene-atlas.brainminds.jp/ ). Briefly, marmoset brains were sectioned at a thickness of 28 μm, and 60 coronal sections were collected at 196 μm intervals for each probe. Of these, 20 sections spaced at 588 μm intervals were selected to comprehensively capture whole-brain spatial expression patterns of the target genes at single-cell resolution.
