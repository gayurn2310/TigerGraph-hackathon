Apolipoprotein E ε4-dependent associations between carotenoids and cognitive decline: Findings from the MIND (Mediterranean-DASH Intervention for Neurodegenerative delay) randomized controlled trial

Background Alzheimer disease (AD) prevention is a public health priority, yet the impact of dietary carotenoids on cognitive decline, particularly in apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4 carriers, remains unclear. Objectives The objective of this study was to examine whether the APOE ε4 genotype modifies the relationship between blood carotenoid concentrations and global cognition. Methods This study was conducted within the Mediterranean-Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension intervention for neurodegenerative delay trial, a 3-y randomized controlled trial comparing the effects of the Mediterranean-Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension intervention for neurodegenerative delay diet with the usual diet on global cognition in older adults. Eligible participants were ≥65 y, had a body mass index (in kg/m 2 ) &gt;25, a family history of AD, suboptimal diets, and a Montreal cognitive assessment score ≥22. The primary outcome was 3-y change in global cognitive function, assessed using a validated composite cognitive score converted to standardized units (SUs). Baseline plasma carotenoid concentrations were measured in a subgroup of participants ( n = 442). Mixed-effects models were used to test the interaction between APOE ε4 status and baseline carotenoid concentrations on cognitive trajectories. Results The mean age was 70.0 y for noncarriers ( n = 308) and 69.4 y for APOE ε4 carriers ( n = 134). Among APOE ε4 carriers, a 1-unit increment in plasma total carotenoids at baseline was associated with higher global cognitive scores [β = 0.17 SU; 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.06, 0.28 SU; P = 0.009]. Similar associations were observed for β-carotene (β = 0.13 SU; 95% CI: 0.05, 0.21 SU; P = 0.001), α-carotene (β = 0.09 SU; 95% CI: 0.02, 0.15 SU; P = 0.008), lutein plus zeaxanthin (β = 0.14 SU; 95% CI: 0.04, 0.25 SU; P = 0.008), lycopene (β = 0.17 SU; 95% CI: 0.07, 0.28 SU; P = 0.005), and β-cryptoxanthin (β = 0.13 SU; 95% CI: 0.05, 0.21 SU; P = 0.03). Associations in noncarriers were weaker or nonsignificant. Conclusions Higher plasma carotenoid concentrations were associated with slower cognitive decline in APOE ε4 carriers, potentially mitigating genetic risk. This trial was registered at clinicaltrials.gov as NCT02817074 .

Introduction Alzheimer disease (AD) is a devastating neurologic condition that significantly reduces quality of life. The absence of effective therapeutic options underscores the importance of primary prevention as a top public health priority. Epidemiologic evidence suggests that certain nutrients, including carotenoids (e.g., α- and β-carotene, lutein, and zeaxanthin) and tocopherols [ 1 , 2 ] as well as dietary patterns [i.e., Mediterranean, Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH), and Mediterranean-DASH Intervention for Neurodegenerative Delay (MIND)] are protective against the risk of AD [ 3 ], dementia [ 4 ], and cognitive decline [ 5 ]. However, findings from randomized controlled trials have been inconclusive [ 6 , 7 ], possibly because of interactions among age, diet, and apolipoprotein E (APOE) genotype. APOE ε4 is one of the strongest risk factors for late-onset AD. Late-onset AD, which typically manifests after age 65 years, accounts for the vast majority of AD cases and is strongly influenced by APOE ε4. In contrast, early-onset AD is relatively uncommon, is often driven by autosomal dominant mutations, and is less associated with APOE genotype. The APOE ε4 risk allele is associated with increased brain pathology [ 8 ], microglial inflammation [ 9 , 10 ], and disruptions in fatty acid metabolism between astrocytes and neurons [ 11 , 12 ], collectively contributing to neurodegeneration and cognitive decline. Emerging evidence suggests that the association between nutrients such as carotenoids and cognitive function may vary by APOE genotype, with APOE ε4 carriers benefiting from higher intakes of antioxidant nutrients [ 13 , 14 ], potentially because of their increased vulnerability to oxidative stress [ 15 ]. Despite this evidence, long-term human studies addressing effective nutritional approaches for preventing AD in APOE ε4 carriers remain limited, particularly considering the mechanisms through which this genetic variation confers an increased AD risk. Understanding how APOE ε4 influences response to specific nutrients is vital to developing personalized dietary strategies for high-risk populations. This study examines the relationship between circulating nutrient concentrations, APOE ε4 status, and their interaction with global cognition among participants from the MIND trial. Methods Study design The MIND study was a 3-year randomized controlled trial to examine the effects of the MIND diet compared to a usual diet on global cognition in older adults at risk of AD [ 6 ]. The MIND diet, a hybrid of the Mediterranean and DASH diets, consists of 15 components—10 brain-healthy food groups (green leafy vegetables, other vegetables, nuts, berries, beans, whole grains, fish, poultry, olive oil, and wine) and 5 food groups considered unhealthy for brain health (red meats, butter and stick margarine, cheese, pastries and sweets, and fried/fast food) [ 16 ]. For safety reasons, wine was excluded from dietary recommendations in this trial, resulting in a maximum adherence score of 14. Each dietary component is scored 0, 0.5, or 1.0 based on intake categorized into tertiles based on predefined cut-offs which reflect intake levels from low to high [ 16 ]. A score of 1 is assigned for higher intake of recommended brain-healthy foods and for lower intake of foods that are advised to be limited. The study was powered to test the effect of the MIND intervention versus the control intervention on the annual rate of change in the global cognitive score over 3 years. With a sample size of 300 participants per arm, the MIND trial has &gt;85% power to detect a between-group difference of 0.02 in the annual rate of cognitive decline (2-sided α = 0.05), considering a 5% dropout rate at each visit. A total of 604 participants were enrolled in the study. Participants were recruited from January 2017 through April 2018 at 2 sites in the Chicago and Boston areas. After initial screening, eligible participants were randomly assigned to the MIND intervention arm or the control intervention arm. The full trial protocol can be accessed in our previous publication [ 6 ]. Study population Recruitment and enrollment criteria were previously published. In brief, we enrolled 604 participants who were 65 years of age or older, overweight (BMI &gt;25), with a family history of AD, suboptimal diet (MIND score &lt;8, out of 14), and a Montreal cognitive assessment score of ≥22 (ranging from 0–30) [ 17 ]. Enrolled individuals agreed not to take nonprescribed vitamin supplements, multivitamins, or individual vitamin supplements, including vitamin E, folic acid, n ‒3 (ω-3) fatty acids, or carotenoids. Primary exclusion criteria included known allergies to the study foods; current use of medications for AD, Parkinson disease, or psychiatric disorders; diagnosed mental health conditions; substance abuse or high alcohol intake; recent history (within 3 months) of stroke or transient ischemic attack; prior brain injury, liver disease, hepatitis C, or HIV infection; medical conditions associated with significant weight fluctuations; and a cancer diagnosis within the past 5 years [ 16 ]. Biomarkers of adherence, including carotenoids, were measured in a subgroup of participants (N = 442), all of whom had APOE ε4 genotype data, and these data were included in the present analyses. Intervention Participants in the intervention arm (the MIND diet group) received foods emphasized in the MIND diet, including extra virgin olive oil, mixed nuts, and blueberries. Participants in the control intervention were instructed to maintain their usual diet. Both groups received the same intensity of dietary counseling led by registered dietitians, including counseling for mild weight loss. Dietary counseling for the MINDdiet group focused on incorporating foods featured in the MIND diet and encouraged using MIND diet recipes to achieve weight loss. The control arm received an equivalent frequency of consultation focused on calorie tracking, portion control, and behavioral modification to lose weight without changing their dietary pattern. Together with study dietitians, participants’ dietary intakes were monitored through coaching and goal setting. All participants were advised to maintain the same level of physical activity. After 3 years of intervention, 602 participants completed the trial with similar weight loss goals achieved by both groups [ 6 ]. Carotenoid nutrient panel Fasting blood samples were collected. Biomarkers were measured at the Nutrition Biochemistry Laboratory at the Harvard Chan School of Public Health [ 18 , 19 ]. In general, plasma concentrations of lutein plus zeaxanthin, β-cryptoxanthin, lycopene, α-carotene, and β-carotene were measured using the method described by Hess et al. [ 20 ] with some modifications. Samples were quantified by HPLC using a Restek Ultra C18 150 × 4.6 mm column with a 3 μ m particle size, encased in a Hitachi L-2350 column oven to prevent temperature fluctuations, and equipped with a Trident guard cartridge system (Restek Corporation). A mixture of acetonitrile, tetrahydrofuran, methanol, and a 1% ammonium acetate solution (68:22:7:3) was used as the mobile phase, at a flow rate of 1⋅1 mL/min, using a Hitachi Elite LaChrom HPLC system comprised of an L-2130 pump in isocratic mode, an L-2455 diode array detector (monitoring at 300 nm and 445 nm), and a programmable AS-2200 autosampler with a chilled sample tray. The system manager software (D-7000, version 3.0) was used for peak integration and data acquisition (Hitachi). The minimum detection limits in plasma were 7.74 mcg/L for α-carotene, 7.31 mcg/L for β-carotene, 5.51 mcg/L for β-cryptoxanthin, 8.49 mcg/L for lycopene, and 6.31 mcg/L for lutein plus zeaxanthin. Because lutein and zeaxanthin coelute on the chromatogram, the 2 are grouped and presented as lutein and zeaxanthin (combined). Internal quality control was monitored with duplicate high-concentration plasma and low-concentration plasma samples in each batch of samples [ 19 ]. For external quality control, the biomarker laboratory in the Nutrition Department at the Harvard Chan School of Public Health participates in the micronutrient measurement quality assurance program, a standardization program for carotenoid analysis sponsored by the National Institute of Standards and Technology , United States. APOE ε4 allele assessment Sanger sequencing was used to detect the rs429358 and rs7412 variants in exon 4 of the human APOE gene. Measurements were conducted at Medpace, Inc. The sequencing results had showed 100% concordance with the published genotypes of the samples. An indicator variable was created based on the 2 single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) for participants with 1 or more copies of the APOE ε4 risk allele. Cognitive assessment Details on cognitive assessment were published previously. In brief, cognition was assessed with an established battery of 12 tests [ 21 ]. There were 5 tests for episodic memory (Word list memory, Word list recall, and Word list recognition) [ 22 ], East Boston story immediate recall, and East Boston story delayed recall [ 23 ]; 2 tests of semantic memory (category fluency: fruits and vegetables [ 22 ] and the multilingual naming test [ 24 ]); 2 tests of executive function (Trail Making Test B [ 25 ] and the Flanker Inhibitory Control and Attention Test from the NIH Toolbox [ 26 ]); and 3 tests of perceptual speed (Oral Symbol Digit Modalities Test [ 27 ], Pattern Comparison Test [ 26 ], and Trail Making Test A). We used the mean and standard deviation (SD) at baseline to standardize the raw scores of each test to z -scores. We used the average of z -scores across all tests to create a global composite score. Higher composite scores reflect better cognitive performance. Global cognition was assessed at baseline and at follow-up visits at months 6, 12, 24, and 36 [ 21 ]. Trained research staff conducted cognitive testing according to a previously reported protocol [ 6 ]. Statistics The primary outcome of the trial was the change in global cognition at year 3. Because of the lack of significant intervention effects on global cognition [ 6 ], we combined the data from both intervention groups for analysis. Data are presented as means and SDs for continuous variables and frequencies for categorical variables. Baseline plasma carotenoid concentrations were used in the analyses. Total carotenoid concentrations were calculated as the sum of α-carotene, β-carotene, β-cryptoxanthin, lutein plus zeaxanthin, and lycopene. Carotenoids were log-transformed if the data were not normally distributed, and then mean-centered by subtracting the respective means. We treated baseline blood nutrient concentrations as both continuous and categorical variables (in tertiles). The 20 participants with the homozygous APOE ε2/ε2 genotype were included as noncarriers. One participant with an incomplete carotenoid profile was excluded from the analyses. Mixed-effect regression models were used to examine the association of baseline carotenoid concentrations with cognition at each visit. We first examined the effect size of baseline carotenoid concentrations on the mean change in global cognition at year 3. The model accounted for age (in years), sex (male/female), energy intake (kilocalories), education (in years), blood nutrient concentrations, APOE ε4 genotype (yes/no), visit, and interaction terms: age × visit, blood nutrients × visit, and APOEε4 × visit. To examine the interaction between blood nutrient concentrations and APOE ε4, we included a 3-way interaction (nutrient × APOE ε4 × visit) in the model, as mentioned earlier. The 3-way interaction term (nutrient × APOE ε4 × visit) was included to assess whether the association between baseline carotenoid concentrations and cognitive trajectories differed by APOE ε4 status over time. This approach allows us to evaluate both the main effects and genotype-specific time-dependent effects. Wald tests from the mixed-effects model were used to assess the significance of the interaction at each visit. The P value for the interaction was reported. Stratification analyses We further examined stratified models by APOE ε4 genotype, accounting for age (in years), education (in years), energy intake (in kilocalories), sex (male/female), visit, and interaction terms: blood nutrient concentrations × visit and age × visit. We further adjusted all models for sensitivity analyses to account for diet assignment (MIND versus usual). To illustrate the effect of APOE ε4 on the relation between baseline blood nutrients and cognition, we plotted the estimated cognitive trajectories of APOE ε4 carriers and noncarriers at the same nutrient concentrations by using the β-coefficient of nutrient concentrations at the 75th percentile and 25th percentile in the model. The Benjamini-Hochberg procedure was used to control the false discovery rate in multiple comparisons. P values from 6 comparisons were ranked and compared against critical thresholds (( k / m ) × α), where k is the rank, m is the total number of comparisons, and α = 0.05. Data analysis was performed using SAS software (version 9.4; SAS Institute Inc.), with a type I error rate of 0.05 set for significance, and all tests were 2-sided. Data visualization was conducted using R (version 4.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Standard protocol approvals, registrations, and patient consents The study was approved by the institutional review boards of Rush University Medical Center, Harvard School of Public Health, and Brigham and Women's Hospital. All the participants provided written informed consent. Results Participants’ mean age was 70.0 y for APOE ε4 noncarriers ( n = 308) and 69.4 y for the carriers ( n = 134) ( Table 1 ). Participants were predominantly White, female, and had a mean BMI of 33.9. Baseline carotenoid concentrations did not significantly differ between APOE ε4 carriers and noncarriers. There was an overall positive association between blood nutrient concentrations at baseline and cognitive scores at year 3 ( Table 2 ), which differed based on APOE ε4 status ( P values for interaction are shown in Table 2 ). Among APOE ε4 carriers, higher circulating nutrients at baseline were associated with slower cognitive decline, as indicated by a positive β-coefficient. A 1-unit increment of plasma total carotenoids at baseline was associated with a higher global cognitive score [β = 0.17; 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.06, 0.28; P = 0.009]. A 1-unit increment of each of the following nutrients at baseline was also associated with improved global cognitive scores: β-carotene (β = 0.13; 95% CI: 0.05, 0.21; P = 0.001), α-carotene (β = 0.09; 95% CI: 0.02, 0.15; P = 0.008), lutein plus zeaxanthin (β = 0.14; 95% CI: 0.04, 0.25; P = 0.008), lycopene (β = 0.17; 95% CI: 0.07, 0.28; P = 0.005), and β-cryptoxanthin (β = 0.13; 95% CI: 0.05, 0.21; P = 0.03) ( Table 2 ). We categorized these nutrients into tertiles for analysis to evaluate these nutrients at different levels. Figure 1 shows the baseline carotenoid concentrations by tertiles and changes in global cognition at year 3, stratified by APOE ε4 status. Among APOE ε4 carriers, participants in the highest tertile of baseline carotenoid concentrations experienced a substantially slower rate of cognitive decline than those in the lowest tertile. Specifically, in APOE ε4 carriers, individuals in the highest tertile of total carotenoids, α-carotene, β-carotene, and lutein plus zeaxanthin had significantly slower cognitive decline by 0.15 standardized unit (SU) (95% CI: 0.02, 0.30 SU; P = 0.03), 0.16 SU (95% CI: 0.02, 0.29 SU; P = 0.02), 0.18 SU (95% CI: 0.04, 0.31 SU; P = 0.01), and 0.16 SU (95% CI: 0.02 to 0.3 SU; P = 0.03), respectively, compared with those in the lowest tertile ( Figure 1 ). The difference in decline rates is approximately equivalent to being 3 y younger in age for total carotenoids, 3.5 years younger for α-carotene, 3.8 years younger for β-carotene, and 3.5 years younger for lutein plus zeaxanthin. Individuals in both the middle and the high tertiles of lycopene (β = 0.24; 95% CI: 0.10, 0.38; P = 0.001, β = 0.19; 95% CI: 0.05, 0.33; P = 0.01) and β-cryptoxanthin (β = 0.26; 95% CI: 0.12, 0.40; P = 0.0002, β = 0.20; 95% CI: 0.06, 0.34; P = 0.01) had significantly slower cognitive decline compared to those in the lowest tertile. These differences were approximately equivalent to being 4.7‒6 years younger in age for lycopene and 4.4‒5.7 y younger for β-cryptoxanthin. This association was not observed in noncarriers. Although noncarriers generally had higher cognitive performance, their global cognitive scores did not appear to vary with plasma carotenoid concentrations. Compared to APOE ε4 carriers in the lowest tertile - presumably the highest-risk group - nonε4 carriers exhibited higher global cognitive scores at year 3 across all nutrient tertiles ( Figure 2 ). Discussion In the present study, we examined the relation between circulating carotenoid t levels at baseline, APOE ε4 status, and their interaction with changes in global cognition among the MIND study participants. Results revealed that the association between circulating nutrients and global cognition depended on APOE ε4 genotype. Higher blood carotenoid concentrations in APOE ε4 carriers demonstrated a strong inverse association with cognitive decline, potentially mitigating the cognitive disadvantage associated with possessing an APOE ε4 risk allele. This suggests that optimizing nutrient concentrations may be particularly beneficial for individuals genetically predisposed to AD, offering a targeted approach to reducing their risk of cognitive impairment. These findings are novel and important because, to our knowledge, 1 ) no prior long-term clinical trial has reported an APOE ε4-specific association between blood nutrients and cognition, and 2 ) the robust and consistent effect size of higher blood carotenoid concentrations on cognitive changes among APOE ε4 carriers could potentially offset the genetic risk posed by the ε4 risk allele. Carotenoids and other antioxidants have been shown to have protective effects in preserving cognition. Yuan et al. [ 1 , 2 ] reported that higher intakes of carotenoids, including lutein, zeaxanthin, and β-cryptoxanthin, were associated with a lower risk of AD and cognitive decline. A meta-analysis of 9 studies reported that carotenoid supplementation may improve cognitive performance in individuals without dementia [ 28 ]. One study showed that higher brain concentrations of carotenoids were associated with better global cognitive performance [ 29 ]. Although the sample size in the study was insufficient to further analyze differences between ε4 carriers and noncarriers, it is evident that nutrient concentrations in the brain play a crucial role in maintaining cognitive health. Our previous findings further demonstrated that higher intakes of vitamin E and long-chain n ‒3 fatty acids were associated with slower cognitive decline in APOE ε4 carriers but not in noncarriers [ 13 , 14 , 30 ]. In the present study, using objectively measured nutrients, we observed that higher circulating concentrations of carotenoids were associated with slower cognitive decline in APOE ε4 carriers. The APOE genotype influences the response to dietary interventions [ [31] , [32] , [33] , [34] ]. Animal studies have shown that the APOE ε4 mice exhibit lower plasma concentrations of β-carotene and lutein, accompanied by increased accumulation of these carotenoids in adipose tissue [ 23 , 24 ]. The findings suggest that the APOE ε4 allele affects the metabolism and transportation of fat-soluble nutrients. Furthermore, the observed association between carotenoid intake and cognitive function appears to be influenced, at least in part, by an individual’s APOE ε4 genotype. These findings may be related to the role of APOE ε4 in various pathways associated with AD neuropathology [ 15 ]. APOE ε4 is one of the strongest genetic risk factors for AD, with carriers having a significantly greater risk of developing the disease. Emerging evidence from both in vivo and in vitro studies demonstrates that APOE ε4 pathologically alters microglial behavior, resulting in a more pro-inflammatory phenotype that contributes to AD progression [ 9 , 10 , 35 ]. APOE is also critical for brain lipid metabolism, particularly lipid transport between astrocytes and neurons, with the ε4 variant potentially impairing neuronal lipid homeostasis and contributing to synaptic dysfunction and neuronal loss [ 12 ]. APOE ε4 also affects cerebral vascular integrity [ 36 ] with evidence from animal models supporting blood-brain barrier (BBB) breakdown in APOE ε4 carriers compared with those with APOE ε3 or APOE ε2 alleles [ 36 ]. In humans, analysis of postmortem brain tissue revealed that patients with AD were prone to BBB breakdown [ 37 ]. Elevated biomarkers of BBB integrity were observed in cognitively normal older adults with APOE ε4 [ 38 ]. Given the limited understanding of nutrient uptake across the BBB and nutrient metabolism in neural tissue, we hypothesize that carotenoids may exert neuroprotective effects through their antioxidant and anti-inflammatory actions, reducing oxidative stress, mitigating neuroinflammation, and supporting normal cell function and neuronal plasticity. Because noncarriers may not experience the diathesis associated with APOE ε4, the protective effects of higher nutrient concentrations were less potent in noncarriers. Research also shows isoform-dependent variations in antioxidant capacity across different APOE genotypes, with APOE ε4 exhibiting the lowest antioxidant activity and APOE ε2 the highest [ [39] , [40] , [41] ]. This suggests that APOE ε4 carriers have significantly reduced antioxidant capacity, which may further amplify oxidative stress and contribute to its adverse effects in the brain. Consequently, maintaining adequate nutrient concentrations may be critical for high-risk APOE ε4 carriers to mitigate the predisposed vulnerabilities associated with this genotype. The results of the present study indicate that higher blood nutrient concentrations may compensate for the reduced antioxidant capacity of APOE ε4, potentially attenuating the inflammatory response induced by APOE ε4, promoting normal cell function in the brain, and thereby slowing cognitive decline. These findings suggest that higher antioxidant intake may be particularly beneficial for APOE ε4 carriers in lowering their risk of cognitive decline. Strengths of the study include the objective measurement of blood nutrient status in older adults within the context of a long-term randomized controlled trial. The comprehensive examination of cognitive function, combined with repeated measurements over 3 years, enabled the analysis of changes in cognition. Our study has limitations. The MIND trial participants were predominantly White adults with high BMIs and high levels of education, which limits the generalizability of the findings to broader populations. Additionally, we used blood nutrient concentrations at baseline to examine the prospective association, which limits our ability to infer causality. The impact of slower cognitive decline on daily functioning was not specifically measured in the present study. In conclusion, among cognitively unimpaired participants, higher plasma carotenoid concentrations were associated with slower cognitive decline in individuals with APOE ε4. High blood carotenoids can potentially mitigate the genetic risk of APOE ε4 on cognition.
