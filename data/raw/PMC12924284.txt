BMI changes from midlife to late life and their relationship with Alzheimer’s disease pathologies

Background Decreasing body mass index (BMI) from midlife to late life has been linked to increased Alzheimer's disease (AD) risk and cognitive decline; however, the association with in vivo AD pathology remains unclear. This study aimed to clarify the relationship between midlife-to-late-life BMI changes and in vivo AD pathologies, specifically amyloid-β (Aβ), tau, and AD-signature region cerebral glucose metabolism (AD-CM). Methods This observational cohort study included non-demented older adults recruited from the Korean Brain Aging Study for Early Diagnosis and Prediction of Alzheimer’s Disease (KBASE) between January 2014 and December 2020. Participants underwent clinical evaluations and positron emission tomography (PET) imaging to measure brain Aβ, tau, and AD-CM. Midlife BMI was retrospectively estimated from self-reported weight at ages 40–50 years, while late-life BMI was measured during the study. Participants were categorized based on BMI changes as decreasing (≤ − 4%), stable (− 4% to &lt; 4%), or increasing (≥ 4%). Associations between BMI patterns and AD pathologies were analyzed using multiple linear regression models. Results A total of 268 participants (mean age 66.6 ± 7.9 years; 56.3% women; 22.5% APOE ε4 carriers) were analyzed. Higher midlife BMI correlated significantly with lower AD-CM (β = − 0.009; 95% CI, − 0.015 to − 0.003; p &lt; .01). In contrast, late-life BMI was not significantly associated with AD-CM. Participants with decreasing BMI patterns from midlife to late life exhibited significantly lower AD-CM compared to those with increasing patterns (β = 0.029; 95% CI, 0.003 to 0.055; p = .027). No significant associations were observed between BMI patterns and Aβ or tau deposition. Conclusion A decreasing BMI trajectory from midlife to late life is associated with enhanced AD-related neurodegeneration, underscoring the clinical importance of monitoring long-term body weight changes in relation to Alzheimer's disease pathophysiology. Background Body mass index (BMI) has emerged as a significant contributing factor for late-life cognitive decline as well as metabolic health [ 1 , 2 ]. However, studies on the relationship between BMI and Alzheimer’s disease(AD) or dementia have produced conflicting results. Some studies suggest that higher BMI during midlife (BMI mid ) is associated with an increased risk of developing AD and dementia in late life [ 3 , 4 ]. In contrast, other studies indicate that higher BMI in late life (BMI late ) might be protective against AD and related cognitive decline [ 5 , 6 ]. These divergent findings suggest that the role of BMI in the development of AD or dementia may change across the lifespan. Additionally, BMI change patterns have been linked to AD and cognitive impairment in later life. Individuals who experience a decline in BMI in late life, as opposed to maintaining a stable BMI, showed greater memory decline and higher incidence of AD [ 7 – 9 ]. Furthermore, a decrease in BMI from midlife to late life was also associated with an elevated risk of both overall-dementia and AD in a previous population-based cohort study [ 10 ]. Regarding the neuropathological substrates underlying the relationship between BMI and AD-related cognitive decline, several postmortem or in vivo AD pathology studies showed that a higher BMI mid was associated with a heavier Aβ burden [ 11 ] and higher BMI late was associated with decreased CSF total or phosphorylated-tau [ 12 – 14 ]. In contrast, a lower BMI late was associated with greater Aβ deposition [ 12 , 15 ], and a higher longitudinal rate of tau accumulation [ 16 ]. In addition, while a higher BMI before late life was correlated with lower regional brain metabolic activity [ 17 ], a higher BMI late was associated with increased AD-signature region cerebral glucose metabolism(AD-CM), suggesting that higher BMI late may help mitigate brain dysfunction [ 18 ]. However, there is still limited information available on the relationship between BMI changes from midlife to late life and AD pathologies. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the relationship between the patterns of BMI change from midlife to late-life and in vivo AD pathologies (including Aβ, tau deposition, and AD-CM) in late-life as measured by brain positron emission tomography (PET) imaging. Methods Participants This study was conducted as part of an ongoing cohort study, the Korean Brain Aging Study for Early Diagnosis and Prediction of Alzheimer's Disease (KBASE) [ 19 ], which began in 2014. Participants were recruited from four sites (i.e., two public centers dedicated to dementia prevention and management and two memory clinics at two university hospitals) in Seoul, South Korea. The description on KBASE study, including the recruitment of participants, sampling, and neuroimaging, is provided in Byun et al. [ 19 ]. As of December 2020, a total of 268 non-demented (196 cognitively normal[CN] and 72 mild cognitive impairment [MCI]) participants aged between 55 and 90 years were included in the present investigation. The CN group was comprised of individuals who had a Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) [ 20 ] of 0 and did not have a diagnosis of MCI or dementia. All participants with MCI met the criteria for amnestic MCI as follows: (a) memory complaints confirmed by an informant, (b) objective memory impairments, (c) independence in functional activities, and (d) no dementia. Regarding criterion (b), the z-scores adjusted for age, education, and sex showed a value below −1.0 for at least one out of the four episodic memory tests. The four memory tests consist of the Word List Memory (WLM), Word List Recall (WLR), Word List Recognition (WLRc), and Constructional Recall (CR) tests, which are part of the neuropsychological evaluation battery of the Korean version of the Consortium to Establish a Registry for Alzheimer’s Disease (CERAD-K). The exclusion criteria were as follows: (a) any serious medical, psychiatric, or neurological disorder that could affect mental function; (b) any severe communication problem that would render clinical examination or brain scanning difficult; (c) contraindications to MRI, such as a pacemaker or claustrophobia; (d) absence of a reliable informant; (e) illiteracy defined as the incapability of reading; and (f) participation in another clinical trial or treatment with an investigational product. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Seoul National University Hospital and SNU-SMG Boramae Medical Center, South Korea, and all participants provided written informed consent. We adhered to the reporting guidelines outlined by the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology ( STROBE ). Clinical and neuropsychological assessment The individuals went through a detailed evaluation at the commencement of the research employing the KBASE protocol [ 19 ], which encompassed the Korean version of the Consortium to Establish a Registry for Alzheimer's Disease Assessment (CERAD-K) battery [ 21 ], carried out by trained psychiatrists. Experienced clinical psychologists administered the Korean version of the CERAD neuropsychological battery [ 22 , 23 ] to all participants. The battery comprises various subsets including verbal fluency (VF), Boston naming test (BNT), Mini-Mental State Examination, WLM, construction praxis (CP), WLR, WLRc, CR, Trail Making Test B, and Stroop Color-Word Test. The total score for the CERAD neuropsychological battery (TS) was calculated by summing the scores of seven tests (VF, BNT, WLM, CP, WLR, WLRc, CR) in the battery [ 24 ] and used as a measure of global cognitive function. Additionally, all participants were methodically evaluated for vascular risk factors (VRFs) such as diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia, coronary heart disease, transient ischemic attack, and stroke. This assessment was carried out based on information gathered through systematic interviews conducted by trained nurses with participants and their informants. A VRF was considered present if the participant had received a diagnosis in a clinical setting or was taking medication for it at the time of enrollment. The vascular risk factor score (VRS) was determined by tallying the number of VRFs present [ 25 ]. Measurement of BMI changes BMI late (ie., current BMI) was calculated using the measured weight in kilograms divided by the height in meters squared. BMI mid was calculated using self-recalled body weight and the current height. The average BMIs at 40 and 50 years old are referred to as the BMI mid . Self-recalled past body weight in non-demented older adults was well-validated [ 26 , 27 ]. When older individuals [mean(SD) = 70(10.5) years of age] reflected on their weights at 40 and 50 years old, the correlation coefficients indicating the relationship between the actual and remembered values were found to be 0.87, 0.89, and 0.90, respectively (all p &lt; 0.0001) in the referenced research study [ 26 ]. In cases where a participant provided a range, the calculation utilized the median value. We categorized the patterns of BMI change from midlife to late-life (BMI mid-to-late ) into three strata: decreasing BMI mid-to-late pattern: BMI change ≤ −4%, stable BMI mid-to-late pattern: −4% &lt; BMI change &lt; 4%, and increasing BMI mid-to-late pattern: BMI change ≥ 4% by reference to a previous report [ 28 ]. Assessment of other potential confounders We conducted a systematic assessment of various factors that could potentially impact the relationship between BMI and/or BMI change patterns and AD neuroimaging markers. Apolipoprotein E (APOE) genotyping was carried out and APOE ε4 (APOE4) positivity was defined as the presence of at least one ε4 allele. Information regarding alcohol consumption (categorized as never/former/drinker), smoking habits (categorized as never/former/smoker), and lifetime physical activity levels was gathered through interviews with nurses. To evaluate lifetime physical activity, we utilized the Lifetime Total Physical Activity Questionnaire [ 29 ] which assigned a metabolic equivalent (MET) value to the intensity of the activity based on the compendium of physical activities [ 30 ]. Measurement of cerebral Aβ deposition All participants underwent [ 11 C] Pittsburgh compound B (PiB) PET scans utilizing a 3.0 T Biograph mMR (PET-MR) scanner (Siemens, Washington DC, USA). The methodology for PiB-PET image acquisition and subsequent preprocessing procedures were described in a prior publication [ 31 ]. To delineate regions of interest (ROIs) and evaluate PiB retention in the frontal, lateral parietal, posterior cingulate-precuneus, and lateral temporal regions, an automatic anatomic labeling algorithm alongside a region combination technique [ 32 ] was employed. Additionally, a composite global cortical ROI, comprising the aforementioned four smaller ROIs, was defined. The global Aβ retention, expressed as a standardized uptake value ratio (SUVR), was calculated by dividing the mean values for all voxels within the global cortical ROI by the mean value of a designated reference region. To analyze the data, the inferior cerebellar gray matter from the spatially unbiased infratentorial template for the cerebellum (SUIT) atlas [ 33 ] was utilized as a reference region. A participant was classified as Aβ positive if the global Aβ retention value surpassed 1.21 [ 34 ]. Measurement of cerebral tau deposition A subgroup of participants ( n = 110) underwent [18F] AV-1451 PET scans utilizing a Biograph True Point 40 PET/CT scanner (Siemens, USA). While the initial Pittsburgh Compound B (PIB)-PET scan was conducted during the baseline assessment, the AV-1451 PET imaging was performed at an average (SD) of 2.6 (0.3) years subsequent to the baseline assessment. The protocols for the acquisition and preprocessing of AV-1451 PET imaging were described in a prior publication [ 31 ]. We computed the AV-1451 SUVR of a predefined ROI associated with the “AD-signature region” to quantify cerebral tau deposition. This metric represents a size-weighted mean of the partial volume-corrected uptake across the entorhinal, amygdala, parahippocampal, fusiform, inferior temporal, and middle temporal ROIs [ 35 , 36 ]. The mean inferior cerebellar gray matter uptake was used as a reference region according to the literature [ 37 ]. Measurement of AD-signature region cerebral glucose metabolism All participants received [ 18 F]fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) PET imaging using a 3.0 T Biograph mMR (PET-MR) scanner (Siemens, Washington DC, USA). The details of FDG PET image acquisition and preprocessing were described in a prior publication [ 38 ]. AD-signature FDG ROIs, such as the angular gyri, posterior cingulate cortex, and inferior temporal gyri, which are sensitive to the changes associated with AD [ 39 ], were determined, AD-signature region cerebral glucose metabolism (AD-CM) was defined as the voxel-weighted mean SUVR extracted from the AD-signature FDG ROIs. Statistical analyses Multiple linear regression analyses were performed to investigate the association between BMI mid or BMI late and AD-related neuroimaging biomarkers, including global Aβ retention, cerebral tau deposition, and AD-CM. In these model, BMI mid (or BMI late ) was entered as the independent variable, each neuroimaging biomarker as the dependent variable, and age, sex, education, diagnosis (CN vs. MCI), APOE4 positivity, and VRS as covariates. To investigate the relationships between BMI mid-to-late and AD-related neuroimaging biomarkers, additional multiple linear regression models were tested. In these models, BMI mid-to-late patterns (decreasing, stable, increasing) were treated as an ordinal variable, with age, sex, education, diagnosis, BMI late , APOE4 status, and VRS included as covariates. Sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of the findings. These included: (1) repeating the analyses in cognitively normal participants only (with diagnosis excluded as a covariate), (2) treating BMI mid-to-late patterns as a nominal variable and applying analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) instead of regression, controlling for the same covariates, and (3) additionally adjusting for alcohol consumption, smoking, and lifetime physical activity. Furthermore, for model using AD-CM as the dependent variable, an additional sensitivity analysis was conducted by including fasting blood glucose level – measured at the time of FDG-PET- as a covariate to account for potential peripheral glucose effects on cerebral metabolism. As an additional analysis, we investigated whether BMI in each life period and BMI mid-to-late patterns were associated with global cognitive function, as measured by the CERAD-TS. Linear regression models were applied for BMI mid and BMI late , adjusting for age, sex, education, APOE4 status, and vascular risk score. Diagnosis was not included as a covariate in these analyses to avoid potential overadjustment, given that cognitive performance is an integral component of diagnostic classification. For BMI mid-to-late patterns, ANCOVA analyses were conducted by treating BMI mid-to-late patterns as a nominal variable, while adjusting the same covariates. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons were subsequently performed using estimated marginal means. Data analysis was conducted using jamovi version 2.2.5 (the Jamovi project, https://www.jamovi.org ) integrated with R (version 4.3). In all analyses, p &lt; 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Results Participant characteristics The demographic and clinical characteristics of all participants ( n = 268) are presented in Table 1 . Significant group differences were found between CN and MCI participants in age, years of education, and APOE ε4 carrier status (all p &lt; 0.05), where the proportion of female participants did not differ significantly. As expected, the MCI group exhibited markedly lower global cognitive performance, as measured by the CERAD-K total score (35.7 ± 9.3 vs. 56.7 ± 8.7, p &lt; 0.001). Regarding biomarker profiles, Aβ positivity was more frequent in MCI participants (58.3%) than in CN participants (21.9%) ( p &lt; 0.001). In the subsample with tau PET data ( n = 110), the MCI group demonstrated significantly higher global tau deposition (2.12 ± 1.09 vs. 1.31 ± 0.21, p &lt; 0.001). Additionally, AD-CM values were significantly lower in MCI group than in CN group (1.32 ± 0.16 vs. 1.42 ± 0.12, p &lt; 0.001), indicating reduced cerebral metabolism. Discussion In the present study, we found a significant association between higher BMI mid and lower AD-CM in non-dementia older adults. This finding aligns with previous studies suggesting that midlife obesity is a risk factor for later-life cognitive decline or AD [ 3 , 40 ], and related to decreased regional brain activity [ 17 ]. Mid-life obesity has been associated with increased inflammation, insulin resistance, and metabolic syndrome, which can lead to impaired cerebral glucose metabolism as well as cerebrovascular damage [ 41 ]. In contrast to the association between higher BMI mid and lower AD-CM, we found no significant association between BMI late and AD-CM. There is ongoing debate regarding the relationship between BMI late and cognitive performance or the risk of dementia. Several studies have reported that higher BMI late might be protective against cognitive decline or the development of dementia [ 42 , 43 ]. Similarly, reports from the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) cohort have demonstrated a positive association in non-demented older adults between higher BMI late and greater cerebral glucose metabolism [ 44 , 45 ], as well as a slower rate of decline in glucose metabolic over time [ 46 ]. In contrast, a large population-based longitudinal study demonstrated that an elevated BMI had an overall detrimental impact on cognitive functions during both midlife and late-life stages [ 47 ]. These conflicting findings reflect the complexity of the relationship between BMI late and late-life cognitive health, highlighting the need for further research to disentangle the complex issue. Meanwhile, we found neither BMI mid nor BMI late was associated with Aβ and tau deposition. Previous studies using PET or CSF biomarkers suggested that lower BMI late is associated with greater Aβ [ 12 , 15 , 48 ] and tau pathology [ 12 – 14 , 49 ] in brain, while a higher BMI mid was associated with a heavier Aβ burden [ 11 ]. The different characteristics of participants may explain the discrepancy between prior findings and the current results. Our study included a very limited number of individuals with severe obesity (BMI ≥ 30) or underweight (BMI ≤ 18.5), whereas, prior studies demonstrating significant associations frequently included participants with severe obesity or underweight [ 11 , 14 , 15 ]. In addition, while we included only non-demented individuals, some previous studies encompassed individuals with dementia as well as non-demented participants [ 12 , 13 , 49 ]. The pattern of BMI change from midlife to late life exhibited a significant association with AD-CM. Specifically, the decreasing pattern was linked to significantly reduced AD-CM compared to the increase pattern. Our result supports previous reports [ 50 , 51 ], which indicated that a decrease in body mass occurs prior to the manifestation of cognitive deficits in individuals diagnosed with dementia. The temporal gap between the reduction in weight and the initiation of dementia symptoms is up to 20 years [ 50 , 51 ]. Some possible explanations can be provided regarding the underlying mechanisms linking decreasing BMI mid-to-late to reduced AD-CM: First, weight loss is known as a phenotype of frailty [ 52 ], and frailty may accelerate neurodegeneration through several biological processes. Higher homocysteine has been linked to both frailty and neurodegenerative changes in the brain [ 53 , 54 ]. Elevated homocysteine levels are associated with endothelial dysfunction, glial activation, and neurodegeneration [ 54 ]. In parallel, frailty-associated biological mechanisms, such as increased oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and synaptic impairment may further exacerbate neurodegenerative pathways [ 52 , 55 ]. Alternatively, decreasing BMI mid-to-late or gradual weight loss in later life may not directly cause neurodegeneration but rather reflect underlying neurodegenerative process. The changes in hypothalamic function and related appetite dysregulation caused by neurodegenerative process could result in weight loss [ 56 , 57 ]. This aligns with the concept of reverse causality, where neurodegeneration leads to weight loss. In contrast to the difference between decreasing and increasing BMI mid-to-late patterns, we found no significant differences in AD-CM between the decreasing and stable BMI mid-to-late patterns. The current study excluded individuals with severe physical or mental problems, who have a high possibility of severe weight loss at the time of enrollment. This might cause individuals with severe weight loss to be underrepresented which might have weakened the influence of BMI mid-to-late on the reduction of AD-CM. In other aspect, the exclusion of participants with dementia, who often experience accelerated neurodegeneration and weight loss as part of AD-CM decrease, resulting in no difference in AD-CM between the decreasing and stable BMI mid-to-late patterns. The association between BMI mid or decreasing BMI mid-to-late and reduced AD-CM in late life remained significant even after controlling for age, sex, education, clinical diagnosis, APOE4 positivity, and VRS. In addition, the association was not moderated by any of these variables. Taken together, these findings suggest that higher BMI mid and decreasing BMI mid-to-late are related to reduced AD-CM, independently of these variables. Consistent with these neuroimaging findings, we also observed that higher midlife BMI and a decreasing BMI trajectory were associated with poorer global cognitive performance. These results are in line with previous epidemiological studies that higher midlife BMI increases the risk of AD and dementia in late life [ 3 , 4 ], and BMI decline during late life is linked to accelerated memory decline and higher incidence of AD [ 7 – 9 ]. Together, These findings suggest that both midlife adiposity and subsequent BMI decline may contribute to later-life cognitive vulnerability possibly through shared metabolic and neurodegenerative pathways. Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the reliance on self-reported weight for calculating midlife BMI (BMI mid ) may introduce recall bias. However, previous studies have demonstrated high validity of recalled midlife weight in older adults, with the correlations between the actual and recalled values ( r = 0.87—0.94), and average disparity of only 0.67 kg over 26-year interval [ 26 , 27 ]. Although the possibility of recall bias due to memory impairment in individuals with MCI cannot be entirely excluded [ 58 ], we conducted a sensitivity analyses restricted to CN participants to minimize this concern. The results remained consistent with the main finding, suggesting that the potential impact of recall bias in the MCI group is likely minimal. Second, a key limitation of this study is that BMI was assessed at only two time points-midlife and late life- which precludes capturing potential fluctuations in weight across adulthood. This approach may misclassify dynamic BMI trajectories, such as initial increases followed by later decrease, as stable patterns. Prior research has suggested that BMI variability itself may be associated with dementia risk and AD-related biomarkers [ 59 , 60 ]. Future studies incorporating repeated BMI assessments across the adult lifespan are needed to clarify the role of BMI fluctuation in Alzheimer’s disease–related neurodegeneration. Third, although our study adopted a longitudinal observation approach, and examined the relationship between BMI mid-to-late and brain pathologies in late life, establishing the direction of causality remains challenging because we can not exclude the possibility of reverse causality (i.e., the effect of decreased brain function or neurodegeneration on decreasing BMI mid-to-late ). Finally, the limited racial and ethnic diversity of the sample may affect the generalizability of the findings. Future research should aim to include a more diverse population. Conclusion The present findings suggest that a decreasing pattern of BMI from midlife to late-life, as well as higher midlife BMI itself, is closely related to accelerated AD-related neurodegeneration.
